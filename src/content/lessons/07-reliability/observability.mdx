## The problem

A user says "checkout is slow". Your system has 40 services. One checkout touches 7 of them. You cannot attach a debugger to a fleet. You cannot read one machine's screen. All you have is the data the system chose to record.

Two questions come up again and again.

1. **What happened inside this one request?** (to debug a failure)
2. **Is the service good enough right now, and should anyone be woken up?** (to run the service)

**Observability** is the ability to answer such questions about a running system from the data it emits. The data comes in a few kinds. The rest of the lesson is which kind answers which question, and how to turn the data into a decision.

## Naive solutions

**Read the logs on each server.** With 40 services on many machines, you would search dozens of log files and try to match lines that belong to one request. Nothing joins them.

**Watch the average latency.** Averages hide the pain. The Google SRE book gives an example: a service with a 100 ms average at 1,000 requests per second. In that case, 1% of requests "might easily take 5 seconds". That is 10 users every second with a 5-second wait. The mean looks fine. The book calls the belief that most requests sit near the mean "just hopeful thinking". The SRE book says "we generally prefer to work with percentiles rather than the mean". A percentile such as p99 is the value that 99% of requests beat. [Lesson 1.3](/learn/01-one-machine/throughput-vs-latency/) introduced the tail.

**Alert on every error.** A single failed request is normal in a big system. If every error pages someone, people learn to ignore pages.

## Three signals

OpenTelemetry, the open standard for this data, lists several **signals**. Three matter most.

| Signal | What it is | Answers | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Metrics** | Numbers measured over time, such as requests per second or error count | "Is something wrong? Is it getting worse?" | No detail about one request |
| **Logs** | A record of an event, with a time and details | "What exactly happened on this machine?" | Large volume. Hard to join across services |
| **Traces** | The path of one request across services | "Where did this request spend its time?" | You cannot keep every trace, so you sample |

OpenTelemetry also lists **baggage** (context passed along between signals) and **profiles** (code-level resource use), which it describes as still in development.

The three work as a team. A metric alert says "p99 latency for checkout doubled". A trace shows that the payment call is the slow part. A log line inside the payment service says why. Traces do not replace metrics: a trace shows one request, and a metric shows the trend across all of them.

### Traces and spans

A **span** is one timed unit of work. It has a name, a start time, an end time, and a **parent span ID**. The parent ID is empty for the first span, the **root**. A **trace** is all the spans that share one trace ID. They form a tree.

How do spans from different services end up in one tree? The caller passes the trace ID and its own span ID in a header on each call. The callee opens a new span whose parent is that ID. Span attributes, which are key-value tags, should be set when the span is created, so that a sampler can use them to decide what to keep.

## What to measure

You cannot record everything. Three checklists tell you where to start.

**The four golden signals** (Google SRE book): latency, traffic, errors, saturation. The book says to track the latency of successful and failed requests separately, because a fast error can make latency look better than it is. Saturation is how full a service is. The book notes that "many systems degrade in performance before they achieve 100% utilization", so set a target for it.

**RED** (Tom Wilkie, Grafana): Rate, Errors, Duration. Use it for each service that handles requests. It is a stand-in for what users feel.

**USE** (Brendan Gregg): for each resource, check Utilization, Saturation, and Errors. Use it for the things a service runs on: CPUs, disks, thread pools, connections.

The SRE book adds a rule about alerts. A monitoring system should answer two questions: "what's broken, and why?" The "what" is a **symptom**. The "why" is a cause. **Page on symptoms**, for example users seeing 500 errors. Use causes, such as a full disk, to find the problem once it has paged you. A cause alert makes sense only when the cause is certain to turn into a symptom soon.

## SLI, SLO, SLA, and the error budget

"Good enough" needs a number. Three terms, as the SRE book defines them:

- An **SLI** (service level indicator) is "a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service". For example: the share of requests that complete in under 100 ms.
- An **SLO** (service level objective) is "a target value or range of values for a service level that is measured by an SLI". For example: "99% of Get RPC calls will complete in less than 100 ms", the book's own example.
- An **SLA** (service level agreement) is "an explicit or implicit contract with your users that includes consequences of meeting (or missing) the SLOs they contain". An SLA usually has money attached. An SLO is an internal goal.

An SLO below 100% leaves room for failure. That room is the **error budget**. The book puts it this way: "An error budget is just an SLO for meeting other SLOs!"

Do the sum. A 99.9% SLO over 30 days gives a budget of 0.1% of requests. If the service were fully down, that budget is gone in 0.001 x 43,200 min = 43.2 minutes. Google's published target for Compute Engine, to give a sense of scale, is 99.95%, which the book calls three and a half nines.

The budget is useful because it can be spent. A team with budget left can ship risky changes. A team that has spent it should slow down and fix reliability.

## Alerting on burn rate

How do you alert on a budget? Not by alerting on each error. The SRE workbook rates a rule like "alert when the error rate over a short window passes the SLO threshold". It has good recall and a fast response, but low precision: it "fires on many events that do not threaten the SLO".

The better signal is the **burn rate**: how fast, relative to the SLO, the service consumes the error budget. A constant 0.1% error rate on a 99.9% SLO uses exactly all the budget in 30 days. That is a burn rate of 1. A burn rate of 10 uses it in 3 days. A burn rate of 1,000 uses it in 43 minutes. These are from the workbook.

The workbook recommends **multiwindow** alerts. An alert fires only when the burn rate is over a threshold in both a long window and a short window:

| Severity | Burn rate | Long window | Short window | Budget used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Page | 14.4 | 1 hour | 5 minutes | 2% |
| Page | 6 | 6 hours | 30 minutes | 5% |
| Ticket | 1 | 3 days | 6 hours | 10% |

The long window makes sure the problem has consumed enough budget to matter. The short window (about 1/12 of the long one, the workbook's guideline) makes the alert stop soon after the errors stop: the 5-minute window drops below the threshold 5 minutes after the errors end, where the 1-hour window would take 60 minutes.

Play with it. Choose an error rate and see which rules fire, and when.

What to notice:

1. **Each rule fires after the same budget loss, however big the incident.** The fast rule fires when 2% of the monthly budget is gone. A total outage reaches that in 52 seconds. A 5% error rate takes about 17 minutes. At 1% (burn 10) the fast rule never fires, because 10 is under 14.4. The slower page rule fires after 3.6 hours, when 5% of the budget is gone.
2. **A slow leak never pages.** An error rate of 0.1% burns at 1, so only the ticket rule fires, after 3 days. That is right: nobody needs waking for a leak that the budget can absorb.
3. **An error rate under 0.1% fires nothing.** The budget covers it.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short alert window | Fast detection and fast reset | Pages on noise | Paired with a long window that confirms the burn |
| Long alert window | Stable, few false pages | Slow to notice a real outage | Slow burns, tickets |
| Metrics | Cheap, long history, good for alerts | No per-request detail | Always, as the base |
| Traces | Per-request detail across services | Cost, so you must sample | Finding where time goes |
| Logs | Full detail of an event | Volume, and hard to join | Explaining a specific failure |
| Tight SLO | Users get higher reliability | Little budget for releases and cost | Critical paths |
| Loose SLO | Room to ship and to fail | Real problems can hide inside it | Non-critical features |

## In practice

<Callout type="tip">
In an interview, close a design with its operations story. Say: "I would track rate, errors, and p99 duration per service, with traces across services. The checkout SLO is 99.9% of requests succeeding, over 30 days. I would page on a fast budget burn, at a 14.4 burn rate, and open a ticket on a slow one." That is a complete answer and it uses the vocabulary of the SRE workbook.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not alert on every error or on CPU alone: alert on symptoms and burn rate. Do not use average latency as the SLO, use a percentile. Do not call an SLA and an SLO the same thing: an SLA has consequences. Do not say "traces replace metrics", because a trace shows one request and a metric shows trends. And do not treat the error budget as a bill to avoid. It is room to take risk, until it runs out.
</Callout>
