## The problem

You type `www.example.com` in your browser. Lesson 2.1 showed what happens next: the browser opens a TCP connection. But TCP cannot connect to a name. A packet needs an IP address, such as `203.0.113.7`. Your browser has a name and needs a number.

Think of your phone's contacts app. You tap "Mum". You do not dial 10 digits by hand. The app turns the name into a number for you. The internet needs the same service, and it needs it for every new site, for every user on Earth, in a few milliseconds.

This lesson covers the whole path from a name to a number. First, why one list on one server fails. Then how many servers share the list in levels. Then how caches keep most lookups short. Last, the price you pay when a number changes.

The system that does this job is the **Domain Name System**, or **DNS**. A **domain name** is the human-readable name, such as `example.com`. DNS is the internet's contacts app.

One more point before we start. The mapping is not fixed. You move a service to a new data center (a building full of servers). A server dies. A company picks a closer machine for each user. So the list changes all day. A list that is huge and also alive needs a careful design.

When that live list breaks, everything that uses the name breaks with it. In October 2025 one empty DNS record took DynamoDB offline in AWS's Northern Virginia region. [The story](#incident) comes after the section on TTL.

## The naive way: one big server

The simplest design is a single table on a single server. Each row is one name and its IP address. Every client on the internet asks this server. Picture one phone book, one receptionist, in one office.

Three things break at once.

**Distance.** Lesson 1.2 showed that light in fiber sets a floor on latency. A user in Sydney asking a server in Virginia waits about 160 ms or more for each answer. Remember that this wait comes before the first byte of every new site. The user has not even started the TCP handshake yet.

**Load.** Every page load on the internet would start with a question to this one server. Billions of questions per day would land on one machine. No machine can serve them all.

**Failure.** If this server fails, every site disappears. You could still reach a site by typing its number. Nobody does that. One broken machine would stop the web.

Two ideas fix all three problems. They show up again in almost every lesson of this course.

- **Split the data in a hierarchy**, so no server needs to know everything.
- **Cache**, so most questions never travel far.

A **cache** is a saved copy of an answer, kept close to the person who asks. The picture is a sticky note on your monitor. You looked up a phone number once. You wrote it on the note. Next time you read the note and skip the phone book. The cache exists because the near copy is far faster than the far original. The catch: the note can go out of date. The rest of this lesson is about that catch.

## The mechanism: a hierarchy of servers

### Read a name from right to left

Look at `www.example.com`. It has three parts, split by dots. Read it from the right.

- `com` is the **top-level domain**, or **TLD**. It is the broadest group. Other TLDs are `org`, `net`, and `uk`.
- `example` is a name registered inside `com`. Someone paid to own `example.com`.
- `www` is a label that the owner of `example.com` created for one machine or service.

There is also an invisible empty part at the very right, after the last dot. It is called the **root**. The full name is really `www.example.com.` with a dot at the end. Browsers hide it.

This shape lets us split the work in three levels, like the levels of a company. Each level knows only the level below it.

- The **root servers** know who runs each TLD. They do not know any website.
- The **TLD servers** for `com` know who runs each name registered under `.com`, such as `example.com`. They do not know what `www` is.
- The **authoritative servers** for `example.com` hold the real answer. "Authoritative" means "the final word": these servers are run by the owner of the name, and no one above them overrules them.

A server that takes part in DNS like this is called a **name server**. An everyday picture: you call a big company and ask for Priya. Reception does not know Priya's desk. Reception knows which floor holds her department, and says "ask floor 3". The floor desk knows which room. The room knows Priya. Nobody knows everyone. Everyone knows who to ask next.

That "ask floor 3" answer has a name. It is a **referral**. A referral says: "I do not have the answer, but these servers are closer. Ask them." Each server in the chain gives either the real data or a referral.

### What an answer looks like

The data in DNS is stored as **records**. A record is one row in a table: a name, a type, and a value. The most common type is the **A record**. It maps a name to an IPv4 address. The "A" stands for "address". Example: `www.example.com` is `203.0.113.7`.

Every record also carries a **TTL**, short for **time to live**. The TTL is a number of seconds. It tells every cache how long it may keep this answer. The picture is the "best before" date on a milk carton. After the date, throw it away and buy fresh. The TTL exists because answers change. Without a deadline, a cache would keep a wrong answer forever. A typical TTL is 300 seconds, which is 5 minutes.

### The resolver does the walking

Your browser could walk the three levels itself. It does not. It asks a helper, called a **recursive resolver**, or just a **resolver**. The resolver does the whole walk for you and brings back the final answer.

The picture is a personal assistant. You say "get me the number for Priya". The assistant makes the calls, asks reception, asks the floor, asks the room, and returns with one number. You never see the steps. The assistant also keeps notes of every answer.

Who runs a resolver? Usually your **ISP** (internet service provider, the company you pay for internet access). Many people also pick a public one, such as `1.1.1.1` or `8.8.8.8`. Your laptop or phone is set up to know its resolver's address.

You do not walk the tree yourself because the resolver is shared. Thousands or millions of users ask the same resolver. When one user's question fills its cache, everyone else gets the answer free. One person's work helps everyone.

Now step through one lookup. First it is a cold one: nothing is cached anywhere. Then it is a cached one.

<DnsWalk client:visible />

Look at what happened. A cold lookup takes four round trips: one to the resolver, then three to the servers above it (root, TLD, authoritative). The first user pays for the walk. The second user, on the same resolver 20 seconds later, pays for one round trip only.

Here is the cost in numbers. Say the resolver is 10 ms away and each upstream server is about 30 ms away (these numbers are made up, but the shape is real). A cold lookup costs 10 + 30 + 30 + 30 = 100 ms. A cached lookup costs 10 ms. Neither number includes the TCP handshake that comes after.

### Three more details

**There are only 13 root server names, but about 2,000 machines.** The names run from A to M. The original DNS design needed the list of root servers to fit in one small message, so 13 is the number that fit. Behind each name sit many machines in many countries. They are run by 12 organizations. The trick that makes this work is **anycast**. Many machines share the same IP address. The routers of the internet (lesson 2.1) send your packet to whichever machine is closest. The picture is a pizza chain with one phone number. Whichever branch is nearest to you picks up. So the root is not one server and not one place. It is the answer to the "failure" problem from the naive design.

**DNS uses UDP on port 53.** Lesson 2.1 showed that UDP has no handshake. DNS fits UDP well. A question is small. An answer is small. Each travels as one datagram. If one is lost, the resolver waits a moment and asks again. The classic limit for a DNS answer over UDP is 512 bytes. A bigger answer needs another way. The resolver can retry over TCP, or both sides can agree to larger messages. For the usual lookup, one packet out and one packet back is all it takes.

**A failed lookup is cached too.** Say someone types `exampel.com` with a typo. The name does not exist. The resolver saves that fact as well, for a short time. This is called **negative caching**. Without it, every typo would travel to the authoritative server again and again. A cached "no" is also an answer.

### Record types you will meet

An A record is only one type. A name can have many records of different types.

| Record | What it holds | Everyday picture | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | An IPv4 address | The street address | `api.example.com` is `203.0.113.7` |
| **AAAA** | An IPv6 address (the 128-bit kind from lesson 2.1) | A street address in the newer numbering | Same name, longer number |
| **CNAME** | An alias: a pointer to another name | "Forwarded to" mail note | `www` is an alias for `example.cdn.net` |
| **NS** | Which servers are authoritative for a name | The "ask floor 3" note | Who answers for `example.com` |
| **MX** | Which servers take email for the domain | The mailroom | Where to deliver mail for `@example.com` |
| **TXT** | Free text | A sticky note on a door | Proof that you own the domain |

CNAME exists because a company often hands part of its service to another company. A **CDN** (content delivery network) is one example: a company that keeps copies of your site on servers near users all over the world. You point `www.example.com` at the CDN's name with a CNAME. If the CDN changes its machines, you change nothing. Only the CDN updates its own records.

## What TTL costs

TTL is a dial. Both ends of the dial hurt.

Say you must change the IP address of `api.example.com`. The old server is gone, or you are moving to a new data center. Switching to a backup server when the main one is gone is called **failover**. You edit the A record at your authoritative server. Is every user on the new address now? No. Every resolver in the world holds a note with the old answer. Each note stays until its TTL runs out.

Here is the two-sided cost.

- **Short TTL, such as 30 seconds.** Notes expire fast, so the change spreads fast. But resolvers come back to your servers often, so your name servers get busy.
- **Long TTL, such as 1 day.** Resolvers come back rarely, so your servers stay quiet. But after you change the IP, some users may reach the dead address for up to a full day.

Resolvers do not ask "has this changed?" because of scale. Millions of resolvers cannot each hold a live connection to your name servers. A deadline on the note is cheap. Asking is expensive.

Try the dial below. The chart shows the share of resolvers that still hold the old address after you change it. The numbers beside it show how many questions per second your authoritative servers must answer. The demo assumes 100,000 resolvers.

<DnsTtlSim client:visible />

The model: each resolver saved its note at a different time. So after your change, the notes expire at random moments spread over one TTL. The share of users on the old address falls in a straight line from 100% to 0%. The right end of the line is the worst case. One full TTL after the change, the last note has expired.

Now the load number. Each resolver asks once per TTL. With 100,000 resolvers and a TTL of 300 seconds, that is 100,000 / 300, which is about 330 queries per second. Cut the TTL to 60 seconds and the load rises to 100,000 / 60, about 1,700 queries per second. Five times shorter means five times the load.

<Callout type="deep" title="DNS as a load balancer, and where it stops">
A name can have several A records. The resolver gives them out in rotating or shuffled order, so users spread over several IPs. This is **DNS round robin**. Providers can go further. **Geo-routing** answers with the IP of the nearest region to the user. **Health-checked failover** stops returning an IP when a check shows the machine is down. Cloud load balancers use this. A **load balancer** is a machine or service that takes incoming requests and spreads them across many servers behind it. An AWS load balancer removes an unhealthy zone's IP from its DNS answers. Its DNS records carry a 60 second TTL.

But DNS is blind to load. It cannot see that one server is full. It cannot finish a half-done connection politely. And some resolvers and apps keep notes longer than your TTL asks. So DNS is good for choosing the front door: a region, or a load balancer. Something faster does the fine-grained spreading behind that door. That is lesson 2.4.
</Callout>

## A real outage: AWS DynamoDB, 2025

>

DynamoDB is AWS's managed key-value database. Clients find it by name, `dynamodb.us-east-1.amazonaws.com`. Automation keeps that name's records up to date. A **Planner** writes plans: which load balancers to list, and with what weights. Three independent **Enactors** apply the plans to Route 53, AWS's DNS service.

1. One Enactor was unusually slow. Before it started, it had checked that its plan was newer than the live one. By the time it wrote the plan, that check was stale.
2. Meanwhile a second Enactor applied a much newer plan. Then the slow Enactor overwrote it with its old plan.
3. The second Enactor then ran a clean-up that deletes very old plans. It deleted the old plan, which was now the live one. All IP addresses for the name were removed. AWS calls the cause a **race condition**: a bug that shows only when two processes act in an unlucky order.
4. Every client that looked up the name got no address. That meant customers, and AWS's own services that depend on DynamoDB. The automation could not repair this state. Engineers had to fix it by hand.
5. The DNS records were restored at 2:25 AM PDT. Clients recovered between 2:25 and 2:40 AM, "as cached DNS records expired".

</IncidentCard>

Map it to this lesson. The name was fine and the servers were fine. Only the list between them broke, and that was enough. Step 5 is the TTL dial from the section above: even after the fix, each client waited for its cached note to expire.

## Numbers

The cost of DNS depends on where the answer is found. The nearer the note, the faster the answer.

| Event | Cost | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Answer from the browser or operating system cache | ~0 ms | The note is on your own machine |
| Answer from the resolver cache | 1 round trip to the resolver, often ~1 to 10 ms | The note is at the shared assistant |
| Cold lookup, full walk | ~4 round trips; ~100 ms is a fair order of magnitude | Resolver, root, TLD, authoritative |
| Refresh after TTL expiry, resolver already knows the name servers | ~1 upstream round trip | The root and TLD steps are skipped |
| Load on authoritative servers, 100,000 resolvers, TTL 300 s | ~330 queries per second | 100,000 / 300 |
| Load on authoritative servers, same resolvers, TTL 60 s | ~1,700 queries per second | 100,000 / 60 |

The refresh can skip the root and TLD steps. Those answers carry very long TTLs, often a day or more. The resolver remembers who runs `.com` and who runs `example.com` long after the IP of `www` expires. So it asks the authoritative server directly. One upstream round trip is enough.

The cold cost is paid once per TTL per resolver, not once per user. This is why one resolver can serve millions of people.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Pick it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short TTL (30 to 60 s) | Changes spread fast. Failover is quick | More query load on your name servers. More cold lookups for users | Failover matters, or you plan to move |
| Long TTL (hours) | Low load. Fewer cold lookups | A change is slow. A bad IP lingers | The IP almost never changes |
| DNS-based balancing | Free, global, works with any server | Coarse. Blind to load. Slow to react | Choosing a region or a load balancer |
| Load balancer in front | Fine control. Instant reaction to a failed server | An extra hop and an extra component to run | Spreading requests inside one region |

## In practice

<Callout type="tip">
Say where DNS sits in the latency budget: "The first request pays for DNS, then TCP, then TLS. After that, the connection and the DNS answer are cached." TLS is the step after the TCP handshake where both sides agree on encryption, and it costs extra round trips of its own. For a global product, add geo-DNS or anycast. Either one sends users to a nearby region. Name the TTL you would use.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not build failover on DNS alone and promise "5 seconds". With a 60 second TTL, some users stay on the dead IP for a minute or more. Some resolvers also stretch TTLs. If you need fast failover, put a load balancer or anycast address in front. If you plan a migration, lower the TTL days before, not minutes before.
</Callout>
