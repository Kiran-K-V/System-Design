## The problem

You open a social app. A page of posts from people you follow appears, newest first. It must appear fast, every time, for every user.

The question that drives the design is simple. A feed is the answer to: "show the latest posts of everyone I follow". When do you compute that answer? When the user asks (a **pull**), or when each author posts (a **push**)? Each choice moves a large cost to a different place, and each has a case where it fails.

Sources for this topic are scarce. I did not reach a primary source with company numbers for this lesson. So every number below is a rough assumption. Do the same in an interview: state your assumptions, then move on to the design.

## Requirements

**Functional.**

1. A user publishes a post.
2. A user follows and unfollows other users.
3. A user views a home feed of posts from the accounts they follow, newest first.

**Below the line.** Ranking by a model, ads, comments and likes, search, and notifications. Ranking changes the order, but not the main cost structure.

**Non-functional.** Feed reads are fast. The system favors availability: a post may reach followers a few seconds late. Reads far outnumber writes.

**Scale.** Interview assumptions, kept rough.

- 100 million daily active users, each opening the feed 10 times a day. That is about **11,600 feed reads a second**.
- 1 in 10 users posts once a day. That is about **116 posts a second**.
- A typical user follows 500 accounts and has 200 followers.
- Reads outnumber writes by about 100 to 1.

## Core entities

- **Post:** `post_id`, `author_id`, `body`, `created_at`.
- **Follow:** `follower_id`, `followee_id`.
- **Feed entry:** `user_id`, `post_id`, and a time. This is the precomputed feed. Store the id only. The body is fetched from the posts table. One copy of a post body beats 200 copies.

## API

| Call | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `POST /posts` with `{ body }` | Publish. The author comes from the auth token |
| `POST /follows/{user_id}` and `DELETE /follows/{user_id}` | Follow and unfollow |
| `GET /feed?cursor=...` | One page of the feed and a cursor for the next page |

Use a cursor and not a page number, so new posts do not shift the pages ([lesson 3.3](/learn/03-api-design/pagination/)). A cursor can be the time and id of the last post the client saw.

## High-level design

Step through it. The design starts with the pull and then adds the push.

<NewsFeedBuild client:visible />

The first working design is a pull. To build a feed, find who the user follows. Then fetch each followee's recent posts, merge them by time, and cut a page. With indexes ([lesson 4.3](/learn/04-data/indexing/)) on `(author_id, created_at)` and on the follow pairs, this works for small systems. It also fits the ideas of [lesson 9.1](/learn/09-patterns/scaling-reads/): add a cache when reads dominate.

## Deep dive 1: pull or push

Switch between the designs below and step through each one.

<NewsFeedFanout client:visible />

**Fan-out on read** costs nothing at post time. All the cost lands on reads. With 500 followees, each feed read does 500 lookups. Across all readers, that is **millions of lookups a second**. Most of them fetch posts nobody has read yet. The feed gets slower for users who follow more people. The scatter-gather pattern from [lesson 4.7](/learn/04-data/sharding/) makes the slowest of 500 calls set the latency.

**Fan-out on write** moves the cost to post time. When a post is created, a queue ([lesson 6.1](/learn/06-async/queues/)) hands it to workers. A worker reads the author's follower list and adds the post id to each follower's feed. A feed read is then one lookup of a ready-made list, then a fetch of those posts by id. Reads are cheap and flat, no matter how many people the user follows.

The price is **write amplification**: one post becomes one write per follower. With about 200 followers on average, a modest stream of posts becomes a much larger stream of feed writes. That is fine. It runs in the background, off the request path, and a queue smooths bursts ([lesson 6.4](/learn/06-async/backpressure-dlq/)).

How big are the stored feeds? Keep only the newest 500 ids per user. Ids are small, so the raw ids for 100 million users fit in hundreds of GB (~400 GB). Real storage costs several times more, because every structure has overhead. Spread it over many shards (separate machines that each hold a slice of the data). If a user's feed is missing or has expired, rebuild it with the pull method from the posts table. The cache is a copy, not the truth.

A **sorted set** in Redis ([lesson 8.2](/learn/08-technologies/redis/)) suits a feed. It is a collection of unique items, each with a number called a score, and Redis keeps the items ordered by that score. Picture a leaderboard that re-sorts itself each time someone is added. Here the item is a post id and the score is the post time.

Two commands do the work. `ZADD` adds an item with its score. A range read returns the highest scores first, so the newest posts come out on top, and you can ask for just the top 20 for one page. Trimming to the newest 500 keeps each feed small.

It is also safe to repeat. Queue delivery can run a job twice ([lesson 6.3](/learn/06-async/delivery-semantics/)). Adding the same post id to a sorted set twice leaves one entry, so a retry does no harm.

## Deep dive 2: the celebrity problem

Fan-out on write assumes that an author has few followers. Some accounts have millions. Say one has 20 million followers and posts once. That is 20 million feed writes from a single post. Even with fast workers, the job takes **minutes, not milliseconds**. The last follower waits a long time. Meanwhile the queue backs up, and ordinary authors' posts wait behind the celebrity's job.

The fix is a **hybrid**. Pick a threshold, such as 100,000 followers. Authors below it are pushed. Authors above it are not pushed at all. At read time, the feed service does two things. It reads the user's ready-made list. It also reads the recent posts of the few celebrities the user follows, and merges them in by time. A typical user follows a handful of celebrities, so this adds a handful of lookups, not 500.

The hybrid has its own costs.

- **Two read paths** to keep correct. The merge must keep the order and the page cursor stable.
- **A threshold to tune.** Too low and you pull too much. Too high and a big account stalls the queue.
- **Celebrity posts are read very often.** They are the best candidates for a cache, since millions of readers fetch the same few rows.
- **Inactive users.** Do not push to users who have not opened the app for a month. Build their feed on demand when they return. This also cuts write amplification.

**Unfollow and delete.** If a user unfollows someone, old posts from that person may still sit in the feed list. Filter them at read time, or accept a short delay. Deleting a post is the same: the feed holds only an id. So the fetch finds the post missing and skips it.

## Trade-offs

| Approach | You gain | You pay | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan-out on read | Cheap posts. Always fresh. No feed storage | Slow, costly reads that grow with follows | Small systems, or users who follow few accounts |
| Fan-out on write | One cheap read. Flat latency | Write amplification. Storage. Slow for big accounts | Most authors have a modest follower count |
| Hybrid | Cheap reads and bounded writes | Two paths. A threshold to tune | Any real social graph with a long tail of huge accounts |
| Feed of ids only | One copy of each post | An extra fetch to turn ids into posts | Almost always |
| Eventual consistency | Availability and speed | A post may be seconds late | Social feeds, where a delay is harmless |

<Callout type="tip">
Say the trade in one line: "Pull makes reads slow, push makes writes big. I push for ordinary authors so reads are one lookup. I pull for celebrities so one post is not 20 million writes." Back it with one fact: "500 lookups per read, across every reader, is millions of lookups a second. So pull alone fails."
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "fan-out on write for everyone" without naming the celebrity problem. Do not say "fan-out on read for everyone" without showing the 500 lookups. Do not store the whole post in every feed: store ids. Do not claim strong consistency for a feed. And do not skip what happens on unfollow, delete, and a cold cache.
</Callout>

<Callout type="deep" title="Write amplification is a dial, not a switch">
The hybrid is one point on a line. At one end, push to nobody and pull everything. At the other, push to everyone. The threshold slides you along it. You can also slide per user: push to active users and skip dormant ones. Seeing the design as a dial turns "which is right?" into "what does my data say?" The follower distribution has a long tail, so the answer is almost never at an end.
</Callout>
