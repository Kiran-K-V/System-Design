Every large company runs a system that answers one question: is everything healthy right now? Lesson [7.5](/learn/07-reliability/observability/) explained what to measure and how to alert on it. This lesson builds the system that stores the measurements. The new ideas are four: a very high write rate, pull versus push, rollups with retention, and a limit on label cardinality.

## Requirements

**Functional.** The system must:

1. Ingest metrics (CPU, memory, latency, custom counters) from services.
2. Let people query and graph metrics with filters, aggregation, and time ranges.
3. Evaluate alert rules, such as "p99 latency above 500 ms for 5 minutes".
4. Send alerts to email, Slack, or a pager.

**Below the line.** Log search, distributed tracing, and anomaly detection by machine learning. They are different systems with different storage. Say so and move on.

**Non-functional.** These numbers are my assumptions for a very large fleet, not data from a company.

- 500,000 servers. Each exposes 100 series. Each series gives one point every 10 seconds.
- Dashboards return in seconds, even for weeks of data.
- An alert condition reaches the evaluator within 1 minute of the event.
- Alerts are reliable. Dashboards may be a few seconds stale.

## Core entities

- **Series.** One metric name plus one set of labels, for example `cpu_seconds{host="a1", region="eu"}`.
- **Sample.** A timestamp and a number, belonging to one series.
- **Rollup.** Min, max, sum, and count of one series over one time window.
- **Alert rule.** An expression, a `for` duration, a severity, and a route.
- **Alert instance.** One rule firing for one label set, in state inactive, pending, or firing.

## API

- `GET /metrics` on every service. The collector calls it. This is the pull path.
- `POST /v1/write` on a push gateway, for jobs too short to be scraped.
- `GET /v1/query?expr=...&start=...&end=...&step=...` for dashboards.
- `POST /v1/rules` and `GET /v1/alerts` for alert rules and their state.

### Pull or push

The collector in step 1 pulls. Prometheus works this way: its overview says time series collection "happens via a pull model over HTTP". Its FAQ lists the gains: you can reliably tell when a target is down, you can open a target in a browser to see its health, and you can start a second collector on a laptop. The FAQ's own verdict is mild: pulling is "slightly better" and "should not be considered a major point". That is the project's opinion, so repeat it as an opinion.

The strongest gain is the down signal. A scrape that fails is data. With push, silence is ambiguous: the service may be dead, or the network may be cut, or the service may have nothing to say.

Push still has a place. A batch job may live for only 5 seconds, so no scrape will catch it. Prometheus offers the Pushgateway for the outcome of such jobs, and its own docs warn about three costs. The gateway becomes a single point of failure. You lose the automatic `up` health signal. And it keeps old series forever, until someone deletes them.

## Deep dives

### Slow long-range queries

A dashboard panel asks for average CPU of 1,000 servers over 30 days, one dot per hour. Toggle the two designs and step through.

<MetricsRollup client:visible />

The fix is the one that lesson [10.2](/learn/10-advanced/time-series/) described for time series: write coarser copies of old data. The query service picks the coarsest resolution that still gives the step the user asked for. Prometheus has a related tool, recording rules. They "precompute frequently needed or computationally expensive expressions" and save the result as new series. That is the same trade: do the work once, at write time.

Keep min, max, sum, and count in each rollup, not only the average. The average hides a 30-second spike. Sum and count let you combine windows correctly.

Retention follows the same logic. A plan, as an assumption: raw points for 15 days, 1-minute rollups for 90 days, 1-hour rollups for 2 years. Old data is not read often, and its detail matters less. For reference, Prometheus keeps raw samples for 15 days by default.

### Absorbing 5 million points a second

The rate is 500,000 servers x 100 series / 10 s = 5,000,000 points per second. A single database node cannot take it. The design has three parts.

- **Many collectors.** Each owns a slice of the targets. A target is assigned by hashing its name, so a collector that dies hands its slice to the others (lesson [4.8](/learn/04-data/consistent-hashing/)).
- **A buffer.** Collectors write batches to a log (lesson [6.2](/learn/06-async/pubsub-logs/)). Writes to the store come from the log at the store's pace. A short spike becomes lag, not loss.
- **A partitioned store.** Partition by a hash of the series, so one series always lands on one node, and by time, so old partitions can be dropped or rolled up as a unit (lesson [4.7](/learn/04-data/sharding/)).

Series are replicated for safety (lesson [4.6](/learn/04-data/replication/)). A metric point is cheap to lose. A lost alert is not. So metrics may use a weaker replication than the alert path.

### Alert evaluation

An alert rule has two timers that people confuse. The **evaluation interval** is how often the rule runs, for example every 15 seconds. The **`for` duration** is how long the condition must stay true before the alert fires. Prometheus puts an active alert that has not yet lasted that long in the **pending** state.

The `for` window stops flapping. Without it, one slow request at 500.1 ms would page someone. With `for: 5m`, the condition must hold at every evaluation for 5 minutes.

The 1-minute target in our requirements is the delay before the evaluator sees the data. It is not the `for` window. Budget it: 10 s scrape interval, a few seconds in the buffer and the write, and up to 15 s until the next evaluation. About 30 seconds worst case, inside the minute.

Two failures matter here.

- **One evaluator dies.** Its alerts go quiet, and nobody notices. Run evaluators as a pair that both evaluate every rule, and let the alert manager remove duplicates. A silent monitoring system is worse than a loud one.
- **Rules are expensive.** Evaluating every rule against raw history every few seconds does not scale. Have rules read recent data only, from the store's hot tier or straight off the buffer. Pre-aggregate expensive expressions with recording rules.

### Cardinality

The number of series is the cost driver. It is the product of the label values. A counter with 20 routes, 4 status codes, and 100 hosts has up to 20 x 4 x 100 = 8,000 series. Add a `customer_id` label with 50,000 values, and the bound becomes 8,000 x 50,000 = 400 million.

Each series costs memory in the ingester and an entry in the index. A single careless label can double the bill or crash the store. The Prometheus instrumentation guide advises keeping metric cardinality below 10 for most metrics, and treating anything over 100 as a reason to rethink.

The control belongs at ingest. The collector counts the series each service owns, and drops new ones past a limit. It reports the drop as a metric of its own. The owning team gets paged about its own label, not the whole company about the store.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pull (scrape) | A free "target down" signal. Central control of rate and targets | Collectors must reach every target. Short jobs slip through | Long-running services in one network |
| Push | Works for short jobs and across firewalls | Silence is ambiguous. A gateway can bottleneck | Batch jobs, edge devices |
| Rollups | Fast long graphs, cheap storage | Old data loses detail | Any retention over a few days |
| Label limits | Bounded memory and cost | Some series are dropped | Always, at ingest |
| Long `for` window | Fewer false pages | Slower alert | Noisy signals. Use a short window for hard outages |

<Callout type="tip">
Name the two rates first: "5 million points a second in, a few thousand queries a second out." Then say why the design is split: writes go through a buffer into a partitioned store, reads go through a query service that picks a rollup. Finish with the alert path as its own small system with a pair of evaluators.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "pull is always better". Short jobs need push. Do not keep all data at full resolution forever, and do not alert on one sample over a threshold. Do not forget cardinality: an interviewer who asks "what if a team adds a user id label?" is checking exactly that.
</Callout>

<Callout type="deep" title="Why not a plain SQL table?">
A row per sample works at small scale, with an index on series and time. It fails for three reasons. Each row carries overhead that dwarfs the 16 bytes of payload. A time-ordered index on a table that takes millions of inserts a second is expensive to maintain. And dropping old data means deleting rows, not dropping a partition. Time-series stores keep points in time-ordered blocks with compressed deltas, so a series costs a couple of bytes per point and expiry is cheap.
</Callout>
