Every large company runs a system that answers one question: is everything healthy right now? The [Observability](/learn/backend/07-reliability/observability/) lesson explained what to measure and how to alert on it. This lesson builds the system that stores the measurements. The new ideas are four: a very high write rate, pull versus push, rollups with retention, and a limit on label cardinality. A label is a tag such as `region="eu"` on a measurement. Cardinality is the count of distinct values a label can take. The last deep dive shows why that count matters.

## Requirements

**Functional.** The system must:

1. Ingest metrics (CPU, memory, latency, custom counters) from services.
2. Let people query and graph metrics with filters, aggregation, and time ranges.
3. Evaluate alert rules, such as "p99 latency above 500 ms for 5 minutes". The p99 is the value that 99 out of 100 requests beat, so it shows how slow the slowest 1 in 100 feels.
4. Send alerts to email, Slack, or a pager. A pager is a service that rings the on-call engineer's phone, day or night.

**Below the line.** Log search, distributed tracing, and anomaly detection by machine learning. They are different systems with different storage. Say so and move on.

**Non-functional.** These numbers are my assumptions for a very large fleet, not data from a company.

- 500,000 servers. Each exposes 100 series. Each series gives one point every 10 seconds.
- Dashboards return in seconds, even for weeks of data.
- An alert condition reaches the evaluator within 1 minute of the event.
- Alerts are reliable. Dashboards may be a few seconds stale.

## Core entities

- **Series.** One measured thing over time, such as the CPU of one machine. It is one metric name plus one set of labels, for example `cpu_seconds{host="a1", region="eu"}`.
- **Sample.** A timestamp and a number, belonging to one series.
- **Rollup.** Min, max, sum, and count of one series over one time window. It is a short summary that stands in for many raw points, like a daily sales total in place of every receipt.
- **Alert rule.** An expression, a `for` duration, a severity, and a route.
- **Alert instance.** One rule firing for one label set, in state inactive, pending, or firing.

## API

- `GET /metrics` on every service. The collector calls it. A call like this is a scrape: the collector reads the service's current numbers. This is the pull path.
- `POST /v1/write` on a push gateway, for jobs too short to be scraped.
- `GET /v1/query?expr=...&start=...&end=...&step=...` for dashboards.
- `POST /v1/rules` and `GET /v1/alerts` for alert rules and their state.

### Pull or push

The collector in step 1 pulls. Prometheus works this way: its overview says time series collection "happens via a pull model over HTTP". Its FAQ lists the gains: you can reliably tell when a target is down. You can open a target in a browser to see its health. You can start a second collector on a laptop. The FAQ's own verdict is mild: pulling is "slightly better" and "should not be considered a major point". That is the project's opinion, so repeat it as an opinion.

The strongest gain is the down signal. A scrape that fails is data. With push, silence is ambiguous: the service may be dead, or the network may be cut. The service may instead have nothing to say.

Push still has a place. A batch job may live for only 5 seconds, so no scrape will catch it. Prometheus is a popular open-source metrics system. It offers the Pushgateway, a small service that holds the results that short jobs push to it until the collector scrapes them, and its own docs warn about three costs. The gateway becomes a single point of failure. You lose the automatic `up` health signal. And it keeps old series forever, until someone deletes them.

## Deep dives

### Slow long-range queries

A dashboard panel asks for average CPU of 1,000 servers over 30 days, one dot per hour.

The fix is the one that the [Time Series Databases](/learn/backend/10-advanced/time-series/) lesson described for time series: write coarser copies of old data. The query service picks the coarsest resolution that still gives the step the user asked for. Prometheus has a related tool, recording rules, which are saved queries that run on a timer. They "precompute frequently needed or computationally expensive expressions" and save the result as new series. That is the same trade: do the work once, at write time.

Keep min, max, sum, and count in each rollup, not only the average. The average hides a 30-second spike. Sum and count let you combine windows correctly.

Retention follows the same logic. A plan, as an assumption: raw points for 15 days, 1-minute rollups for 90 days, 1-hour rollups for 2 years. Old data is not read often, and its detail matters less. For reference, Prometheus keeps raw samples for 15 days by default.

### Absorbing 5 million points a second

The rate is about 5 million points per second (~500,000 servers, 100 series each, one point every 10 seconds). A single database node cannot take it. The design has three parts.

- **Many collectors.** Each owns a slice of the targets. A target is assigned by hashing its name. So a collector that dies hands its slice to the others (the [Consistent Hashing](/learn/backend/04-data/consistent-hashing/) lesson).
- **A buffer.** Collectors write batches to a log (the [Pub/Sub and Logs](/learn/backend/06-async/pubsub-logs/) lesson). Writes to the store come from the log at the store's pace. A short spike becomes lag, not loss.
- **A partitioned store.** Partition by a hash of the series, so one series always lands on one node. Also partition by time, so old partitions can be dropped or rolled up as a unit (the [Sharding](/learn/backend/04-data/sharding/) lesson).

Series are replicated for safety (the [Replication](/learn/backend/04-data/replication/) lesson). A metric point is cheap to lose. A lost alert is not. So metrics may use a weaker replication than the alert path.

### Alert evaluation

An alert rule has two timers that people confuse. The **evaluation interval** is how often the rule runs, for example every 15 seconds. The **`for` duration** is how long the condition must stay true before the alert fires. Prometheus puts an active alert that has not yet lasted that long in the **pending** state.

The `for` window stops flapping. Without it, one slow request at 500.1 ms would page someone. With `for: 5m`, the condition must hold at every evaluation for 5 minutes.

The 1-minute target in our requirements is the delay before the evaluator sees the data. It is not the `for` window. Budget it from three delays: the scrape interval, the time in the buffer and the write, and the wait for the next evaluation. Together they stay inside the minute.

Two failures matter here.

- **One evaluator dies.** Its alerts go quiet, and nobody notices. Run evaluators as a pair that both evaluate every rule, and let the alert manager remove duplicates. A silent monitoring system is worse than a loud one.
- **Rules are expensive.** Evaluating every rule against raw history every few seconds does not scale. Have rules read recent data only, from the store's hot tier (the fast storage that holds the newest data) or straight off the buffer. Pre-aggregate expensive expressions with recording rules.

### Cardinality

The number of series is the cost driver. It is the product of the label values. Take a counter with labels for route, status code, and host. Every combination of values can be its own series, so a few thousand series is normal. Add a `customer_id` label with tens of thousands of values, and the bound jumps by that same factor, into the hundreds of millions.

Each series costs memory in the ingester (the part of the store that accepts incoming writes) and an entry in the index. A single careless label can double the bill or crash the store. The Prometheus instrumentation guide advises keeping metric cardinality below 10 for most metrics. The guide also advises treating anything over 100 as a reason to rethink.

The control belongs at ingest. The collector counts the series each service owns, and drops new ones past a limit. It reports the drop as a metric of its own. The owning team gets paged about its own label, not the whole company about the store.

## Numbers

Treat every figure in this lesson as an assumption. The shape is what matters. Ingest is a huge, steady write stream. Rollups shrink old data a lot. A hidden label can multiply the series count.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pull (scrape) | A free "target down" signal. Central control of rate and targets | Collectors must reach every target. Short jobs slip through | Long-running services in one network |
| Push | Works for short jobs and across firewalls | Silence is ambiguous. A gateway can bottleneck | Batch jobs, edge devices |
| Rollups | Fast long graphs, cheap storage | Old data loses detail | Any retention over a few days |
| Label limits | Bounded memory and cost | Some series are dropped | Always, at ingest |
| Long `for` window | Fewer false pages | Slower alert | Noisy signals. Use a short window for hard outages |

<Callout type="tip">
Name the two rates first: "5 million points a second in, a few thousand queries a second out." Then say why the design is split: writes go through a buffer into a partitioned store. Reads go through a query service that picks a rollup. Finish with the alert path as its own small system with a pair of evaluators.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "pull is always better". Short jobs need push. Do not keep all data at full resolution forever, and do not alert on one sample over a threshold. Do not forget cardinality. An interviewer who asks "what if a team adds a user id label?" is checking exactly that.
</Callout>

<Callout type="deep" title="Why not a plain SQL table?">
A row per sample works at small scale, with an index on series and time. It fails for three reasons. Each row carries overhead that dwarfs the 16 bytes of payload. A time-ordered index on a table that takes millions of inserts a second is expensive to maintain. And dropping old data means deleting rows, not dropping a partition. Time-series stores keep points in time-ordered blocks with compressed deltas. So a series costs a couple of bytes per point, and expiry is cheap.
</Callout>
