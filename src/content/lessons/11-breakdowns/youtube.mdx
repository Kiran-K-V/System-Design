## The problem

A creator uploads a 5 GB file from a phone on a shaky connection. Minutes later, a viewer on a local train with a weak signal and another on home broadband both press play. Both should see the video start quickly and keep playing.

Three things break the obvious design of "save the file, serve the file":

- A single upload of 5 GB fails near the end, and the creator starts over.
- The file is in one format and one size. A viewer on a weak network stalls. A viewer on a phone downloads a picture far sharper than the screen needs.
- Every viewer fetches from the same storage, so the busiest video overloads it.

The three new ideas are in the fixes. One is **resumable upload**: send the file in numbered parts, so a failure costs one part and not the whole file. Another is a **transcoding pipeline** that is a graph of jobs. The last is **adaptive bitrate streaming** from a CDN. The next section defines each of these words, because the rest of the lesson relies on them.

## How video reaches a screen

You can code, but you may never have handled video. Eight words matter. Each one has a picture and a number.

**Frame, resolution, and codec.** A video is a stack of still pictures, called **frames**, shown about 30 times per second. The **resolution** is the size of one picture, counted in pixels (the tiny dots of colour). "1080p" means 1,080 rows of pixels, normally 1,920 pixels wide. Think of a mosaic: more tiles give a sharper picture, and cost more to store. Raw video is huge. One 1080p frame is 1,920 × 1,080 × 3 bytes (one byte each for red, green, blue), which is about 6.2 MB. At 30 frames per second that is about 187 MB per second, or about 1.5 Gbit per second. No home connection can carry that. A **codec** fixes this. A codec is a recipe that squeezes video into far fewer bytes, and a matching recipe that unpacks it for the screen. H.264 is a common one. Think of vacuum-packing clothes for a suitcase: the same clothes, a much smaller bag. At 5 Mbit per second, the squeezed video is about 300 times smaller than the raw one (1.5 Gbit/s divided by 5 Mbit/s).

**Bitrate.** The **bitrate** is how many bits one second of the squeezed video needs. Think of a tap: the bitrate is how much water the video needs per second, and your connection is the pipe. If the pipe is thinner than the tap, the cup runs dry and the picture freezes. A higher resolution needs a higher bitrate. In this lesson, 1080p uses 5 Mbit/s, 720p uses 3, 480p uses 1.5, and 240p uses 0.4. These four are our assumptions, not company data. A 300-second view at 3 Mbit/s moves 3 × 300 / 8 = 112.5 MB.

**Rendition and rung.** One version of the video at one resolution and one bitrate is a **rendition**. Imagine a ladder of renditions, from sharpest and heaviest at the top to blurriest and lightest at the bottom. Each step of the ladder is a **rung**. Why keep several? A viewer on fibre takes the top rung. A viewer on a weak signal takes a lower one and still sees a moving picture.

**Transcoding.** **Transcoding** means turning the uploaded file into the renditions. It decodes the original and encodes it again at each resolution and bitrate. Think of a translator who rewrites one book into four editions. It is slow and heavy on the CPU. We design it in the deep dive.

**Segment.** A **segment** is a short slice of one rendition, a few seconds long. Think of a book cut into pages: you can read page 40 without page 39 in your hand. We use 6 seconds, so a 10-minute video has 600 / 6 = 100 segments per rung. There is one catch. Squeezed video usually stores each frame as a change from the frame before it. That saves bytes but makes a frame useless alone. So the cutter starts each segment on a **keyframe**, a frame that stores the whole picture. Think of a flip-book that has one full drawing at the start of each chapter. A player can then start any segment cold.

**Playlist (manifest).** A **playlist** is a small text file that lists addresses. It is the table of contents. One playlist lists the rungs. Another lists every segment of one rung, in order, with each segment's length. We call the whole set the **manifest**. The player reads it first, then asks for segments by address.

**Adaptive bitrate streaming.** **Streaming** means the player plays the start of the video while it still downloads the rest. **Adaptive** means the player chooses a rung again for each segment, from how fast the last one arrived. Think of a scooter rider who shifts gear on a steep flyover. **HLS** (HTTP Live Streaming) is a widely used way to do this. Each segment is a plain file fetched with a normal web request, and the playlist tells the player where the files are. Another standard, DASH, works the same way with a different file format. The player keeps a **buffer**: video already downloaded and waiting to be shown. Think of a water tank between the pipe and the tap. When the tank empties, the picture **stalls**.

**CDN edge.** A **CDN** (content delivery network) is a fleet of cache servers placed in many cities (lesson [2.7](/learn/02-networking/cdn-proxies/)). One such server is an **edge**. Think of a factory (the **origin**, which owns the real files) and many corner shops (the edges) that stock copies. A request the edge can answer from its stock is a **hit**. A request it cannot answer, so it asks the origin, is a **miss**. A segment never changes after it is written, so it is ideal stock for a shop.

## Requirements

Follow the six-step method from lesson [0.3](/learn/00-orientation/design-method/). It asks for requirements, then entities, then the API, then a data flow, then a high-level design, then deep dives. We take them in that order.

**Functional, above the line:**

1. Upload a video.
2. Watch a video.

**Below the line:** search, comments, recommendations, channels, exact view counts, copyright checks. Counting views, for instance, is its own design (see the Top-K case study, [11.9](/learn/11-breakdowns/top-k/)).

**Non-functional.** The numbers are interview assumptions:

- Availability over consistency (lesson [4.9](/learn/04-data/cap-pacelc/)). A video that turns up a minute late in some region is acceptable.
- Uploads of tens of gigabytes must work, and must resume.
- Playback starts fast and does not stall on weak networks.
- Processing is asynchronous: the creator gets a status, not a held request.

## Core entities

- **Video**: `video_id`, uploader, title, status (`uploading`, `processing`, `ready`, `failed`).
- **Upload**: `upload_id`, `video_id`, the list of parts done. Each part has a number and an **ETag**, a short receipt string that the store returns for a stored part.
- **Rendition**: one version of the video at one bitrate and resolution.
- **Segment**: a few seconds of one rendition, stored as an object.
- **Manifest**: the playlists that list the renditions and their segments.

The metadata is small. It goes in a database partitioned by `video_id`, which means each video's row lives on one machine picked by hashing its id. The bytes go in blob storage, which keeps big files as whole objects under keys (lesson [8.11](/learn/08-technologies/blob-storage/)). Never put video in the database.

## API

- `POST /uploads` with `{title, size}` returns `{upload_id, part_urls}`. A **signed URL** is a web address that the server has stamped with permission to do one thing, such as store one part, for a short time. So the client can send bytes straight to storage without holding any password.
- `PUT <part_url>` sends one part and returns an ETag.
- `GET /uploads/{id}` lists the parts already stored. This is how a client resumes.
- `POST /uploads/{id}/complete` starts processing.
- `GET /videos/{id}` returns metadata and the manifest address. The manifest and segments themselves come from the CDN.

Now the data flow. Processing is a pipeline: raw file, segments, encodes, manifest. The deep dives draw it.

## Deep dives

### Resumable upload

The mechanism is multipart upload, which lesson [8.11](/learn/08-technologies/blob-storage/) covers, and the pattern is in lesson [9.6](/learn/09-patterns/large-blobs/). Think of moving house with boxes. If the van breaks down, you do not carry the furniture back to the old flat. You count the boxes that arrived and fetch the rest. Here is what it buys at video scale. Say a file is 10 GB and the part size is 8 MB.

- Parts: 10,000 MB / 8 MB = 1,250 parts.
- Time at 100 Mbit/s: 10 GB is 80 Gbit. The math is 80 Gbit / 0.1 Gbit/s = 800 s, about 13.3 minutes.
- Fail after 1,000 parts: a single PUT restarts at zero and repeats 800 s of work. With parts, the client asks `GET /uploads/{id}`, sees 1,000 done, and sends the other 250: 2,000 MB, about 160 s.

Two details keep this honest. First, the upload row has a status. A cleanup job aborts uploads that stay `uploading` for too long. Stored parts are billed until the upload ends, so an abandoned 10 GB upload would cost money forever. Second, the client can compute a **fingerprint** (a short checksum, a number computed from the bytes) of each part. After a phone restart, the client may not remember what it sent. It can ask the server for the stored parts and compare fingerprints. A part with the same number and the same fingerprint is skipped. A part that differs is sent again. This also catches a part that was cut short by a dropped connection.

### Transcoding as a graph of jobs

Transcoding is slow, so the design question is how to structure it. Two words first. A **DAG** (directed acyclic graph) is a set of jobs joined by arrows, where an arrow means "must finish first", and there are no loops. An **orchestrator** is the program that tracks which jobs are done and starts the next ones. Think of a kitchen with a head chef: the sauce and the pasta cook at the same time, and the plate is assembled only when both are ready. Compare the two shapes.

<YoutubeDagCompare client:visible />

The pipeline has these stages: split into segments; encode every (segment, rung) pair; extract audio and thumbnails (also independent); build the manifest after all encodes are done. A job can start the moment its parents are done. For a 10-minute video with 6-second segments and 4 rungs, that is 100 × 4 = 400 encode jobs. With 400 workers, the encode stage takes about as long as the slowest single job.

Three rules make it robust:

- **Idempotent jobs.** Idempotent means that doing it twice gives the same result as doing it once. The output key is `(video_id, rung, segment)`. A retry overwrites the same object and does not create a duplicate (lesson [3.4](/learn/03-api-design/idempotency/)).
- **A durable orchestrator.** Durable means it survives a crash. Temporal (lesson [8.9](/learn/08-technologies/temporal/)) keeps the graph state and retries a failed node. A queue with workers and visibility timeouts (lesson [6.1](/learn/06-async/queues/)) is the simpler alternative when the graph is fixed. The patterns are in lesson [9.7](/learn/09-patterns/long-running-tasks/).
- **A status the creator can see.** `processing` moves to `ready`, or to `failed` after retries run out. The creator never waits on an HTTP request.

### Adaptive bitrate streaming

The player holds the manifest. A **master playlist** lists the rungs, and for each rung it gives the bandwidth that rung needs and the address of a **media playlist**. The media playlist lists the segment addresses and their durations. Nothing here needs a special server. Every item is a file on a CDN.

How does the player choose? Standards say that players should switch rungs to match the network. They leave the choosing rule to each player. The rule in the picture below is our own simple one: take the highest rung whose bitrate is at or below half the measured speed. Why half? The speed changes second by second, and a margin keeps the buffer from draining. Real players use more careful rules that also watch how full the buffer is.

<YoutubeAbrWalk client:visible />

Why segments? A segment that starts on a keyframe can be decoded without earlier segments. So the player can switch rungs at any segment boundary. Why short ones? A short segment means a fast switch and a fast start, and each is a small, cacheable file. The price is more files and more requests.

### CDN delivery and hot videos

Segments are plain files, immutable once written. That is ideal for a CDN. The first viewer in a region causes a miss, and that miss pulls the segment from blob storage into the nearest edge. The next thousand viewers get hits. So a viral video overloads nobody: its segments sit on many edges, and each edge serves its own city.

What reaches the origin is the long tail. These are old videos that few people watch. Each edge drops their segments to make room for popular ones, so a request for one often misses. Say a video is watched three times a month in one city. The edge probably evicted the segment between views, so every view is a miss.

The metadata side has its own hot spot. The database is partitioned by `video_id`, so a viral video sends all its reads to one partition. Think of one checkout lane that every shopper picks. Put a cache in front of that database (lesson [5.2](/learn/05-caching/cache-placement/)) and let replicas (extra copies of the data that serve reads) absorb the rest. A video row changes rarely, so a cached copy stays correct for minutes.

## Numbers

The method is lesson [1.4](/learn/01-one-machine/estimation/). Here we keep only the conclusions.

**Upload side.** Uploads arrive at a steady rate of a few per second. Each one is large, so the raw bytes arriving add up to tens of gigabits per second. Every upload also becomes several renditions (copies at different bitrates). Together the renditions take about as much space as the raw file. So storage grows fast, and the encode work grows with it: each video fans out into hundreds of small jobs, one per segment per rung.

**Watch side.** Viewing dwarfs uploading. Many millions of people watch at once, and each one pulls a new segment every few seconds. The total data sent out to viewers is in the range of terabits per second (~5 Tbit/s under our assumptions). No origin can serve that. A CDN has to.

**The origin share.** A CDN with a high hit ratio sends only a small slice of those requests to the origin. The origin sees mostly the long tail of old, rarely watched videos.

The only company figure I can source is from YouTube's press page. That page says over 20 million videos are uploaded daily. The page shows no date, so I use it only as a rough sanity check. I quote no hours-per-minute or hours-watched figure, since I found no current primary source.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct multipart upload | No app server in the byte path, resume | Part bookkeeping, orphan cleanup | Any large file |
| Whole-file transcode | Simple | Slow, no partial retry | Tiny files only |
| DAG of segment jobs | Parallel, retry one node | An orchestrator to run | Long videos, many rungs |
| Few rungs | Less storage and compute | Coarser quality steps | Small catalogs |
| Many rungs | Better fit for each network | More storage and encode cost | A big, varied audience |
| Short segments | Fast start and switch | More requests and files | Interactive, mobile playback |
| CDN in front | Egress and latency drop | Cache fill on cold videos | Always for video |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say the three steps in order: "The client uploads directly in parts and can resume. An event starts a graph of jobs that cuts the video into segments and encodes each at several bitrates. The player reads a manifest and picks a bitrate per segment from the CDN." Then give the one fact that justifies the CDN. That fact is that total viewing egress is far more than any origin can serve.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not stream the raw upload to viewers. Do not transcode inside the upload request. Do not store video bytes in the database. Do not claim one file at one bitrate is enough for a weak network. And do not say exact real-time view counts are free: that is a different problem.
</Callout>

<Callout type="deep" title="Why not make segments longer?">
Longer segments mean fewer files and fewer requests, which saves CDN and request cost. But the player can only switch rungs at a boundary. The player must download a whole segment before it knows the speed. With 10-second segments a bad network is noticed late, and a start takes longer. Many services choose a few seconds as the compromise. The exact value is a tuning choice, not a law.
</Callout>
