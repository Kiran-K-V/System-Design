## The problem

A creator uploads a 5 GB file from a phone on a shaky connection. Minutes later, a viewer on a bus with a weak signal and another on fibre both press play. Both should see the video start quickly and keep playing.

Three things break the obvious design of "save the file, serve the file":

- A single upload of 5 GB fails near the end, and the creator starts over.
- The file is in one format and one size. A viewer on a weak network stalls. A viewer on a phone downloads a picture far sharper than the screen needs.
- Every viewer fetches from the same storage, so the busiest video overloads it.

The three new ideas are in the fixes. One is **resumable upload**. Another is a **transcoding pipeline** that is a graph of jobs. The last is **adaptive bitrate streaming** from a CDN.

## Requirements

Follow the six-step method from lesson [0.3](/learn/00-orientation/design-method/).

**Functional, above the line:**

1. Upload a video.
2. Watch a video.

**Below the line:** search, comments, recommendations, channels, exact view counts, copyright checks. Counting views, for instance, is its own design (see the Top-K case study, [11.9](/learn/11-breakdowns/top-k/)).

**Non-functional.** The numbers are interview assumptions:

- Availability over consistency (lesson [4.9](/learn/04-data/cap-pacelc/)). A video that turns up a minute late in some region is acceptable.
- Uploads of tens of gigabytes must work, and must resume.
- Playback starts fast and does not stall on weak networks.
- Processing is asynchronous: the creator gets a status, not a held request.

## Core entities

- **Video**: `video_id`, uploader, title, status (`uploading`, `processing`, `ready`, `failed`).
- **Upload**: `upload_id`, `video_id`, the list of parts done (number and ETag).
- **Rendition**: one version of the video at one bitrate and resolution.
- **Segment**: a few seconds of one rendition, stored as an object.
- **Manifest**: a text file that lists the renditions and their segments.

The metadata is small and goes in a database partitioned by `video_id`. The bytes go in blob storage. Never put video in the database.

## API

- `POST /uploads` with `{title, size}` returns `{upload_id, part_urls}`, signed URLs for each part.
- `PUT <part_url>` sends one part and returns an ETag.
- `GET /uploads/{id}` lists the parts already stored. This is how a client resumes.
- `POST /uploads/{id}/complete` starts processing.
- `GET /videos/{id}` returns metadata and the manifest address. The manifest and segments themselves come from the CDN.

The data-flow step matters here, because processing is a pipeline: raw file, segments, encodes, manifest. It is drawn in the deep dive below.

## Deep dives

### Resumable upload

The mechanism is S3 multipart upload, which lesson [8.11](/learn/08-technologies/blob-storage/) covers, and the pattern is in lesson [9.6](/learn/09-patterns/large-blobs/). Here is what it buys at video scale. Say a file is 10 GB and the part size is 8 MB.

- Parts: 10,000 MB / 8 MB = 1,250 parts.
- Time at 100 Mbit/s: 10 GB is 80 Gbit. The math is 80 Gbit / 0.1 Gbit/s = 800 s, about 13.3 minutes.
- Fail after 1,000 parts: a single PUT restarts at zero and repeats 800 s of work. With parts, the client asks `GET /uploads/{id}`, sees 1,000 done, and sends the other 250: 2,000 MB, about 160 s.

The upload row has a status. A cleanup job aborts uploads that stay `uploading` for too long, so stored parts do not cost money forever. The client may also compute a fingerprint of each part. So the client can tell the server "I already have this one" after a restart.

### Transcoding as a graph of jobs

**Transcoding** means re-encoding a video into other sizes and bitrates. It is slow and heavy on CPU. The design question is how to structure it. Compare the two shapes.

<YoutubeDagCompare client:visible />

A **DAG** (directed acyclic graph) is a graph of jobs where arrows mean "must finish first". There are no loops. The pipeline has these stages: split into segments; encode every (segment, rung) pair; extract audio and thumbnails (also independent); build the manifest after all encodes are done. A job can start the moment its parents are done.

Three rules make it robust:

- **Idempotent jobs.** The output key is `(video_id, rung, segment)`. A retry overwrites the same object (lesson [3.4](/learn/03-api-design/idempotency/)).
- **A durable orchestrator.** Temporal (lesson [8.9](/learn/08-technologies/temporal/)) keeps the graph state and retries a failed node. A queue with workers and visibility timeouts (lesson [6.1](/learn/06-async/queues/)) is the simpler alternative when the graph is fixed. The patterns are in lesson [9.7](/learn/09-patterns/long-running-tasks/).
- **A status the creator can see.** `processing` moves to `ready`, or to `failed` after retries run out. The creator never waits on an HTTP request.

### Adaptive bitrate streaming

**Adaptive bitrate** (ABR) means the player changes quality while the video plays. It needs the video in several versions, cut into short segments.

HLS is one standard, and it is public as RFC 8216. A **master playlist** lists **variant streams**, each with a `BANDWIDTH` value. Each variant points to a **media playlist**, a list of segment URLs with their durations. DASH works the same way with a different file format. The RFC says clients should switch between variants to adapt to the network. The RFC also says the switching algorithm itself is outside the spec. So the rule in the picture below is "highest rung at or below half the measured speed". This is an illustrative rule of ours. Real players use more careful ones that also watch how much video is buffered.

<YoutubeAbrWalk client:visible />

Why segments? A segment that can be decoded without earlier segments lets the player switch rungs at any boundary. HLS has a tag for this, `EXT-X-INDEPENDENT-SEGMENTS`. Why short ones? A short segment means a fast switch and a fast start, and each is a small, cacheable file. The price is more files and more requests.

### CDN delivery and hot videos

Segments are plain files, immutable once written. That is ideal for a CDN (lesson [2.7](/learn/02-networking/cdn-proxies/)). The first viewer in a region causes a miss. The next thousand are hits. A viral video overloads nobody, because its segments sit on many edges. What hits the origin is the long tail: old, rarely watched videos that miss often. The metadata database, partitioned by `video_id`, sees a hot partition for a viral video. So put a cache in front of that database (lesson [5.2](/learn/05-caching/cache-placement/)) and let replicas absorb reads.

## Numbers

All inputs are assumptions. The method is lesson [1.4](/learn/01-one-machine/estimation/).

**Upload side**

- 1M uploads per day. 1,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ 11.6 uploads per second.
- Raw size 500 MB on average. Ingest ≈ 11.6 × 500 MB ≈ 5.8 GB per second, which is about 46 Gbit per second.
- Ladder of four rungs at 5, 3, 1.5, and 0.4 Mbit/s: total 9.9 Mbit/s. A 10-minute video is 600 s. The math is 9.9 Mbit/s × 600 s / 8 = 742 MB of renditions. That is close to the raw size.
- Stored per day: 1M × 742 MB ≈ 742 TB of renditions. You also store 500 TB of raw, if you keep it.
- Jobs per video: 600 s / 6 s = 100 segments × 4 rungs = 400 encode jobs.

**Watch side**

- 500M views per day, each 5 minutes (300 s) at 3 Mbit/s on average.
- Bytes per view: 3 Mbit/s × 300 s / 8 = 112.5 MB. Per day: 500M × 112.5 MB ≈ 56 PB.
- Average egress: 56 PB / 86,400 s ≈ 650 GB per second ≈ 5.2 Tbit per second. No origin can serve that. A CDN has to.
- Average viewers at once: 500M × 300 s / 86,400 ≈ 1.7M. Each fetches one segment per 6 s: about 290,000 segment requests per second.
- If the CDN hits 95% of the time (an assumption), the origin sees 5%: about 14,500 requests per second.

The only company figure I can source is from YouTube's press page. That page says over 20 million videos are uploaded daily. The page shows no date, so I use it only as a rough sanity check. It is about 20 times the upload rate above. I quote no hours-per-minute or hours-watched figure, since I found no current primary source.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct multipart upload | No app server in the byte path, resume | Part bookkeeping, orphan cleanup | Any large file |
| Whole-file transcode | Simple | Slow, no partial retry | Tiny files only |
| DAG of segment jobs | Parallel, retry one node | An orchestrator to run | Long videos, many rungs |
| Few rungs | Less storage and compute | Coarser quality steps | Small catalogs |
| Many rungs | Better fit for each network | More storage and encode cost | A big, varied audience |
| Short segments | Fast start and switch | More requests and files | Interactive, mobile playback |
| CDN in front | Egress and latency drop | Cache fill on cold videos | Always for video |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say the three steps in order: "The client uploads directly in parts and can resume. An event starts a graph of jobs that cuts the video into segments and encodes each at several bitrates. The player reads a manifest and picks a bitrate per segment from the CDN." Then give the one number that justifies the CDN. That number is average egress in the terabits per second.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not stream the raw upload to viewers. Do not transcode inside the upload request. Do not store video bytes in the database. Do not claim one file at one bitrate is enough for a weak network. And do not say exact real-time view counts are free: that is a different problem.
</Callout>

<Callout type="deep" title="Why not make segments longer?">
Longer segments mean fewer files and fewer requests, which saves CDN and request cost. But the player can only switch rungs at a boundary. The player must download a whole segment before it knows the speed. With 10-second segments a bad network is noticed late, and a start takes longer. Many services choose a few seconds as the compromise. The exact value is a tuning choice, not a law.
</Callout>
