## The problem

You must pick a model for a product. The options differ in size by 50 times: 8 billion parameters, 70 billion, 405 billion. A bigger model is usually better. It is also slower and costs more for every request, for as long as the product lives.

Behind that choice sits another one that someone else made. This second choice covers how big to build the model. It also covers how much text to train it on. A training run costs a fixed budget of compute. The same budget can buy a big model trained briefly, or a small model trained long. **Scaling laws** are the measured rules for how loss falls. Loss is the model's error on held-out text. It falls as you add parameters (N), training tokens (D), or compute (C).

For you, the reader, the rules answer two questions. Why do small models today match big models from two years ago? And what does model size do to cost per request? The [AI Design Method](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/ai-design-method/) lesson gave the formula for [cost per request](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/ai-design-method/). This lesson shows what sets the number behind the price.

## Two rules of thumb

**Training: about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token.** A FLOP is one floating-point operation, an add or a multiply. Kaplan et al. (2020) write the training compute as C ≈ 6N per token. The forward pass costs about 2N: each weight takes one multiply and one add. The backward pass, which computes the gradients, costs about twice the forward pass. So 2N + 4N = 6N. Over D tokens:

<Math block tex="C \approx 6\,N\,D" />

**Serving: about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token.** Serving runs only the forward pass. A bigger model costs more per generated token in direct proportion to its size. These are floor estimates. They ignore attention over long prompts, which the [Attention and Its Cost](/learn/ai/a1-model-function/attention-cost/) lesson covers, and Kaplan notes the same for training (their model width is much larger than the context length divided by 12).

Together the two rules explain the economics: training is a big one-time bill, and serving is a smaller bill paid on every token for the life of the product.

## Naive solution: build the biggest model you can afford

Early scaling work (Kaplan et al., 2020) found smooth power laws. Loss falls as a power of N (exponent about 0.076) and of D (about 0.095). Their advice for a fixed compute budget: put most of it into model size. The optimal N grows as C to the power 0.73, so most of each increase in compute should go into more parameters and only a little into more data.

Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) tested the advice and found it wrong. Their three fitting methods agree. N and D should grow at close to the same rate. Their exponents for N are 0.50, 0.49, and 0.46. Kaplan's 0.73 is the outlier. The paper names a cause. Kaplan et al. used a fixed number of training tokens and a fixed learning-rate schedule for all models. Small-data runs were then judged on an unfinished schedule. The unfinished schedule "contributes to the conclusion that model size should increase faster than training data size". Chinchilla matched the schedule to the data.

The proof was a head-to-head. Chinchilla used the same compute as Gopher (280B parameters). But Chinchilla was 4 times smaller, 70B. It was also trained on 4 times more data. It scored higher on MMLU, a 57-subject knowledge test. The paper says it "uniformly and significantly outperforms" Gopher. As a bonus, a 4x smaller model has a smaller memory footprint and a lower inference cost.

## The mechanism: split the budget

Fix C. Then C = 6ND leaves one free choice. Chinchilla's Table 3 lists, for each size, the number of tokens that sits on the best frontier:

| Parameters | Tokens | Tokens per parameter |
|---|---|---|
| 1B | 20.2B | 20.2 |
| 10B | 205.1B | 20.5 |
| 67B | 1.5T | 22.4 |
| 175B | 3.7T | 21.1 |
| 280B | 5.9T | 21.1 |

The paper does not state a ratio. We derived it: dividing each row gives 20 to 22. So "about 20 tokens per parameter" is our reading of Table 3, a handy rule and not an exact law. Set D = 20N, and for a given budget the best model size follows directly.

For comparison, we use the Kaplan rule extrapolated from its fit. This is our derivation from Kaplan's Table 5.

1. **Check the table.** The Chinchilla split here matches the paper's Table 3.
2. **The two rules split apart.** At one large budget, Chinchilla picks a mid-size model with lots of data. Kaplan's rule picks a far bigger model with far less data.
3. **Why it matters for model choice.** Following the Kaplan rule, you would build a model many times larger. Every request would pay for all those extra weights. That model would also have seen too little data.
4. Llama 3 8B sits far below the optimal line for its own budget. That choice was deliberate.

## Training once, serving forever

Chinchilla answers "which model gives the lowest loss for a training budget?" Most teams ask a different question. Their question is "which model gives a given quality for the lowest total cost, training plus serving?" Training is paid once. Serving is paid for every token, for the whole life of the product.

Meta trained Llama 3 8B on over 15T tokens. That is far more data than the compute-optimal amount for its size, by roughly two orders of magnitude (our estimate from the 20-per-parameter rule). Meta reports that the 8B and 70B models "continued to improve log-linearly" up to 15T tokens. So the extra data kept paying off. The 405B model is much closer to the rule.

Why train a small model so long? Because every served token costs 2N. A small model is several times cheaper to serve on every token. Extra training buys quality, and a small N keeps the serving bill small.

With the Chinchilla fit (Eq. 10 of the paper), the 70B model on 1.4T tokens and the 8B model on 15T tokens land at almost the same loss. That is an extrapolation far outside the data the fit was made from. Real models differ in data quality. So read it as "similar, by this fit", not as a measured result.

The cost story is the point. The small model costs more to train, but each served token is cheaper. After enough served tokens (roughly a trillion, our estimate), the small model has paid back the extra training, and the gap grows with traffic. A busy product gets there fast.

| Model | Parameters | Training tokens | Tokens per parameter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Llama 3 8B | 8B | 15T | far above 20 |
| Llama 3 70B | 70B | 15T | well above 20 |
| Llama 3 405B | 405B | 15.6T | closer to 20 |

Parameters and token counts are from Meta's blog and the Llama 3 paper. The last column is our reading of the ratio.

FLOPs are not dollars. A provider's price is set by hardware, utilization, and margin. But the direction is clear. On the Anthropic model overview page (as of 2026-10-15), the smaller models list much lower prices per million tokens than the larger ones, and output costs more than input. At high volume, that gap decides which model you can afford. Anthropic does not publish parameter counts, so we cannot say the price follows size. The [models overview page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/overview) is the source for these numbers, and [the Build, Buy, or Fine-tune lesson](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/build-buy-finetune/) shows when renting a GPU for a small model beats the API.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large model | Higher quality on hard tasks | Cost and latency on every token | Hard reasoning, low volume |
| Small model, trained long | Low cost per token, fast | Higher quality is capped, hard tasks fail | High volume, narrow tasks |
| Compute-optimal sizing (about 20 tokens per parameter) | Best loss per training FLOP | Higher serving cost than a smaller model | You train once, serve little |
| Over-training (far beyond 20) | Better loss at a small N | Training cost rises with D | You expect heavy serving |
| Distillation | A small model learns from a big one | A second training pipeline | You need big-model quality at small size |

Distillation, from Hinton et al. (2015), aims to "compress the knowledge in an ensemble of models into a single model". We read only the abstract, so we say no more about the method here.

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Tie size to money in one line: "Serving costs 2N FLOPs per token. So an 8B model is several times cheaper per token than a 70B one. At a million requests a day I would start with the small model, measure it on my golden set, and move up only for the requests where it fails." Then route easy requests to the small model.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "bigger is always better". And do not quote "20 tokens per parameter" as if it were a law of nature. It is a rule for the cheapest training run at a given loss. It ignores the cost of serving, which is why new models break it on purpose. Also do not claim the Chinchilla rule is exact. Table 3 gives about 20 to 22.
</Callout>
