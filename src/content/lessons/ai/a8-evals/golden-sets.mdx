## The problem

A team must pick a model for its SQL assistant. Model X scores 95% on a well-known public benchmark of SQL questions. Model Y scores 91%. The team also built its own test, using 87 questions taken from real customer chats. On that test, X scores 60% and Y scores 78%.

Which numbers should the team trust?

The answer decides money and customer experience, and the two tests disagree about which model is better. This lesson shows how to build the team's own test so you can trust it, and why a public score can mislead. It answers six questions:

1. What is a golden set, and where do its cases come from?
2. How do you cover the rare cases?
3. How do you avoid fooling yourself while you tune?
4. How do you know the labels are right?
5. How many cases do you need?
6. Why can a public benchmark be wrong for you?

## Question 1: what is a golden set, and where do its cases come from?

In the [Eval-Driven Design](/learn/ai/a8-evals/eval-driven-design/) lesson, an eval was a fixed set of test cases plus a grader. A **golden set** is the best version of that set. Each case has an input, a verified label (pass or fail, or the right answer), and a tag for the scenario it covers. "Golden" means you trust it more than anything else you have.

An everyday picture: the master copy of a map. Every other map is checked against it, so errors in the master spread everywhere.

Where do the cases come from? From your own users, first. The [Error Analysis](/learn/ai/a8-evals/error-analysis/) lesson showed how to read real traces and find failure types. Those traces are the raw material. Real traces have your real wording, your real mess, and your real mix of problems. Nobody on your team would invent them.

Shopify's engineers describe the same move in their [2025 post on building their Sidekick assistant](https://shopify.engineering/building-production-ready-agentic-systems). They moved away from hand-made "golden" datasets to what they call Ground Truth Sets that "reflect actual production distributions". They sample real conversations and write the grading rules from what they see. They advise having at least three product experts label each set of conversations.

## Question 2: what do real traces miss?

Real traces over-represent what is common. A refund question may appear 200 times and a warranty claim in a messy format may appear twice. Rare scenarios still matter. A rare one may be the one that costs you a customer, or breaks a law.

So you need a way to add cases for the rare scenarios. A **synthetic** case is one you write or generate on purpose, not one that came from a real user. Think of a fire drill. You do not wait for a real fire to test the exits.

## Question 3: how do you avoid fooling yourself?

You will change your prompt many times to raise the score. After enough changes, the prompt fits the test cases the way a student fits last year's exam. The score stops measuring skill and starts measuring memory. This is called **overfitting to the test**.

The fix is old and simple: keep some cases back and do not look at them while you tune.

## Question 4: how do you know the labels are right?

A golden set is only as good as its labels. People disagree. Two experts may label the same answer differently. You need a way to measure that, and a way to decide what "good enough" means.

## The naive way: write 30 questions and trust a leaderboard

The first plan is to have one engineer write 30 questions from memory, label them alone, and test on them. The second plan is to trust a public score. Both fail.

The 30 questions come from one head. They cover what that engineer thought of, not what users ask. One person's labels cannot be checked. And 30 cases give only a wide range of what the true pass rate might be.

The public score has its own flaws, covered in Idea 6. In short: the questions may have been seen during training, and the leaderboard may favor those who tuned for it.

## The real fix: six ideas

### Idea 1: start from real traces and tag the scenarios

**What it is.** Take cases from the failures and the typical traces you read in A8.2. Tag each one with a scenario (what the user wanted) and a variant (typical, messy, edge case). Write the expected result or the rule it must follow. Include failures. Hamel Husain's guidance on judge sets suggests about 30 to 50 passing and 30 to 50 failing examples in each of the dev and test sets, so the set is not mostly easy passes. A set of easy passes hides regressions.

**Picture.** A coverage map of a city. Each cell is a district. You want at least a few test visits to every district.

**Why it exists.** Tags let you see where the set is thin, and report results per scenario, not as one blended number.

**Number.** Airbnb's engineering post on eval-driven development gives a guide, not a measurement: "Create a golden dataset of 50–100 examples", and it "MUST include bad examples". That is a good size for a first version.

### Idea 2: fill gaps with synthetic cases, built from dimensions

**What it is.** Look at the coverage map and add cases to the thin cells. Do not ask a model for "20 test questions". Hamel's FAQ warns that this gives generic, repetitive output. Use his structured method:

1. Pick **dimensions**, which are the ways user requests vary. For a support bot: issue type, how upset the customer is, and whether there is earlier conversation.
2. Write about 20 combinations by hand. Each one is a tuple, one value per dimension, such as (warranty claim, angry, no history).
3. Ask a model to generate more tuples, and then, in a separate step, turn each tuple into a realistic message. Two steps give less repetitive wording than one.
4. Run the messages through the real system and read the traces.

Fix known problems first. If the prompt already ignores a rule, fix the prompt before you write test cases for it.

**Picture.** A casting call. You do not ask for "some actors". You write what each part needs, and then audition for each part.

**Why it exists.** You can start before you have users, and you can reach scenarios that real data rarely shows.

**Number.** In the animation, 68 real cases leave 7 thin or empty cells. Adding 19 synthetic cases brings every cell to at least 3, for 87 in total.

One warning. Once you add rare cases, the set no longer mirrors your traffic. The pass rate over all 87 is not the production pass rate. Report the result per scenario, or weight each scenario by its real share of traffic.

### Idea 3: split the set, and version it

**What it is.** Split the labeled cases into two parts: a **dev** part you look at and tune against, and a **test** part you keep for honest scores. For the labels you use to check an LLM judge (the [Code Checks and LLM Judges](/learn/ai/a8-evals/judges/) lesson), Hamel's guide suggests 10 to 20% for a small training part and 40 to 45% each for dev and test, with 30 to 50 of each label in dev and test. Then **version** the set: give it a name such as golden-v1, never edit it in place, and make golden-v2 for any change.

**Picture.** A teacher keeps back an exam the class has never seen. Practice questions are for study. The final is for the grade.

**Why it exists.** Tuning on every case turns the score into a memory test. Versions make scores comparable. A score on v1 and a score on v2 are not the same measurement.

**Number.** The 87 cases split into 44 dev and 43 test. Each half has about 43 to 44 cases. We will see next why even that is a small sample.

### Idea 4: measure the labels, not just the answers

**What it is.** Have two people label a sample of the cases and compare. The simple measure is raw agreement: the share of cases where both gave the same label. But raw agreement misleads when most cases have one answer. The better measure is **Cohen's kappa**, a number that corrects for the agreement you would get by guessing. Kappa is 0 when the labelers agree no more than chance, and 1 when they agree perfectly.

**Picture.** Two people guess at heads or tails with a mostly-heads coin. They will agree most of the time with no skill at all. Kappa subtracts that free agreement.

**Why it exists.** If the people cannot agree, the label is not a fact. A judge or a model cannot be held to a standard that people do not share.

**Example.** Two labelers each mark nine in ten cases as pass, and they agree on nine in ten cases. Raw agreement sounds great. But two people who pass most cases would often match by luck. Once you remove that luck, kappa is only about 0.4, so a large part of the apparent quality was chance. Shopify reported a human baseline of 0.69 on its Sidekick labels, and Airbnb's guide notes: "Perfect agreement isn't achievable — even humans disagree."

### Idea 5: count how many cases you need

**What it is.** The number of cases decides how finely you can tell two versions apart. A small set gives a wide range of doubt around your measured pass rate. A bigger set narrows it. Halving the range costs about four times the cases.

**Picture.** A poll. Asking 10 people tells you almost nothing about a city. Asking 1,000 tells you quite a lot.

**Why it exists.** Teams read a 3-point gain on 100 cases as real. It is not.

**Example.** On a set of about 100 cases, a gap of a few points between two versions can be pure noise. Testing both on the same cases and counting flips, as in the [Eval-Driven Design](/learn/ai/a8-evals/eval-driven-design/) lesson, does much better. It is why a 100-case set can still guide you.

### Idea 6: assume public benchmarks leak, and measure how much

**What it is.** A public benchmark is a published test set that many people use to compare models. **Contamination** means some of its test items appeared in a model's training data. A model that has seen the answer is not being tested on skill. The first piece of evidence is old. A 2021 study documenting the C4 training corpus by Dodge and colleagues found "evaluation examples from other benchmark NLP datasets" inside it. A 2023 position paper by Sainz and colleagues states that contamination "causes an overestimation of the performance of a contaminated model", and also that its extent "is unknown, as it is not straightforward to measure".

**Picture.** A student who found last year's exam paper in the library. A high mark may be skill, or it may be memory. You cannot tell from the mark.

**Why it exists as a risk.** Models train on huge copies of the web. Benchmarks sit on the web. And with a closed model you cannot inspect the training data.

**Number.** In 2024, researchers wrote a fresh set of grade-school math problems, GSM1k, built to match the well-known GSM8k in style and difficulty. Several models scored lower on the new set, with accuracy drops of up to 8%. A model's tendency to produce GSM8k examples tracked the size of its drop (a Spearman r-squared of 0.36). That is a controlled test of memorization.

How do you look for overlap when you can see the training text? The GPT-3 paper (2020) did it with **n-grams**: runs of n words in a row. It flagged any test item that shared a 13-word run with the training data, removed those, and re-scored the "clean" part.
The checker shows both limits. Short runs flag innocent items, and long runs miss a rewrite of the same problem. An overlap scan finds copies. It does not find paraphrases.

What can you do when you cannot see a closed model's training data? Four defenses:

- **Keep your own set private.** Never paste it into a public repo or a public prompt.
- **Build a fresh matched set.** As GSM1k did, write new cases in the style of the public ones, and see whether the score falls.
- **Use held-out splits.** The SWE-Bench Pro coding benchmark (2025) has 1,865 problems from 41 repositories. Its public part uses 11 of those repositories, a held-out part uses 12, and a commercial part uses 18 private ones. The held-out and commercial problems are not public. The authors call it a "contamination-resistant testbed".
- **Rotate.** Retire items from a set after they have been seen, and add new ones.

## Numbers

Quality bar for the set: every scenario has at least 3 cases, at least two people labeled a sample, and kappa on that sample is at least about 0.6 (Shopify's judge reached 0.61 against a human baseline of 0.69). A full run of the set finishes in a few minutes and costs little.

Labeling is the bigger bill. Every case needs a person to read it and judge it, and a second person must check a sample. People are the cost, not the model calls. Keep the set small enough to maintain and large enough to see the changes you care about.

## Trade-offs

| Source of cases | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real traces | True wording and mix, finds surprises | Slow to collect, privacy work, rare cases are rare | Always the first source |
| Synthetic cases | Fast, targets rare scenarios, no users needed | May not sound real, cannot show how common a failure is | Filling thin cells, and starting before launch |
| Public benchmark | Comparable across teams and models | May be in training data, may not match your task | Shortlisting models, never the final choice |
| Private held-out set | Trusted score for your task | Not comparable across teams, costs labeling time | Choosing a model and gating a release |

## What experienced teams know

1. **Contamination often matters less than feared, so measure it before you panic.** The GPT-3 paper (Brown et al., 2020) removed any test item that shared a 13-gram with its training data, then compared scores. It reported that potential contamination was often high, with a quarter of benchmarks over 50% flagged, yet "in most cases performance changes only negligibly". It found real evidence for only a few tasks, such as PIQA and Winograd. Do the clean-subset comparison. If the score does not change, the leak did not help.

2. **A fresh matched set is the best detector you can run on a closed model.** You cannot read the training data of a model behind an API. You can write 40 new questions in the style of a public set and see whether the score drops. The [GSM1k study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00332) found drops of up to 8%. Keep a small stash of unpublished cases for this check.

3. **An n-gram scan finds copies and misses rewrites.** A rewrite of a test item, the same problem in new words, can pass a 13-word scan as clean. A translated or paraphrased leak passes the scan. So treat a clean scan as "no copies", not "no contamination".

4. **A leaderboard is a target people tune against.** The [Leaderboard Illusion paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879) (April 2025) studied the Chatbot Arena rankings. It found that some providers test many private variants and publish the best. Meta tested 27 private variants before the Llama 4 release. A ranking built that way is biased toward the winner of the private contest. Use rankings to build a shortlist. Use your own private set to choose.

5. **Raw agreement flatters. Kappa tells the truth.** Two labelers who pass 9 of 10 cases and agree 90% of the time score a kappa of only about 0.44. If you report agreement, report kappa next to it. Set the target from the human ceiling: Shopify's judges were held to a human baseline of 0.69, not to 1.0.

6. **A model retirement notice is an eval deadline.** Anthropic's [deprecations page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations) promises at least 60 days' notice before retirement of a publicly released model, and advises testing replacements "well before the retirement date". On 2026-09-30 it announced that Claude Sonnet 4.5 retires on 2026-11-30, with claude-sonnet-5-5 as the recommended replacement. That leaves under two months. If your golden set is versioned and runs in minutes, the migration is an afternoon. If not, it is a scramble.

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "I build the golden set from real traces, tagged by scenario, then fill thin scenarios with synthetic cases built from dimensions. I split it into dev and test and version it. Two people label a sample and I report kappa. I size the set for the effect I need to see, and I compare versions case by case. For model choice I use a private set, because public benchmarks can leak into training data." This covers sources, split, labels, size, and contamination.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "we picked the model with the best benchmark score". Do not tune on every case and then report the same cases as the result. Do not claim a clean n-gram scan proves a benchmark is clean. And do not report one blended pass rate over a set where you added rare cases on purpose.
</Callout>
