## The problem

A team runs a support chatbot for an online shop. Lesson [A8.1](/learn/ai/a8-evals/eval-driven-design/) told them to build an eval. So they built a dashboard with three scores from 1 to 5: helpfulness, tone, and safety. Every score sits near 4.2. The dashboard is green.

Customers still write, "The bot told me I have 30 days to return this. I do not." Support agents say the same thing every week. The dashboard does not move.

Why? The team chose the three scores before they looked at a single real conversation. They guessed what could go wrong. They guessed wrong. The real failures, such as a made-up return window, fit none of the three scores.

This lesson answers five questions:

1. What do you look at, and where?
2. Where do the categories of failure come from?
3. What do you write down for each failure?
4. How do notes become a ranked list of problems?
5. When have you read enough?

It matters because the whole eval module depends on it. The tests in lesson [A8.3](/learn/ai/a8-evals/golden-sets/) and the judges in lesson A8.4 should come from this step, not from guesses. Hamel Husain, who has helped many companies set up evals, writes in his [FAQ on evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) that his teams spend "60-80% of our development time on error analysis and evaluation". This is where most of the work is.

## Question 1: what do you look at, and where?

You look at **traces**. A trace is the full record of one run of your AI system. For a chatbot, it holds the customer's message, the prompt your code built, any documents the system fetched, the model's reply, and anything else that happened on the way. Think of it as the flight recorder of one conversation. When a plane has a problem, investigators do not read a satisfaction survey. They read the recorder.

Why traces and not just final answers? A wrong answer has a cause somewhere earlier. The final text says that the answer was wrong. The trace shows why. Maybe the system fetched the wrong document. Maybe the prompt lost a rule. Maybe the model ignored a fact that was right there in the context.

A made-up example to size it: a chat with 6 turns, where each turn has a customer message, a fetched document, a tool call, and a reply, holds 6 x 4 = 24 events. Reading only the last reply shows 1 of them.

Reading traces needs to be easy, or people stop doing it. Hamel's [guide to evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/) says to "remove all friction from the process of looking at data". He notes that a small custom viewer for your own traces, built with a tool such as Streamlit or Gradio, takes less than a day. Show the whole conversation on one screen, with a box for a note and a pass or fail button. Lesson A9.1 covers how to record traces in the first place.

## Question 2: where do the categories of failure come from?

You might think you can list them up front: wrong facts, bad tone, refusals. Why can you not?

Because you do not know what your system gets wrong until you read what it does. A 2024 study of people who grade model outputs, "Who Validates the Validators?", describes this directly. The researchers call it **criteria drift**. In their words, "grading outputs helps users define criteria". People need rules to grade, and they need to see outputs to know what rules they want. Some rules only appear after the third odd answer.

An everyday picture: you cannot write the review form for a restaurant before you eat there. Only after the meal do you learn that "the waiter brought the wrong dish" is a thing you care about.

So the plan is to let the categories come out of the data. That is the opposite of how most teams start.

## The naive way: pick metrics first, or let a model read for you

Two shortcuts are common. Both fail.

**Shortcut 1: ready-made metrics.** Many tools offer scores such as "helpfulness", "coherence", and "toxicity". You switch them on. The numbers look scientific. Hamel's FAQ is blunt about this: generic evaluations "waste time and create false confidence when you use them as quality measures". The 4.2 on the dashboard above is an example. It is a number with no link to what customers complain about. These scores are good for one thing: finding traces that are worth reading.

**Shortcut 2: ask a model to read all the traces and list the problems.** It is fast. It is also blind in the same places your system is. A model given your categories will see only those categories. Hamel's guidance on automation says to read raw traces yourself at the start: "Never skip this or delegate it."

Neither shortcut lets the data surprise you. Surprise is the point.

## The real fix: read, note, group, count, stop

The method has five ideas. Langfuse, a tool vendor, published a five-step version in August 2025. The steps are: gather traces, take notes, group them, label and count, then decide what to do.

### Idea 1: write a note on the first thing that went wrong

**What it is.** Read each trace. Write one short free-text note on the first thing that went wrong. Use no fixed categories yet. The Langfuse guide says: "No predefined categories yet — let the failures define themselves." The common name for this step is **open coding**: you tag the data in your own words, with no menu. Also mark the trace pass or fail. Hamel recommends that the note be detailed enough to use later as an example inside a judge prompt.

**Picture.** A doctor's notes after each patient visit. Not "category 3". Plain words: "chest pain after climbing stairs, no fever".

**Why it exists.** Free text lets you capture things you did not expect. A menu of categories hides them.

**Why the first thing, and not every thing.** One early mistake causes many later ones. Say the system fetched an out-of-date return page. The bot then states a wrong deadline, calls a wrong date format, and apologizes in a muddled way. That is four visible problems from one cause. If you note all four, the cause gets counted four times, and it looks like four different issues. Note the earliest, and fix that. Most of the others go away.

**Number.** Plan about 3 minutes per trace. This is an illustrative guess. Time yourself on the first ten. At 3 minutes, 100 traces take 300 minutes, which is 5 hours. That is a day of work for one person, and it replaces weeks of guessing.

### Idea 2: group the notes into failure types

**What it is.** Once you have a pile of notes, group the ones that mean the same thing and give each group a short name. The research term is **axial coding**, but "grouping" is the plain word. The result is a short list of **failure types**. Hamel's FAQ says to do this after you have written notes on about 30 to 50 traces yourself. After that, a model can help propose groups. A person must still check them, because a model may merge things that need different fixes. His example: it may put "login crashes" and "login is slow" in one group.

**Picture.** Sorting laundry. First you throw everything in one heap. Then you make piles: whites, darks, delicates. A good pile is one where every item gets the same treatment.

**Why it exists.** One note is an anecdote. A group with a count is a finding. Groups also decide the work: each group needs one kind of fix. If a pile needs two fixes, split it.

**Number.** The animation shows 12 of 40 failed traces. Watch the big pile split in two. "Old refund page" means the system fetched the wrong text. Teams call that a retrieval problem. "Invented 30 days" means the model made up a rule. Teams call that a generation problem. A prompt change cannot fix the first. A better search cannot fix the second.

### Idea 3: count each group, then choose an action

**What it is.** Label every trace in your sample with its group. Then count. You now have a table: failure type, number of traces, share of the total. Langfuse's last step is to decide, for each failure type, between three actions: fix the prompt or code, add an **evaluator** (an automatic check that catches it on future traces), or just monitor it for now.

**Picture.** A hospital triage board. It lists complaints by how common and how serious they are. The staff do not treat the loudest patient first.

**Why it exists.** Without counts, teams fix the failure that was in last week's angry email. With counts, they fix the one that happens most.

**Number.** In the animation, the counts are 14 for wrong documents, 10 for refusals, 9 for invented rules, and 7 for wrong orders. That is 40 failures. The loudest complaint was refusals. The largest group is wrong documents: 14 of 40, or 35% of failures. If those 40 failures came from 100 traces, wrong documents hit 14% of all traces.

Which action? Use the cheapest one that works. If a failure is a plain bug, such as an out-of-date page, fix it and move on. Hamel's FAQ says the same about prompts: if a prompt ignores a rule, fix the prompt before you write test cases for it. Build an evaluator when the failure could return, or when it is hard to see. Use a **code check** for things a program can test, such as "the reply contains the order id". Use a judge for fuzzy rules, such as "refused when policy allowed the refund". Lesson A8.4 teaches both.

### Idea 4: stop when new traces add nothing new

**What it is.** How many traces is enough? Not a fixed count. Hamel's FAQ says to read at least 100 traces and to continue "until new traces stop revealing failure modes or changing existing ones". That state is called **saturation**: more reading no longer changes the list.

**Picture.** Picking apples. At first, each tree gives a new kind of fruit to the basket. Later you pick only more of the same.

**Why it exists.** Reading is the costly step, and you want to stop when it stops paying.

**Number, part 1: how sure is the failure rate?** Say you read 100 traces and 40 failed. The rate you measured is 40%. The true rate is not exactly 40%. A standard 95% range for a share measured on 100 samples is about 31% to 50%. That is a wide band. Hamel's judge guide says that below 60 examples, "the confidence intervals are often too wide to support a useful conclusion". A **confidence interval** is that band of plausible true values.

**Number, part 2: will you even see a rare problem?** Say one failure type shows up in 1% of traces. In 100 random traces, the chance of seeing it at least once is 1 - 0.99^100. That is 63%. So 100 traces miss a 1% problem 37% of the time. For a 95% chance of one sighting, you need about 299 traces. To see it 3 times is much harder: only 8% at 100 traces.

Try both with the explorer. Move the sliders. Notice how slowly the range narrows as n grows, and how a rare problem stays hidden.

### Idea 5: do it again on a schedule

**What it is.** Your system, your users, and your models all change. A list of failure types from last quarter goes stale. Hamel's FAQ suggests a full review of at least 100 fresh traces every 2 to 4 weeks. Between those, read 10 to 20 traces a week, focusing on outliers. An **outlier** here is an unusual trace: a very long chat, a very slow reply, or a thumbs-down.

**Picture.** A regular health checkup, plus a quick temperature check between visits.

**Why it exists.** New features create new failures. A model update changes old behavior without warning. Lesson A9.6 covers what to do when quality drops in production.

**Number.** At 3 minutes per trace, a weekly check of 15 traces costs 45 minutes. A full cycle of 100 costs about 5 hours. Over a month, one full cycle plus four weekly checks is 5 + 4 x 0.75 = 8 hours. That is a small price against a failure that goes unnoticed for weeks.

## Numbers

A budget for the first pass. Quality bar: every failure type with a share of 5% or more of failures has a named fix, an evaluator, or a reason to wait. Latency budget: one day of reading, not a week. Cost per trace: about 3 minutes of one person (illustrative). For a team that pays $60 an hour fully loaded (also illustrative), 100 traces cost about 5 x $60 = $300. Compare that with one week of a wrong refund policy.

| Quantity | Value | Derivation |
|---|---|---|
| Traces to start | at least 100 | Hamel's FAQ |
| Hand-written notes before model help | 30 to 50 | Hamel's FAQ |
| Reading time at 3 min per trace | 5 hours | 100 x 3 min |
| Range on a 40% rate at n = 100 | about 31% to 50% | standard 95% range for a share |
| Chance to see a 1% problem in 100 traces | 63% | 1 - 0.99^100 |
| Traces for a 95% chance to see a 1% problem | about 299 | ln(0.05) / ln(0.99) |

## Trade-offs

| Approach | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Read every trace yourself | Finds failures nobody named, builds product sense | Hours of human time | The first pass, and any time the system changes a lot |
| Model groups your notes | Fast, handles hundreds of notes | May merge groups that need different fixes | After 30 to 50 hand notes, always with a human check |
| Model labels all traces | Scales to thousands | Blind to what you did not name; needs its own checks | Counting known failure types at volume, once labels are validated |
| Ready-made scores | Zero setup | False confidence, no link to your failures | Only to find traces worth reading |

## What experienced teams know

1. **Note the first failure, not every symptom.** The Langfuse [guide to error analysis](https://langfuse.com/blog/2025-08-29-error-analysis-to-evaluate-llm-applications) tells reviewers to write "a free-text note about the first thing that went wrong". The reason is arithmetic. If a bad retrieval causes 3 visible errors and you note all 3, one cause is counted 3 times and the ranking is wrong. Note the earliest error, fix it, and re-read: the rest usually vanish.

2. **Expect your rules to change as you read.** The "Who Validates the Validators?" study found that criteria depend on the outputs a person has seen. So the first 20 traces you graded were graded by an earlier, vaguer version of you. After your list of failure types settles, go back and re-label the first batch. Keep an "unsure" pile and revisit it. The [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272) is linked below.

3. **100 traces will miss a 1% problem more than a third of the time.** The chance of seeing it is 63%, as derived above. Mix random traces with targeted ones: long conversations, angry users, rare product lines, traces where a thumbs-down was clicked. Hamel recommends weekly attention to outliers for this reason.

4. **Do not hand over the first pass, and do check the model's groups.** Hamel's [note on automating evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/what-parts-of-evals-can-be-automated-with-llms.html) says to read raw traces yourself first, and to review every group a model proposes. A model is a good second reader. It is a poor first reader, because it only sees what its prompt names.

5. **Ready-made scores are a flashlight, not a verdict.** Hamel's FAQ calls generic metrics a source of "false confidence". Use them to rank which traces to open, such as the 50 with the lowest "helpfulness" score. Do not report them as quality.

6. **A targeted sample proves a failure exists, not how common it is.** If you hunt for angry users, your sample has more angry users than your traffic does. So the failure share in that sample is not the production rate. Report rates only from a random sample. Use targeted samples to find new types, and tag every trace with how it was picked.

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "I start by reading about 100 real traces. For each failed one, I write a note on the first thing that went wrong, with no fixed categories. I group the notes, split any group that needs two fixes, and count. The counts decide what to fix first and which failures get an automatic check. I stop when new traces stop adding new types. I repeat every few weeks and read a handful each week." This shows method, stopping rule, and cadence.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "I would add metrics for helpfulness, accuracy, and tone" before you have seen any data. Do not let a model label everything on day one. Do not rank failures by how loud the complaint was, and do not report a failure rate from 20 hand-picked traces.
</Callout>
