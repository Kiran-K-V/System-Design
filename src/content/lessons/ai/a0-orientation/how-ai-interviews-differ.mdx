## The problem

You know how a backend design interview works. You get a prompt, you state requirements, you draw boxes, and you defend them against load, failure, and growth. The interviewer asks: does it scale?

Now the prompt is "Design a support bot that answers questions from our policy documents." You draw a load balancer, a cache, a queue, and a vector store. Every box is sound. The interviewer asks one question: **how do you know the answers are right?**

If you have no answer, the design fails. It can handle a million requests per second and still give wrong answers at a million requests per second.

That is the change. A backend system returns the same result for the same input. An AI system returns a result that is right some fraction of the time. So the interview adds questions about that fraction, and about what each answer costs.

The backend track shows how a design review scores an answer, and how the same answer deepens from mid level to senior to staff. The AI interview keeps all of that. It adds new axes on top.
</CrossTrackLink>

## Two interview families

AI design prompts fall into two families.

**Classic ML.** You design a system around a model you train yourself: a feed ranker, a fraud detector, an arrival-time predictor. The hard parts are data, features, labels, and metrics. Hello Interview lists five areas that interviewers typically score for this family: problem navigation, input data and labels, model design, integration and evaluation, and communication. The page says each company has its own rubric, so treat the list as a guide.

**GenAI.** You design a system around a foundation model you do not train: a RAG assistant, a coding agent, a chat service. Chip Huyen puts the split this way: AI engineering "focuses on building applications on top of foundation models", while the older practice of ML systems focuses on applications built on traditional ML models. In the second family the model is a given. The hard parts move to evaluation, cost, latency, and failure handling.

## Four new axes

Add four axes to the backend rubric. They are the same four on almost every GenAI prompt.

1. **Quality bar.** A number that says what "right" means. Example: "at least 90% of answers match policy on a golden set of 200 real tickets."
2. **Eval plan.** How you measure that number. Who writes the test set, what grades it (code, a human, or another model), and how often it runs.
3. **Cost.** Dollars per request, then per day. Not "it should be cheap".
4. **Risk.** The ways it fails, how often, and what happens when it does. Include a human fallback.

Latency stays on the list from the backend track. Cost and latency now depend on the model you choose, so they join the quality bar in one trade-off.

Why these four? Because without them no design change can be judged. Hamel Husain, who has helped many teams ship AI products, says unsuccessful products usually share one root cause: a failure to create robust evaluation systems. Without an eval, each fix is a guess. Teams fix one failure and create another.

## What a staff answer adds

Compare two answers to the support bot prompt.

A **mid-level** answer: "Embed the documents, store them in a vector database, retrieve the top five, send them to the model." This is correct and incomplete. It names no bar, no test, no price.

A **staff** answer adds four things, in this order.

1. **Eval first.** "Before I design anything: we need 200 real tickets with correct answers, and a grader. Our bar is 90% correct, with p95 latency under 3 seconds."
2. **Budgets with numbers.** "At 1M requests per day and a cap of $0.01 per request, I can spend $10,000 per day. That rules out the largest model on every request." Lesson [A0.2](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/ai-design-method/) shows the arithmetic.
3. **Failure modes with rates.** "If the model is wrong 8% of the time and wrong answers about refunds have legal cost, I add a check that the answer cites a policy passage, and a handoff to a human."
4. **A rollout plan.** "Shadow mode first, then 5% of traffic, with the same metrics as the offline eval and a rollback."

Notice what did not change. The staff answer still has the retrieval pipeline. It just does not start there. Anthropic's guidance for building agents says to look for "the simplest solution possible", and to add complexity only when needed. A staff answer applies the same rule to the interview: simple first, measured, then more.

## Numbers

Real failures show why interviewers care about the new axes.

| Case | What happened | Missing axis |
|---|---|---|
| Air Canada chatbot, 2024 | The bot told a passenger he could claim a bereavement fare within 90 days after travel. The policy said otherwise. The tribunal found the airline "did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate" and ordered it to pay $812 (the fare difference). | Quality bar, eval, risk |
| Mata v. Avianca, 2023 | Lawyers filed a brief with cases that ChatGPT had invented. On 2023-06-22 the court fined them $5,000. | Risk, human check |

Sources: the Radio-Canada report on the tribunal decision, and the LawNext report on the sanction. Neither team had a way to measure how often the model was wrong. Both paid for it.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Gain | Pay | When to choose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start the answer with the architecture | Looks like a familiar backend answer | No way to judge any choice. Interviewers score it as a gap. | Never first |
| Start with the quality bar and eval | Every later choice has a test | Slower start, and some interviewers must be led back to it | Always on GenAI prompts |
| Quote many numbers | Shows rigor | A wrong number can sink a point | When you can derive it in one line |
| Cover every axis equally | Looks complete | Runs out of time | Cover all in one sentence each, then go deep where the prompt is hardest |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Open with: "Before the architecture, let me set the quality bar and how we measure it." Then give a number, even a rough one, and say you would confirm it with the product owner. A stated number that you can change beats a vague goal.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "we will use an LLM as the judge" and stop. Say what the judge is checked against: human labels on a sample. Do not say "temperature 0 makes it deterministic" either. Lesson [A0.3](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/probabilistic-components/) shows that it does not. And do not skip cost because "the API is cheap". At a million requests per day a one-cent request is $10,000 per day.
</Callout>

<Callout type="deep" title="Why eval comes before fine-tuning in the literature">
Chip Huyen's AI Engineering table of contents places evaluation methodology (chapter 3) before finetuning (chapter 7). OpenAI's model optimization guide gives the same order: write evals first, then prompt, then consider fine-tuning only after a gap shows. The order is not style. A fine-tune costs data labor and compute. An eval tells you whether you need one.
</Callout>
