## The problem

The [How AI Design Interviews Differ](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/how-ai-interviews-differ/) lesson said the interviewer wants a quality bar, an eval plan, a cost, and a risk plan. That is four things to remember, and 45 minutes to fit them in. Without an order, candidates do one of two things. They start with architecture and run out of time before the new axes. Or they list every axis in one breath and go deep on none.

You need a method: a fixed order that puts the new axes where they belong.

<CrossTrackLink id="00-orientation/design-method">
The backend track teaches six steps for any design: requirements, core entities, API, data flow, high-level design, deep dives. It also teaches two loops back to requirements. The AI method keeps that spine. It changes what goes into steps 1 to 3, and it adds a rollout step at the end.
</CrossTrackLink>

## Naive solution: architecture first

A natural start is to draw the pipeline you have seen before: documents, embeddings, a vector store, a model. It feels productive. It fails for one reason. At minute 20 someone asks, "Is the answer better with the bigger model?" You cannot say. There is no test. Each design change is an opinion.

Hamel Husain calls the result "whack-a-mole". You fix one failure and cause another, because nothing tells you what the fix cost elsewhere. OpenAI's model optimization guide gives the cure in its first step: write evals first, to set a baseline.

## The mechanism: six steps

Step through the method for a support bot. All numbers in the animation are illustrative.

<Ai0MethodFlow client:visible />

**1. Requirements with a quality bar.** List what the system does, then say what "good" means as a number you can fail. Example: "answers are correct on at least 90% of a golden set." A golden set is a fixed list of inputs with known good outputs.

**2. Eval plan.** Choose the test set, the grader, and the people who read transcripts. A grader is the thing that scores an answer: code, a human, or another model used as a judge. Anthropic's guide on evals for agents uses these terms: task, trial, grader, transcript, outcome, harness. Grade the outcome, not the exact path the model took. Hamel's levels: unit tests on every change, human and model review with simple good or bad labels, and A/B tests once the product is mature.

**3. Latency and cost budget.** Write both as numbers before you pick a model. The next section shows what drives cost.

**4. Data and model choice.** Now choose. Start with a hosted model and a prompt. Add retrieval when the model lacks facts. The [Build, Buy, or Fine-tune](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/build-buy-finetune/) lesson covers the full decision.

**5. Architecture.** Draw the boxes. Each one exists because an earlier step asked for it. The eval runs on the design. If it misses the bar or the budget, go back to step 4.

**6. Rollout, monitoring, failure modes.** Ship in stages. Watch the same metrics as the offline eval. For each failure, say how often it happens and what the fallback is.

Why is eval step 2, not step 5? Because steps 3 to 6 are all choices, and a choice needs a test. Eval is the one thing that stays fixed while the design changes.

A common answer order for an interview is as follows. First, confirm the depth the interviewer wants, then state requirements and metrics. Next, describe the data and the serving path. After that, cover evaluation, feedback, and risks. Third-party write-ups of ML design interviews often use six steps too. The steps are: problem framing, data pipeline, features, model, training and evaluation, deployment and monitoring. Use them as a cross-check. If your answer has no place for the eval before the model, move it up.

## Numbers: the budget

A budget turns a business limit into a limit on design. Pick a spend cap per request first. Then ask what the request costs. Cost per request grows with the tokens you send in and the tokens the model writes back. Output tokens cost more each than input tokens. A long prompt can still dominate the bill, because it is paid for on every call.

Prices differ a lot between models. The table shows price per 1M tokens, standard tier, as of 2026-10-15. Provider pricing pages show no effective date except Google's, which said "last updated 2026-10-14 UTC". Prices change, so re-check before you quote one in a real design.

The top models cost many times more than the small ones. So a tight per-request cap can rule out the largest model on every request. A budget limits the model choice before any quality test runs. It does not choose the model for you: a model that fits the budget must still clear the quality bar. Lesson [A2](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/gpu-memory-math/) and the cost lessons later in this track do the full arithmetic.

Latency needs the same discipline. For one open-source setup, Llama 3.1 8B runs on one H100 with the vLLM default setup. An E2E Networks benchmark reports a time to first token of 28.84 ms for a short chat request. Time to first token is the wait before streaming starts. For long outputs the total is far larger. So a p95 target of 3 seconds is often a limit on output length, not on first-token delay. Measure your own stack.

A cheaper model for easy requests and a stronger one for hard requests is a standard lever. Anthropic's agent guide calls it routing. It cuts the average cost without lowering the bar on hard cases.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Gain | Pay | When to choose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eval before architecture | Every change has a test | Slow first 10 minutes. Needs labeled data. | Always |
| Strict cost cap | Forces simple designs and routing | May cap quality | Consumer scale or thin margins |
| Loose cost cap | Room for the best model | Cost shock at scale | Low volume, high value per request |
| LLM as judge | Cheap, fast grading | Judge errors. Needs a check against human labels. | After you have labeled a sample |
| Human review | Highest trust | Slow and costly. Labels 25 to 50 traces per batch in Hamel's example. | Seed set and spot checks |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say the six steps in one sentence at minute 3: "I will set the quality bar, plan the eval, set budgets, choose data and model, draw the architecture, then cover rollout and failures." The interviewer now knows where you are going and can steer you.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not treat the budget as a closing remark. If you pick the model first and compute the cost last, you may find a late problem. The design may be three times over budget with 5 minutes left. Do not use OpenAI's hosted Evals platform as your named harness either. Its guide says it becomes read-only on 2026-10-31 and shuts down on 2026-11-30. Name the method (golden set, grader, trials), not one vendor.
</Callout>
