## The problem

Your support bot scores 71% on its test set. The bar for launch is 90%. Three people in the room each want a different fix.

- One says: rewrite the prompt.
- One says: connect the bot to the policy documents.
- One says: fine-tune the model.

All three are right sometimes. Picking the wrong one costs real time. A prompt edit takes minutes to test. A fine-tune needs a training set, a training run, a new test, and a plan for when the base model changes. If the failure was a missing fact, the fine-tune does not even fix it.

This lesson answers four questions:

1. What can you change about a model, and what does each change cost?
2. How do you know which change a failure needs?
3. When does fine-tuning pay for itself?
4. Who will let you fine-tune today, and for how long?

The [Build, Buy, or Fine-tune](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/build-buy-finetune/) lesson gave the decision tree. This lesson goes deeper on the fine-tuning branch.

## Question 1: what can you change about a model?

Start with what a model is. A model is a function. Text goes in, and text comes out. Inside the function sits a huge list of numbers. Those numbers are called **weights**. A model with 70 billion weights is called a "70B" model. Everything the model knows and every habit it has lives in those numbers.

Think of a mixing desk with 70 billion knobs. The sound that comes out depends on where each knob sits. **Training** is the process of turning those knobs, a tiny amount at a time, until the output gets better.

When your code calls a model, it cannot touch the knobs. It can only choose the input. The input text is called the **prompt**. This gives you three levers.

1. **Change the prompt.** Write clearer instructions. Add an example. Reorder the text.
2. **Add documents to the prompt.** Look up the right text at request time, and paste it into the prompt. The name for this is **retrieval-augmented generation**, or **RAG**. The model reads the text you found, and answers from it.
3. **Change the weights.** Keep training the model on your own examples. This is **fine-tuning**.

An everyday picture: you hired a new support agent. The prompt is the instruction sheet on their desk. RAG is you opening the policy binder to the right page before every call. Fine-tuning is sending the agent on a two-week training course. The sheet is quick to edit. The binder is always current. The course changes how the agent talks and works, and it takes the longest to redo.

Why do all three exist? Because they change different things at different prices. The numbers below make this concrete:

| Lever | What it changes | Time to try an idea |
|---|---|---|
| Prompt | The instructions the model reads | Minutes |
| RAG | The facts the model reads | Hours to build once, then each document change is instant |
| Fine-tune | The model's own habits | A training set first. One research team trained a strong chat model from 1,000 carefully chosen examples, so the set can be small, but choosing them is the work |

The last row comes from the LIMA study. Its authors trained a 65-billion-weight model on only 1,000 curated prompt and answer pairs. People judged its answers equal to or better than GPT-4's in 43% of cases. The authors read this as: almost all knowledge is learned while the model first trains on huge amounts of text (**pretraining**), and a small set of examples is enough to teach the model how to answer. Their idea is the key to this whole lesson. Fine-tuning teaches **how to answer**. It is a poor way to teach **what is true**.

## Question 2: how do you know which lever a failure needs?

A doctor diagnoses before prescribing. You do the same with an **error analysis** (the [Error Analysis](/learn/ai/a8-evals/error-analysis/) lesson). Run your **test set**: a fixed list of questions with known good answers. Read the answers that failed. Give each failure a name. Count the names.

Most failures fall into a few groups, and each group points to a lever.

| What you see in the failed answer | Likely cause | Lever |
|---|---|---|
| States an old or invented fact | The model never saw the right fact | RAG |
| Asks for a source, and none exists | Weights cannot show where a fact came from | RAG |
| Ignores a rule that is in the prompt | The instruction is unclear or buried | Prompt |
| Right content, wrong tone or format, after several prompt rewrites | The habit is stuck in the weights | Fine-tune |
| Quality is fine but every call carries 3,000 tokens of rules and examples | You pay for the same long text on every request | Fine-tune (to shrink the prompt) |

A **token** is a piece of text, about three quarters of a word (the [Tokens and Tokenizers](/learn/ai/a1-model-function/tokens/) lesson). Providers bill per token. Long prompts cost money and add delay, because the model must read them before it writes the first word.

Look at the order of the levers: prompt, RAG, then fine-tune. It follows cost. Each lever you try is a test you can re-run on the same test set. You move to the next lever only when the failure that is left cannot be fixed by the cheaper one.

## The naive way: fine-tune to teach the model your facts

This is the most common wrong choice. The team has thousands of policy documents. The model does not know them. The plan: train the model on the documents.

It fails in two ways.

**First, facts change.** The weights are frozen the day training ends. A policy that changes on Tuesday stays old in the model until the next run. A RAG index updates the moment a document does.

**Second, training on facts can break the model's other skills.** Thinking Machines, a research lab, ran this test in 2025 on an 8-billion-weight open model (an **open-weight** model is one whose weights anyone can download). They trained it further on a pile of internal company documents. Then they measured two things. One was a question-answering test on those documents. The other was IF-eval, a public test that checks whether the model follows instructions about format and style.

| Model | Question answering on company facts | Following instructions (IF-eval) |
|---|---|---|
| Before training | 18% | 85% |
| Trained on 100% documents | 43% | 45% |
| Trained on 70% documents, 30% chat | 36% | 79% |

The facts went up. The model's ability to follow instructions fell by almost half. Mixing in chat data helped, but the authors report there was no mix that kept the original instruction-following score. This effect is called **forgetting**: new training pushes out older skills. The picture: a student who crams one binder the night before the exam and writes a worse essay the next morning.

The lesson: put facts in retrieval. Use fine-tuning for habits. And whenever you tune, re-run a general test, not only the new one.

## Question 3: when does fine-tuning pay for itself?

There is one case where fine-tuning is mostly about money. Your prompt carries long rules or many examples, and you send it on every request. After tuning, the habits live in the weights, so the prompt can shrink.

Work it out in words. A shorter prompt means fewer input tokens on every request. At a million requests, those saved tokens add up to a real bill.

That looks large. Now add **prompt caching** (the [Prompt Caching: Mechanics and Economics](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/prompt-caching/) lesson). A long prompt that repeats on every request does not have to be read from scratch each time. The provider keeps the repeated start, and bills later reads at a much lower price. If most of your prompt tokens hit the cache, the saving from a shorter prompt shrinks a lot, and it did not need a training run. Payback then takes much longer, because you spend a fixed sum on data, training, and tests. Caching first, tuning second.

A shorter prompt also cuts delay, because the model reads fewer tokens before it starts to write (the [Prefill vs Decode: TTFT and TPOT](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/prefill-decode/) lesson). This part of the gain does not show up in dollars.

Two more costs the calculator hides. A tuned model may cost more to serve than the base model: use the multiplier slider to test that. And the tuned model is tied to one base model. When the base model retires, you must tune again.

## Question 4: who lets you fine-tune today?

This question matters more than it did two years ago. A fine-tune lives on the platform that trained it. If the platform changes its rules, your work moves with it or dies. Here is the picture as of 2026-10-16, from each provider's own pages.

| Provider | What the pages say |
|---|---|
| OpenAI | Its fine-tuning guide says the company is "winding down the fine-tuning platform". The platform is "no longer accessible to new users". The guide lists supervised fine-tuning and preference tuning on three GPT-4.1 snapshots, and reinforcement fine-tuning only on o4-mini. The deprecations page gives dates: from 2026-07-02, organizations that had not run a tuned model in the past 60 days could not create jobs. On 2027-01-06, "active existing customers will no longer be able to create new fine-tuning jobs". Tuned models keep serving until their base model is deprecated. Several tuned snapshots, including tuned o4-mini, shut down on 2026-10-23. Lessons [Preference Tuning and DPO](/learn/ai/a11-finetuning/preference-tuning/) and A11.4 teach preference tuning and reinforcement fine-tuning. |
| Anthropic | I found no fine-tuning guide in the Claude API docs: the old fine-tuning page returns "not found". Claude 3 Haiku could be fine-tuned on Amazon Bedrock (generally available 2024-11-01). Bedrock's table today still lists it as the only Claude model you can fine-tune, in one region. Anthropic's own API retired Claude 3 Haiku on 2026-04-20, and says Bedrock sets its own dates. |
| Amazon Bedrock | Supervised fine-tuning for Amazon Nova and Meta Llama models. Reinforcement fine-tuning, where you supply a scoring function, for Nova 2 Lite and two open models, gpt-oss-20B and Qwen3 32B (added 2026-02). Distillation, which trains a small model on a big model's answers. |
| Open weights, managed | Thinking Machines offers Tinker, a service where you write the training loop and they run the GPUs, on open-weight models from 4 billion to 1 trillion weights. It bills "actual tokens used, not inactive time". |
| Open weights, your own GPUs | The [SFT and LoRA](/learn/ai/a11-finetuning/sft-lora/) lesson shows the memory math. You own the result, and you can move it anywhere. |

Google's Vertex AI docs also list supervised and preference tuning for Gemini. I did not confirm which Gemini versions are tunable, so I leave them out of the table.

Our reading, not a provider claim: tuning a closed frontier model is a shrinking, provider-owned feature. Tuning an open-weight model is portable. The closed-model path ties your data, your tests, and your result to one vendor's schedule. The next section turns this into practice.

## Numbers

Every design needs a quality bar, a latency budget, and a cost per request. For a fine-tune, fill them in like this.

- **Quality bar.** Your test set score on the failure you want to fix (for example 71% to 90%), and a general test that must not fall (for example, instruction following). Both are measured before and after.
- **Latency budget.** A shorter prompt cuts the time before the first word. A smaller tuned model cuts it more. Measure both on your own traffic.
- **Cost per request.** Prompt tokens times the effective price, as in the calculator. A tuned model with a much shorter prompt costs much less per request, until caching narrows the gap.
- **One-time cost.** Building the training set is the largest part. LIMA's 1,000 examples show the set can be small, but each one must be good.

## Trade-offs

| Lever | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prompt | Minutes to try, easy to roll back | Long prompts cost tokens on every call | Always first |
| RAG | Fresh facts, citations, no training | An index to run, and retrieval adds delay | The failure is a missing or stale fact |
| Fine-tune | Habits, style, format, shorter prompts | A training set, a run, a new test, and a re-tune for each new base model | Prompts cannot fix the behavior, or the prompt bill is large |

## What experienced teams know

1. **Tuning on facts can break instruction following.** In the Thinking Machines test above, a model trained on company documents rose from 18% to 43% on company questions and fell from 85% to 45% on instruction following. No document-to-chat mix kept the old score. Put facts in retrieval. If you must tune on documents, run a general test before and after, and gate the release on both. Source: [Thinking Machines, on-policy distillation post, 2025-10-27](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/).

2. **A fine-tune is a lease on someone else's platform.** OpenAI will stop letting existing customers create jobs on 2027-01-06. Claude 3 Haiku, the one Claude model offered for tuning, retired on Anthropic's own API in April 2026. Keep your training data as plain files in your own storage, and keep your test set in a format no vendor owns. Then moving to open weights is a re-run, not a rewrite. Source: [OpenAI deprecations](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations) and [Claude model deprecations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations).

3. **Your test tool may be on a deadline too.** OpenAI's hosted Evals product becomes read-only on 2026-10-31 and shuts down on 2026-11-30. A team that planned its fine-tune tests inside it must move them first. Keep tests in your own repository, run by your own code. Source: [OpenAI deprecations](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations).

4. **Prompt caching often removes the money case.** A long, repeated prompt is cheap when the provider caches it: Sonnet 5.5 bills cache hits at one twentieth of the input price. The calculator shows a 19-day payback turning into 128 days at a 90% hit rate. Run the numbers with your real cache hit rate before you approve a tuning project. Source: [Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing).

5. **Data quality sets the ceiling, not data count.** LIMA reached a strong chat model from 1,000 curated pairs. Its authors argue the model already knows the content, and the examples only show the style. So spend your effort on 200 to 1,000 carefully reviewed examples before you collect 50,000 noisy ones. Source: [LIMA paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206).

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "I name the failure first. A missing or stale fact goes to retrieval, an ignored rule goes to the prompt, and a stuck style or a heavy prompt bill goes to fine-tuning. I run the levers in cost order and re-run the test after each. Before I tune, I price it against prompt caching, and I keep my training data and tests portable, because hosted tuning is being wound down at some vendors." That covers diagnosis, cost, and risk.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not answer "we will fine-tune the model on our documents so it knows our policies". It stores facts badly, goes stale, and can damage other skills. Do not forget the exit plan either: say what happens when the base model retires.
</Callout>
