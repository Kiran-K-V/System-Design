## The problem

A 70B model in BF16 needs about 140 GB for its weights (the [GPU Memory Math](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/gpu-memory-math/) lesson). An H100 has 80 GB. So you rent two H100s, and the weights fill almost all of that memory. Little is left for the KV cache and everything else.

The second cost is speed. Decode reads every weight once per generated token, and it is limited by memory bandwidth, not by arithmetic (the [Prefill vs Decode: TTFT and TPOT](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/prefill-decode/) lesson). A step cannot finish faster than the time to read the weights. Fewer bytes per weight means less to read and less to store.

## Naive solution: buy more GPUs

You can add cards. Eight H100s hold the 70B model with room to spare. Each card costs money every hour whether it is busy or not (the [Self-host vs API](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/self-host-vs-api/) lesson). Splitting a model across cards adds communication between them. It is the right answer when you need the quality, but it is not the only lever. The model's numbers do not have to be stored in 16 bits.

## The mechanism: store fewer bits per weight

**Quantization** means storing each weight with fewer bits and rounding it to the nearest value the format can hold. The format sets how many distinct values a weight can take. Those counts are 65,536 for 16 bits, 256 for 8 bits, and 16 for 4 bits. A scale factor stretches that small set of values over the range of the weights.

Memory follows directly from bytes per parameter:

<Math block tex="\text{weight bytes} = \text{parameters} \times \text{bytes per parameter}" />

| Precision | Bytes per parameter | Distinct values | 70B model | Fits on 80 GB cards |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BF16 or FP16 | 2 | about 65,000 | 140 GB | 2 cards, 20 GB free |
| INT8 or FP8 | 1 | 256 | 70 GB | 1 card, 10 GB free |
| INT4 | 0.5 | 16 | 35 GB | 1 card, 45 GB free |

The sizes are parameters times bytes. The "free" figures are card memory minus the weights. They count weights only. Scale factors, activations, and runtime buffers take more. Treat INT4 as a lower bound on size.

Free memory is where the KV cache lives. Each token of context adds to the KV cache (the [KV Cache](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/kv-cache/) lesson). More free memory means more tokens of cache. The INT8 card runs, but it has little room for long contexts or many users. This lesson counts the KV cache at BF16. Quantizing it is a separate choice.

### Pick a model and a precision

1. **70B on one H100.** INT8 and INT4 fit. BF16 does not.
2. **405B.** In BF16 it needs more memory than one 8-GPU node holds. In INT4 it shrinks to a few cards.
3. **Mixtral.** Memory follows the 47B total parameters, not the 13B used per token (the [GPU Memory Math](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/gpu-memory-math/) lesson).

## Which methods exist

Rounding each weight to the nearest value is the simplest method. It loses more than you would like at 4 bits, so there are better ones. The sources below are paper abstracts, so we quote only what they state.

- **GPTQ** (Frantar et al.) quantizes to 3 or 4 bits after training. It quantized a 175B model in about 4 GPU hours. The paper reports inference speedups of about 3.25x on an A100 and 4.5x on an A6000 against FP16. It says accuracy loss is negligible but gives no number in the abstract.
- **AWQ** (Lin et al.) protects the roughly 1% of weights that matter most. The paper says doing so "can greatly reduce quantization error". It reports more than 3x speedup over Hugging Face FP16 on desktop and mobile GPUs.
- **FP8** keeps floating-point form with 8 bits. The formats are E4M3 (4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits) and E5M2. The FP8 paper reports results that match 16-bit quality on the tasks it tested, for training. NVIDIA's H100 page lists FP8 peak throughput at twice the BF16 figure (3,958 against 1,979 TFLOPS, both with sparsity). That matters most for prefill, which is limited by arithmetic.

### What you gain and what you do not know

In a memory-bound decode step, time follows bytes read. Going from 2 bytes to 0.5 bytes cuts the read to a quarter. So the byte ratio is an upper bound on the decode speedup. Real servers lose some of it to dequantization work and to KV reads. Measured speedups above come from the papers' own setups.

What you pay is quality. Rounding changes the model's output. **We give no quality-loss number here, because none we loaded is specific to your model and task.** Run your eval (the [AI Design Method](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/ai-design-method/) lesson) at each precision. Look at the cases that matter, not only the average score.

## Small models: a cheaper lever

The other way to cut bytes is to start with fewer parameters. A model trained longer on more data can reach a quality level at a small size (the [Scaling Laws and Model Choice](/learn/ai/a1-model-function/scaling-laws/) lesson). Its weights are smaller, so every one of the effects above comes along. These effects are less memory, fewer bytes per step, and fewer GPUs. You can also combine the two: a quantized small model.

**Distillation** trains a small model to copy a larger one. The DistilBERT paper reports a model 40% smaller and 60% faster that keeps 97% of the original's language understanding. That is a 2019 result on a small language model. So treat it as a pattern, not as a promise for today's models. The [Distillation and Training Data](/learn/ai/a11-finetuning/distillation-data/) lesson covers distillation.

### Real data: Shopify

Shopify's engineering post on product understanding reports 40 million LLM calls and about 16 billion tokens per day. They fine-tuned open vision models, applied FP8 quantization, used key-value caching, and served with Triton and a Kafka-based pipeline. Selective field extraction cut median latency from 2 seconds to 500 ms and GPU usage by 40%. The latency and GPU figures are the company's claims about one system. The post does not split the gains between FP8 and the other changes. So we cannot say how much FP8 alone gave.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Gain | Pay | When to choose |
|---|---|---|---|
| BF16, more GPUs | Best quality for the model | Highest memory and GPU bill | Quality is the limit and you can afford the cards |
| FP8 or INT8 | Half the memory, faster on supporting hardware | A small quality change to measure | Default first step for self-hosting |
| INT4 (GPTQ, AWQ) | A quarter of the memory, one card for 70B | A larger quality change to measure, and extra checks on long inputs | Memory is the limit, and your eval still passes |
| Smaller model | Cheaper everywhere | A lower quality cap | Narrow tasks at high volume |
| Distilled model | Large-model behavior at small size | A training pipeline to build and own | A stable task with many examples |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say it in words: "At BF16 a 70B model needs two H100s with almost no spare memory. At INT8 it fits on one card with a little spare. At INT4 it leaves plenty of room for KV. Decode reads fewer bytes, so it speeds up by up to the byte ratio. I would pick the precision by running my eval, because quality loss depends on the model and task."
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not quote a quality loss from memory, such as "INT4 loses 1%". You have no source for your model. Do not forget the KV cache when you say "it fits". A model that fits with 10 GB free may not serve long prompts. Do not say a 4x smaller number format gives a 4x faster product. It is an upper bound on decode only.
</Callout>
