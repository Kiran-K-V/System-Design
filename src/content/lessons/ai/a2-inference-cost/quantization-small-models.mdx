## The problem

An AI language model is a huge pile of numbers. Engineers call those numbers **weights**. The model learned them during training, and it reads them every time it answers. A "70B" model has 70 billion weights.

A GPU (graphics processing unit, a chip built to do the heavy math of AI) keeps the weights in its own memory. An H100 is a popular AI chip from NVIDIA, and it has 80 GB of memory. Most models store each weight in BF16 (a way to store a number in 16 bits, which is 2 bytes). A bit is one on/off switch, and a byte is 8 bits. So 70 billion weights times 2 bytes is about 140 GB (the [GPU Memory Math](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/gpu-memory-math/) lesson).

That does not fit in 80 GB. So you rent two H100s, and the weights fill almost all of that memory. Little is left for the KV cache (the notes the model keeps about the text so far, so it does not redo work) and everything else.

The second cost is speed. Decode is the step where the model writes its answer one token at a time. A token is a small piece of text, about a word or part of one. For each token, the GPU reads every weight once.

Picture a fast cook who must wait for a delivery truck before each dish. The cook is not slow. The truck is. In the same way, decode waits for memory to deliver bytes, not for arithmetic. Engineers say decode is limited by memory bandwidth, which means how many bytes per second memory can deliver (the [Prefill vs Decode: TTFT and TPOT](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/prefill-decode/) lesson).

A step cannot finish faster than the time to read the weights. Fewer bytes per weight means less to read and less to store.

## First idea: buy more GPUs

You can add cards. Eight H100s hold the 70B model with room to spare. Each card costs money every hour whether it is busy or not (the [Self-host vs API](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/self-host-vs-api/) lesson). Splitting a model across cards also adds talk between them, like a team that must pass notes before each answer.

Buying cards is the right answer when you need the best quality. But it is not the only lever. The model's numbers do not have to be stored in 16 bits.

## The mechanism: store fewer bits per weight

Think of a ruler. A ruler with a mark every millimeter lets you write a length very exactly. A ruler with a mark every centimeter is smaller and simpler, but you must round each length to the nearest mark.

Models work the same way. **Quantization** means storing each weight with fewer bits and rounding it to the nearest value the format can hold. The format sets how many different values a weight can take. A 16-bit format has 65,536 values. An 8-bit format has 256. A 4-bit format has 16. A scale factor (one multiplier) stretches that small set of values over the range of the weights.

The names in the table below are formats. FP means floating point (a way to store numbers that can be tiny or huge). INT means integer (a way to store whole numbers). So FP16 is floating point in 16 bits, INT8 is whole numbers in 8 bits, and INT4 is whole numbers in 4 bits. FP8 is floating point in 8 bits.

Memory follows directly from bytes per parameter. A parameter is another word for one weight.

<Math block tex="\text{weight bytes} = \text{parameters} \times \text{bytes per parameter}" />

| Precision | Bytes per parameter | Distinct values | 70B model | Fits on 80 GB cards |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BF16 or FP16 | 2 | about 65,000 | 140 GB | 2 cards, 20 GB free |
| INT8 or FP8 | 1 | 256 | 70 GB | 1 card, 10 GB free |
| INT4 | 0.5 | 16 | 35 GB | 1 card, 45 GB free |

The sizes are parameters times bytes. The "free" figures are card memory minus the weights. They count weights only. Scale factors, the temporary numbers made while the model runs, and working space take more. So the real INT4 size is a bit larger than the table says.

Free memory is where the KV cache lives. Each token of context adds to the KV cache (the [KV Cache](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/kv-cache/) lesson). Context is the text the model is reading, such as your question and the earlier chat. More free memory means room for more tokens of cache. The INT8 card runs, but it has little room for long contexts or many users. This lesson counts the KV cache at BF16. Quantizing it is a separate choice.

## Smarter ways to round

Rounding each weight to the nearest value is the simplest method. At 4 bits it loses more than you would like. So researchers built smarter methods. Each one below comes from a research paper, and we quote only what the paper states.

- **GPTQ** rounds weights to 3 or 4 bits after the model is already trained, with no retraining. It rounded a 175B model in about 4 hours of GPU time. The authors say the accuracy loss is negligible, but the abstract gives no number.
- **AWQ** protects the roughly 1% of weights that matter most, so they lose less in the rounding. The authors report that this greatly reduces the rounding error. They also report faster inference than the 16-bit version.
- **FP8** keeps floating-point form in 8 bits. A paper found that 8-bit training matched 16-bit quality on the tasks it tested. NVIDIA also lists the H100's peak FP8 arithmetic speed at twice its BF16 speed. That matters most for prefill, the step where the model reads your whole prompt. Prefill is limited by arithmetic, not by memory.

### What you gain and what you do not know

When memory delivery is the limit, step time follows the bytes read. Going from 2 bytes to 0.5 bytes cuts the read to a quarter. So the byte ratio is the best case for the decode speedup.

Real servers get less than the best case. Before the GPU does arithmetic, it must turn the small stored values back into bigger numbers. That work is called dequantization, and it costs time. The server also still reads the KV cache. Any speedup a paper reports comes from its own test setup, not yours.

What you pay is quality. Rounding changes the model's output. **We give no quality-loss number here, because none we loaded is specific to your model and task.** Run your eval at each precision (the [AI Design Method](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/ai-design-method/) lesson). An eval is a test: you ask the model many questions and score the answers. Look at the cases that matter, not only the average score.

## Small models: another way to save

The other way to cut bytes is to start with fewer parameters. A model trained longer on more data can reach a good quality level at a small size (the [Scaling Laws and Model Choice](/learn/ai/a1-model-function/scaling-laws/) lesson). Its weights are fewer, so every effect above comes along. These effects are less memory, fewer bytes per step, and fewer GPUs. You can also combine the two: a quantized small model.

**Distillation** trains a small model to copy a larger one. Picture a student who learns by studying a teacher's answers. DistilBERT, a small language model from 2019, was 40% smaller and 60% faster than the model it copied, and it kept most of that model's language understanding. That is an old result on a small model. So treat it as a pattern, not as a promise for today's models. The [Distillation and Training Data](/learn/ai/a11-finetuning/distillation-data/) lesson covers distillation.

### Real data: Shopify

Shopify runs online stores. Its engineering post describes AI that reads product listings. The post reports 40 million LLM calls and about 16 billion tokens per day. (An LLM is a large language model, the kind of AI that reads and writes text.)

The team made several changes. They fine-tuned open vision models, which means they trained models that can read images further on their own examples. They applied FP8 quantization. They reused earlier work with key-value caching (the KV cache idea from above). They served the models with Triton (a program that runs models for other programs) and a pipeline built on Kafka (a tool that passes messages between programs). They also asked the model to extract only the fields they needed.

Together, these changes cut the typical (median) wait from 2 seconds to 500 ms. GPU usage fell by 40%. Those figures are the company's claims about one system. The post does not split the gains between FP8 and the other changes. So we cannot say how much FP8 alone gave.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | Gain | Pay | When to choose |
|---|---|---|---|
| BF16, more GPUs | Best quality for the model | Highest memory and GPU bill | Quality is the limit and you can afford the cards |
| FP8 or INT8 | Half the memory, faster on supporting hardware | A small quality change to measure | Default first step for self-hosting |
| INT4 (GPTQ, AWQ) | A quarter of the memory, one card for 70B | A larger quality change to measure, and extra checks on long inputs | Memory is the limit, and your eval still passes |
| Smaller model | Cheaper everywhere | A lower quality cap | Narrow tasks at high volume |
| Distilled model | Large-model behavior at small size | A training pipeline to build and own | A stable task with many examples |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say it in words: "At BF16 a 70B model needs two H100s with almost no spare memory. At INT8 it fits on one card with a little spare. At INT4 it leaves plenty of room for the KV cache. Decode reads fewer bytes, so it speeds up by at most the byte ratio. I would pick the precision by running my eval, because quality loss depends on the model and task."
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not quote a quality loss from memory, such as "INT4 loses 1%". You have no source for your model. Do not forget the KV cache when you say "it fits". A model that fits with 10 GB free may not serve long prompts. Do not say a 4x smaller number format gives a 4x faster product. The byte ratio is the best case for decode only.
</Callout>

## Further reading

- [Frantar et al., GPTQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17323): post-training quantization to 3 or 4 bits.
- [Lin et al., AWQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978): protecting salient weights.
- [Micikevicius et al., FP8 Formats for Deep Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05433): the two ways to lay out an FP8 number. You do not need them for this lesson.
- [Sanh et al., DistilBERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01108): distillation to a smaller model. It reports 40% smaller, 60% faster, and 97% of the original's language understanding.
- [Shopify Engineering, multimodal LLMs for the global catalogue](https://shopify.engineering/leveraging-multimodal-llms): FP8, latency, and GPU figures.
