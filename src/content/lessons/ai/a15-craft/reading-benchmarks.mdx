## The problem

A made-up team runs an invoice service. One model call reads each invoice and returns the supplier, the date, and the total as JSON. The team has a golden set of 150 real invoices ([the Golden Sets, Synthetic Data, and Contamination lesson](/learn/ai/a8-evals/golden-sets/)). The current model passes 94% of them.

On Tuesday a new model, "Model Z", launches. The post says: 92% on a famous coding benchmark, first place on a public chat leaderboard, and a chart that puts Model Z above every rival. A manager forwards the link with one line: "Can we switch?"

The team switches on Wednesday. On Thursday the invoice pass rate reads 88%, and the bill is higher. Nothing in the launch post was a lie. None of it was about invoices either.

This lesson teaches you to read a launch post or a leaderboard with doubt, and to settle the question on your own task in about an hour. It answers six questions:

1. What does a benchmark score actually measure?
2. Is the test still a test?
3. Is the answer key right?
4. What did the run look like, and who compared whom?
5. What happens when the score becomes the goal?
6. Who can you trust, and what do you do yourself?

[The Golden Sets, Synthetic Data, and Contamination lesson](/learn/ai/a8-evals/golden-sets/) taught you to build a private golden set and to suspect public benchmarks of leaking. This lesson takes that suspicion further, and then turns the golden set into a tool for judging new models.

## Question 1: what does a benchmark score measure?

A **benchmark** is a fixed set of tasks, a grader that marks each answer, and a rule that turns the marks into one number. Every score you see is a result of three parts: the test, the grader, and the run (which model settings, how many tries, which tools).

An everyday picture: a driving test. Passing it tells you the person can drive that route, in that car, in that weather, with that examiner. It does not tell you they can drive a lorry through snow.

Why do benchmarks exist? Because comparing models by hand is slow. A shared test lets many teams compare many models cheaply. That is a real gain. The risk is to read the number as "how good the model is", when it means "how well the model did on this test, under these settings".

The embeddings lesson shows the same trap. The MTEB paper (a large embedding benchmark) says in its own abstract that "no particular text embedding method dominates across all tasks" ([the Choosing Embedding Models lesson](/learn/ai/a5-rag/embedding-models/)). A model that wins on average can lose on your task. The same holds for chat and coding models. The [Scaling Laws and Model Choice](/learn/ai/a1-model-function/scaling-laws/) lesson gave the other half: a bigger model is not always the right pick, because you pay for it on every call.

So keep three questions next to every score: what was the test, how was it graded, and how was it run?

## Question 2: is the test still a test?

You met **contamination** in [the Golden Sets, Synthetic Data, and Contamination lesson](/learn/ai/a8-evals/golden-sets/): test questions that appeared in a model's training data. A model that has seen the answer is recalling, not solving. The [Golden Sets, Synthetic Data, and Contamination](/learn/ai/a8-evals/golden-sets/) lesson showed a fresh matched set (GSM1k) and an n-gram scan. Here is one more tool, and it works on closed models.

**Split by date.** LiveCodeBench is a coding benchmark built from contest problems, and every problem has a release date. The authors compared scores before and after each model's training cutoff. Their paper reports "a stark drop in the performance of DeepSeek, GPT-4-O, and Codestral on LeetCode problems released after" a model-specific date. For GPT-4-O the drop starts on problems released since November 2023, "its specified cutoff date". The authors read this as a sign that these models "are likely trained on the older LeetCode problems". A drop at the cutoff is a strong hint, not proof. It is cheap to look for. Ask any vendor for results on problems newer than the model's cutoff.

Now the second way a test stops working. **Saturation** [mid] means that most strong models score near the top of the scale, so the test no longer tells them apart. Think of an exam where every student gets 96% to 98%. You learn nothing about who is better.

A third problem sits next to saturation. A public test is public for good. Epoch AI's review of SWE-bench Verified (a coding benchmark of 500 bug fixes from open-source projects) says that, as of 2026-09-03, 100% of its tasks and 100% of its solutions are public. The review also quotes OpenAI's own analysis: all frontier models tested "have seen at least some of the problems and solutions during training". And it quotes OpenAI's conclusion that gains on this benchmark "increasingly reflect how much the model was exposed to the benchmark at training time". That is OpenAI's reading, passed on by Epoch. This lesson cites Epoch's page, not OpenAI's.

## Question 3: is the answer key right?

A test is only as good as its **labels**: the answer key the grader uses. If the key says 42 and the right answer is 24, a model that answers 24 is marked wrong. The [Golden Sets, Synthetic Data, and Contamination](/learn/ai/a8-evals/golden-sets/) lesson measured this for your own set with Cohen's kappa. Public benchmarks have the same flaw, and the flaw is large.

- **MMLU**, a famous 57-subject knowledge test. The "Are We Done with MMLU?" paper re-checked 5,700 questions by hand and estimated that "6.49% of MMLU questions contain errors". In the Virology subject, 57% of the checked questions had errors. The errors change rankings: on Virology, Llama 3.1 405B ranked 16th on all questions and first when only correct questions were used.
- **Humanity's Last Exam**, a benchmark of very hard knowledge questions. FutureHouse checked the text-only chemistry and biology questions (321 of them) and found that 29 ± 3.7% (95% interval) had answers that directly conflict with evidence in peer-reviewed papers. Epoch audited a random sample of 48 questions and found 22 (46%) with substantial errors that change the right answer. The sample is small, so treat the exact figure with care.
- **SWE-bench Verified.** OpenAI audited 27.6% of the tasks, and Epoch reports that 59.4% of those had flawed tests that rejected correct fixes. Epoch's own reading: "over 20% of questions have scoring-based issues".

Epoch rates both SWE-bench Verified (as of 2026-09-03) and Humanity's Last Exam (2026-08-11) as "Flawed" on its own review rubric. That is one evaluator's judgment, and you can read the rubric.

What follows for you? Our reading: if about one question in sixteen has a wrong key, no model can honestly score 100%, and gaps of a point or two near the top of the table are close to noise. Saturation and label noise work together. When models crowd the top, the wrong keys decide who is first.

## The naive way: pick the model at the top

The first plan is to open a leaderboard, take the top model, and switch. It fails for the three reasons above: the test may be leaked or saturated, the key may be wrong, and the test is not your task. It also fails for reasons the next questions cover: the voting may favor style, the run may have been generous, and the number may have been tuned for.

## Question 4: how was the number made?

Two things shape a claimed score after the test is fixed: who voted, and how the run was set up.

### Idea 1: arena-style voting has its own biases

**What it is.** An **arena** is a crowdsourced board. A visitor sends one prompt, sees two answers, and picks the one they prefer. Many such votes are fed into a statistical model (the LMSYS post calls it a Bradley-Terry regression) that gives each model one strength score. The board ranks models by that score.

**Picture.** A taste test at a market stall. People pick the dish they like. That tells you what people enjoy, not which dish is the most nutritious or the most correct.

**Why it is useful.** It measures what a plain test cannot: which answer people like. For a chat product, that matters.

**Why it misleads.** Four biases are known.

1. **Style.** LMSYS added "style features" to its regression: answer length, number of markdown headers, bold elements, and lists. Its finding: "length was the dominant style factor". Longer, well-formatted answers win votes. The blog adds that this controls for style, and that the aim is to tell apart a model that is better on substance from one that is better on style. Your invoice job wants exact field values, not charm.
2. **Voters and prompts.** The prompts and voters are whoever shows up. They are not your users.
3. **Selection.** The "Leaderboard Illusion" paper (April 2025) estimated that Google and OpenAI each got about a fifth of all arena data (19.2% and 20.4%), while 83 open-weight models together got 29.7%. It also estimated that extra arena data can give relative gains of up to 112% on the arena's own distribution, which it calls overfitting to the arena rather than a gain in general quality. The [Golden Sets, Synthetic Data, and Contamination](/learn/ai/a8-evals/golden-sets/) lesson covered the count of private variants Meta tested. The numbers here are the paper's estimates.
4. **Tuned variants.** In April 2025, TechCrunch reported that Meta used "an experimental, unreleased version" of Llama 4 Maverick for its arena score. A Meta spokesperson called it "a chat optimized version we experimented with that also performs well on LM Arena". LMArena replied that Meta's "interpretation of our policy did not match what we expect from model providers", and that Meta should have said it was "a customized model to optimize for human preference". The standard Maverick, scored afterward, was "ranked below models including OpenAI's GPT-4o, Anthropic's Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Google's Gemini 1.5 Pro" as of 11 April 2025.

A first place on an arena is a fact about voters' taste for one model version. Use it to build a shortlist for a chat product. Do not use it to pick a model for extraction or code.

### Idea 2: read the run settings, not only the number

**What it is.** The same model can post very different numbers depending on how the run was set up. Five settings do most of the damage.

**Tries.** A task can be scored on the first try or on many tries. Four rules are common:

- **pass@1** [mid]: the model gets one try. Artificial Analysis says it "generally" uses this: "a model must produce the correct answer on its first attempt".
- **pass@k** [mid]: the task counts as solved if any of k tries is right. Anthropic's evals post defines it as "the likelihood that an agent gets at least one correct solution in k attempts".
- **pass^k** [mid]: the task counts only if all k tries are right. The same post: "the probability that all k trials succeed". It uses a 75% per-try rate: over three trials, the chance of passing all three is about 42%.
- **Majority vote**, also called self-consistency [mid] or cons@k: the model answers k times and the most common answer is the final answer. TechCrunch described xAI's cons@64 as giving a model "64 tries to answer each problem" and taking "the answers generated most frequently". Critics noted that xAI's Grok 3 chart left out a rival's cons@64 score. TechCrunch's point: omitting it "might make it appear as though one model surpasses another".

Two lessons from the comparison. A chart is only fair if every bar uses the same rule. And only one rule fits your product. A job that makes one call per task lives on the "one try" rule, and a customer-facing agent that must be right every time lives on the "all k right" rule.

**Settings and tools.** A **thinking budget** is the number of tokens a model may spend reasoning before it answers. Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.5 launch post gives a good example of disclosure, and it is a vendor claim: "All evals were run with a 64K thinking budget, interleaved scratchpads, 200K context window, default effort (high)", "averaged over 5 independent trials", with exceptions listed, such as "SWE-bench Verified (no thinking budget)". The post also says one result used "parallel test-time compute, a method that aggregates multiple 'tries' from the model and selects from among them". Without the footnotes you could not tell any of this. Epoch's rubric lists the same traps: token and turn budgets, and tool access.

**The harness.** A **harness** (also called a scaffold) is the program around the model. It gives the model tools, files, and turns, runs the loop, and collects the result. Epoch's SWE-bench Verified review says results are "highly dependent on the scaffold used". A custom harness built by the vendor can lift a score. A rival may be run on a different harness. The Opus 4.5 post is open about this: its footnote says Anthropic improved the hosting environment, which raised Gemini 3 to 56.7% and GPT-5.1 to 48.6% on one benchmark "from the values reported by their developers", using the Terminus-2 harness. That is a vendor rerunning rivals on its own setup. It is disclosed, which is good. You still cannot check it.

**The machine.** Even the hardware matters. Anthropic's engineering team found that on Terminal-Bench 2.0 "the gap between the most- and least-resourced setups" was 6 percentage points (p < 0.01). Above about 3 times the specified resources, extra headroom starts to help agents solve problems they could not solve before. The post says the exact multiplier "should thus be reported". Leaderboard gaps are often smaller than 6 points.

### Idea 3: when a number becomes the goal

**What it is.** **Goodhart's law** [mid] says that a measure stops being a good measure once people optimize it. Manheim and Garrabrant describe the failure this way: a metric that can be used to improve a system "is used to an extent that further optimization is ineffective or harmful".

**Picture.** A school that is judged on exam results starts teaching the exam. Results rise. Learning may not.

**Why it matters here.** A model maker is rewarded for a high number, so it has a reason to tune for the number. Some of that tuning is open and fair. Some of it is not. You will see both: Meta's chat-optimized arena variant above, and the SWE-bench Verified exposure OpenAI described. Epoch's review also notes that another group (RDI) "identified a reward hack that achieves a perfect score". A **reward hack** [mid] is a way to score high without doing the task.

**What follows.** A benchmark is most useful when it is new and least useful when it is famous. The more teams optimize a number, the less it tells you. That is not an accusation of cheating. It is how any target works.

## Question 5: whom can you trust?

A vendor reports its own number, picks the settings, and decides what to show. That does not make the number false. It makes it a **vendor claim**. An **independent evaluator** is a group that is not the model maker, that runs models itself or collects results, and that publishes its method. As of 2026-10-28:

| Evaluator | What it does | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/about) | Gathers results from benchmarks it runs and from "reputable external sources". Also reviews benchmarks with a public rubric. | Shows the flaws of the tests themselves | Still a public test set that can leak |
| [Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/intelligence-benchmarking) | Runs models "under identical conditions with consistent prompting strategies, temperature settings, and evaluation criteria", mostly pass@1 | Same rules for every model | A score belongs to one index version (v5.0 as of 2026-10-28) |
| [METR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499) | Measures a "time horizon": how long a task, in human time, a model can finish with 50% success | Measures what a model can do on long tasks | A different question from yours |
| [Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/about) | "Privately held test sets"; says this "prevents training to the test set" | Hard to leak | You cannot read the questions to check the keys |
| [Scale Labs](https://labs.scale.com/leaderboard) | Leaderboards that combine private and open datasets; a model can only be featured "the FIRST TIME" an organization sees the prompts | Hard to tune for | Same: you cannot audit private items |

Independent does not mean unbiased, and none of these tests your task. Use them as a second source. If a vendor claims first place and no independent board agrees, treat the claim as unconfirmed. Always read the method page. A rank is only comparable inside one method version.


## Question 6: how do you check a new model yourself?

A checklist tells you what to doubt. It does not tell you the answer. The answer comes from your own golden set ([the Golden Sets, Synthetic Data, and Contamination lesson](/learn/ai/a8-evals/golden-sets/)), run on the new model and the old one, side by side.

**What it is.** A **new model check** is a one-hour routine that runs your golden set on a candidate and on your current model, compares them case by case, and ends with one decision.

**Picture.** A test drive. You do not buy a car from the brochure. You drive it on your own roads.

**Why it works.** The set is private, so it cannot have leaked. The labels are yours, so you trust the key. It is your task, so the match problem disappears. And it runs in minutes, so you can run it for every launch.

**Example.** Use this plan. The times are a suggested budget, not a measured one.

```text
NEW MODEL CHECK  (budget: 60 minutes)
Candidate (pinned ID): ____   Baseline: ____   Golden set: golden-v__ (N=__)
Claim that made us look (quote and URL): ____

00-10  SETUP
[ ] Same prompt, tools, and golden-set version for both models. No re-tuning yet.
[ ] Settings the new model accepts. Test the first call. Record in the manifest.
[ ] Repeats: 1 run each if short on time, 3 if outputs vary.
10-30  RUN
[ ] Run both models on the full set. Save one result per case: id, pass, output.
[ ] Record cost per 100 cases and slowest-case latency for both.
30-45  READ
[ ] List flips: cases that went pass->fail and fail->pass. Read the pass->fail ones first.
[ ] Cut by scenario tag. Is the gain or loss in one scenario?
[ ] Read at least 10 transcripts. Is the grader fair? Is the new model wrong or only different?
45-55  PROBE THE CLAIM
[ ] Write 20 fresh cases on the one skill the launch post boasted about.
[ ] Run both models on them. Keep these cases out of the golden set.
55-60  DECIDE
[ ] ignore | shortlist | start migration (the [Model Migration Playbook](/learn/ai/a15-craft/model-migration/) lesson)
[ ] Write one sentence: why, and what would change the decision.
```

Equal totals can hide opposite changes. A candidate and a baseline can both pass 98 of 150 cases while the new model fails 12 that the old one passed and passes 12 that the old one failed. The flips are the real result. This script lists them from two result files, one JSON line per case.

```python
import json, sys

def load(path):
    return {r["id"]: r["pass"] for r in map(json.loads, open(path))}

a, b = load(sys.argv[1]), load(sys.argv[2])  # baseline, candidate
ids = sorted(a.keys() & b.keys())
gain = [i for i in ids if not a[i] and b[i]]
loss = [i for i in ids if a[i] and not b[i]]
print(f"{len(ids)} cases  baseline {sum(a[i] for i in ids)}  candidate {sum(b[i] for i in ids)}")
print(f"fail->pass {len(gain)}   pass->fail {len(loss)}")
print("read these first:", loss[:10])
```

Finish with a result block you can paste into a ticket.

```text
RESULT   golden-v__  N=__  repeats=__
                 baseline   candidate   flips (fail->pass / pass->fail)
overall          __ / __    __ / __     __ / __
scenario ____    __ / __    __ / __     __ / __
claim probe (20 fresh cases)   baseline __/20   candidate __/20
cost per 100 cases  __ vs __        slowest case  __ s vs __ s
Decision: ignore | shortlist | start migration
Why, in one sentence:
```

Two cautions. A gap of a few points on a few hundred cases can be noise, so read the flips and repeat the run before you decide ([the Golden Sets, Synthetic Data, and Contamination lesson](/learn/ai/a8-evals/golden-sets/) shows how wide the doubt is). And a new model is a new release: tokenizer, accepted settings, and prompt fit can all change, so see [the Model and Prompt Versioning lesson](/learn/ai/a9-llmops/versioning/) before you ship.

## Trade-offs

| Source of evidence | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vendor launch post | Fast, first to appear, often detailed footnotes | Vendor-chosen settings, rivals rerun by the vendor, no one has checked it yet | To learn what to test, never to decide |
| Public leaderboard | Easy to compare many models | Leaks, saturation, label noise, style bias | To build a shortlist |
| Arena-style voting | Measures human taste in chat | Style bias, selection effects, not your prompts | Chat products, with style control on |
| Independent evaluator | Same rules for every model, public method | Not your task; private sets cannot be audited | As a second source for any claim |
| Your own golden set | Your task, your labels, private | Costs labeling time; small sets are noisy | Always, before any switch |
| Live A/B test | Real users, real outcomes | Slow and costly; see [the Online Evals and A/B for LLM Features lesson](/learn/ai/a8-evals/online-evals-ab/) | After your set says "go" |

## What experienced teams know

1. **Infrastructure can move a score more than the gap between models.** Anthropic's [infrastructure noise post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/infrastructure-noise) found a 6-point gap on Terminal-Bench 2.0 between the most- and least-resourced setups, while "top spots on leaderboards" are "often separated by just a few percentage points". So a benchmark result needs its resource settings stated, and your own check should run both models on the same machine and limits.

2. **The footnote is the claim.** Read the methodology lines of [a launch post](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-4-5) first. The thinking budget, the number of trials, the harness, and who reran the rivals are all there. A post that hides them is weaker evidence than one that lists them, and even a well-footnoted post is still a vendor claim.

3. **Benchmarks age.** SWE-bench Verified once helped teams compare coding models. Epoch's [review](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/swe-bench-verified/review) (2026-09-03) rates it "Flawed", and quotes OpenAI that gains there "increasingly reflect how much the model was exposed to the benchmark at training time". Check the date of the evidence. Prefer new or private tests for a decision.

4. **The hardest test is not the cleanest.** A hard expert benchmark is easy to get wrong. FutureHouse's [audit](https://futurehouse.org/research/hle-exam) found 29 ± 3.7% of the checked chemistry and biology questions in Humanity's Last Exam conflicted with published evidence, and Epoch's [review](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/hle/review) found 22 of 48 sampled questions with answer-changing errors. Difficulty is not accuracy.

5. **Date splits catch leaks that no one announces.** LiveCodeBench's [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07974v2) found that scores fall on problems released after a model's cutoff. Build the same split into your own checks: keep a stash of cases written after the model's training cutoff.

6. **Gut feel is unreliable too, which is why you measure.** In METR's [2025 study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), 16 experienced developers on 246 issues took 19% longer with AI tools. Before the study they expected a 24% speed-up, and afterward they still believed they were 20% faster. METR now marks those results as out of date for current models, so use the lesson, not the number: people judge speed by feel and are often wrong.

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say it as a routine: "I treat a launch post as a lead, not evidence. I check the test (leaked, saturated, label noise), the run (pass@1 or best-of-n, tools, thinking budget, whose harness), and the comparison (who reran the rivals, who voted). I look for an independent evaluator that confirms it. Then I run my own golden set on the new and old models, read the flips, probe the one claimed skill with fresh cases, and decide in an hour." That shows doubt, a method, and a time box.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "we picked the model at the top of the leaderboard". Do not treat a vendor chart as a measured fact. Do not compare a majority-vote score with a one-try score. Do not call a model "better" from a gap of a point or two on a few hundred cases. And do not say a benchmark is "fair" only because it is famous: fame is how a test gets leaked and tuned for.
</Callout>
