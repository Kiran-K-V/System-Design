## The problem

A company has 25 teams that call model APIs. They share one provider account, so they share one rate limit. At 03:10 one team's batch job starts to send prompts 13 times longer than usual. The request count stays flat. The token count does not. Within minutes the checkout assistant and the support bot get 429 errors, and the invoice for the hour is eight times normal (an illustrative night, rebuilt in the incident drill below).

Lesson [A3.8](/learn/ai/a3-llm-apis/multi-provider-gateway/) built a gateway for one team. This case asks what it must do as a **shared platform**: many **tenants** (here, teams), one pool of capacity, one invoice. Three words hold the case. **Isolation:** one team's mistake must not become another team's outage. **Exactness:** the platform knows what each team spent, and the total matches the invoice. **Evidence:** a route change, a fallback, and a cheaper model each need an eval.

## Requirements and the quality bar

**Functional.** A team calls one internal API with its own key. Each team has a quota on tokens per minute (TPM) and requests per minute (RPM), and a monthly budget. The gateway fails over, caches repeats, and writes a usage record per call. An eval-owned route table says which model serves which alias. **Below the line:** prompt tools, fine-tuning, content filters, semantic caching.

**Non-functional.** These bars are ours, not a vendor's.

| Bar | Target | How we measure it |
|---|---|---|
| Isolation | A team inside its guarantee sees at most 0.1% gateway-made 429s while another team sends 6 times its load | Weekly surge test in staging, 429s per team |
| Failover | A request that hits a failing primary reaches the backup within 5 s | Failure drills: 529, timeout, spend-cap 429, error inside a stream |
| Cost exactness | Ledger total within 0.5% of the invoice | Monthly reconcile |
| Overhead | At most 10 ms added at p95 | Trace spans on the gateway path |

**The bar that justifies a shared platform is isolation.** A gateway can be fast, cheap, and exact and still fail it.

**Scale (illustrative).** Peak traffic is tens of requests per second, each with a long prompt and a short answer. Provider limits are from the Anthropic [rate limits page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits), Sonnet 5.5, Scale tier, as of 2026-10-19: they cap requests per minute (RPM), input tokens per minute (ITPM), and output tokens per minute (OTPM).

At this traffic, input tokens run closest to their limit. A request-count quota sees a quiet system while the input bucket empties. That is how the 03:10 job got through.

## Eval plan

Lesson [A0.2](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/ai-design-method/) puts the eval before the design. A gateway has three.

1. **Route quality.** Each route has a golden set (lesson [A8.3](/learn/ai/a8-evals/golden-sets/)). A cheaper model enters a route only when its pass rate clears the bar there, graded by code checks and by a judge tested against human labels (lesson [A8.4](/learn/ai/a8-evals/judges/)). The slice that matters most is the **accepted slice** of a cascade: the requests the cheap model kept. Nobody re-checks those.
2. **Failover drills.** Weekly, inject each failure into staging. Record time to backup and the backup's pass rate (lesson [A9.5](/learn/ai/a9-llmops/provider-failover/)). We add the clock: 5 s.
3. **Budget accuracy.** Ledger against invoice, as a share of the invoice.

Online signals: 429 rate per team, share of calls that fell back, escalation rate per cascade, cache hit rate, and cost per successful task (lesson [A9.2](/learn/ai/a9-llmops/cost-engineering/)).

## Budgets

**Latency.** LiteLLM, an open-source gateway, publishes its own benchmark on a [benchmarks page](https://docs.litellm.ai/docs/benchmarks): overhead of a few milliseconds at the median and under 30 ms at p95, depending on instance count. The page gives no date or version (company claim). It shows that a few milliseconds are possible, not that your build will reach them. In our plan, the quota take and the cache lookup each cost one round trip to a shared store, and the rest is small. The ledger write stays off the request path. That keeps the total under the 10 ms bar.

Failover costs more. If the first-token timeout is a few seconds, one extra try on the primary breaks the 5 s bar. So the bar sets the retry count: one attempt on the primary, then the backup.

**Dollars.** Prices are USD per million tokens, as of 2026-10-19, from [Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing) and [OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing) (standard text, under 272K tokens).

| Model | Input | Output | Relative cost of a request |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anthropic Opus 5.5 | $4 | $20 | Highest |
| Anthropic Sonnet 5.5 | $2 | $10 | Mid |
| Anthropic Haiku 5.5 (prompts up to 100,000 tokens) | $0.10 | $0.50 | About 20 times cheaper than Sonnet |
| OpenAI gpt-6.1-sol | $2.00 | $10.00 | Mid, like Sonnet |
| OpenAI gpt-6-luna | $0.10 | $0.50 | Cheapest, like Haiku |

From the same pages: a Sonnet 5.5 cache read is far cheaper than normal input, Haiku 5.5 costs five times more per token above 100,000 prompt tokens, and the Batch API takes 50% off.

## Core entities and API

**Team**, **Key** (one team, a feature tag), **Alias** (`chat-main`, points at a route), **Route** (ordered targets, shares, the eval score and date that admitted each, a rollback target), **Quota**, **Budget**, and **Ledger row** (team, feature, model, token classes, dollars).

```
POST /v1/messages     the key names the team
  200  answer + x-gw-model (who answered), x-gw-fell-back
  429  team quota: retry-after set, body names the bucket
  402  budget cut-off: no retry-after, a human must act
GET  /admin/usage?group_by=team,feature
```

A quota 429 may be retried after the wait. A 402 must not be, the same rule as the spend-cap 429 of lesson [A3.6](/learn/ai/a3-llm-apis/limits-retries-idempotency/).

## A naive design and where it breaks

One proxy, one shared provider key, no quota, three retries at every layer, a cache keyed by a hash of the body, cost estimated from prompt length. Each part fails with a number.

1. **No quota.** In the simulator below, team B surges to several times its load for 30 s against a shared limit. With one shared bucket every team loses the same share of its requests, including teams A and C, both inside their own share.
2. **Retries everywhere.** Three layers of three tries make 27 provider calls per user action (lesson [A3.6](/learn/ai/a3-llm-apis/limits-retries-idempotency/)).
3. **A body-hash cache.** If the key drops part of the context, one tenant can read an answer built for another.
4. **Estimated cost.** The estimate breaks when the tokenizer changes or streaming hides usage.

## High-level design

Five terms first. A **token bucket** is a counter that refills at a fixed rate up to a cap. A call takes tokens, and an empty bucket refuses it (lesson [7.4](/learn/backend/07-reliability/rate-limiting/)). The **quota store** keeps the buckets in one shared fast store so every gateway instance sees one count. The **ledger** is a table with one row per model call. The **route table** says which model serves each alias and with what share. To **reconcile** is to compare two records of the same money and explain the gap.

## Deep dive 1: quotas that no team can drain

<CrossTrackLink id="07-reliability/rate-limiting">
A token bucket refills at rate r and holds at most b tokens. The rate sets the long-run average. The depth sets the largest burst. Counters must live in one shared place when many servers enforce one limit. The backend lesson teaches this. Here we cover what changes when the thing counted is a model call.
</CrossTrackLink>

### The cost is unknown until the call ends

An ordinary request costs one unit. A model request costs input tokens plus output tokens, and the output count is unknown until the model stops. The gateway must decide at the door.

Providers face this too, and answer it differently. Anthropic says ITPM "rate limits are estimated at the beginning of each request, and the estimate is adjusted during the request to reflect the actual number of input tokens used". For output, "the `max_tokens` parameter does not factor into OTPM rate limit calculations". OpenAI says "Your rate limit is calculated as the maximum of `max_tokens` and the estimated number of tokens", with the estimate "based on the character count of your request" (both as of 2026-10-19). One field, two rules.

The gateway protects teams from each other, so it should be strict in the safe direction. It **reserves, then refunds**. At admission it takes input tokens plus a reserve for output. When the call ends, it puts back the unused part.

**Worked example.** A request has a few thousand input tokens and a `max_tokens` of 1,000. The model writes only 250. The gateway takes the input plus the full 1,000 at the door. When the call ends it refunds the 750 the model did not use. The final charge is the input plus the real output. The refund makes the charge exact. The reserve matters only in between.

**What the reserve costs.** Tokens stay held while the call runs. Say a deploy raises `max_tokens` from 1,000 to 8,000 while the model still writes about the same. Now every request holds far more tokens for the full duration of the call. The bucket empties before the first refunds return. For a short time, team A gets 429s while it is inside its guarantee. The simulator reproduces this.

Two rules follow. Reserve the p95 of the route's output length, not the model maximum. And let the bucket go a little negative when an answer runs over, then refuse new calls until it recovers. Azure's token-limit policy has the same weakness: "concurrent or near-concurrent requests can temporarily exceed the configured token limit". Plan for overshoot.

A call must also pass a request bucket, which stops a flood of tiny calls. Only tokens are refunded.

### Fair share and burst credit

**Guarantee.** Each team gets a share of the provider limit. Keep the sum below the limit, or the guarantee is a lie. We allocate most of the provider limit and keep some as headroom. In the simulator, teams A, B, and C have guarantees in a 5 to 3 to 2 ratio.

**Depth.** A bucket holds guarantee x depth: burst credit that a quiet team banks. A deeper bucket lets a team send a larger burst at once. Keep the sum of depths well under the provider's own bucket.

**Borrowing.** A strict per-team bucket wastes capacity: when A's bucket is full, its refill is thrown away. Fair sharing lends that overflow, and only the overflow, to teams that still want more, in proportion to their guarantees. The lender loses nothing. This is weighted max-min fairness: nobody gets more than it asks for, and spare capacity goes to those still asking.

<Ai13GatewayQuotaSim client:visible />

Try the three policies at the default settings (depth 10 s, `max_tokens` 1,000, surge 6x). Results over the 30 s surge:

| Policy | Team A | Team B (surging) | Team C |
|---|---|---|---|
| One shared bucket | Loses requests, though inside its share | Loses the same share as the others | Loses requests, though inside its share |
| Bucket per team | Untouched | Turned away most of the time | Untouched |
| Per team + burst credit | Untouched | Turned away a bit less | Untouched |

The shared bucket spreads the pain by demand, so the loudest team wins. The per-team bucket confines the damage to B. Burst credit gives B some extra requests from capacity A and C did not use. B is still turned away often, since a surge of that size is more than the platform can serve. Quotas do not create capacity. They decide who waits.

**Priority.** When the provider itself is short, drain batch and eval traffic first.

**Where the count lives.** With four gateway instances, a local full-limit counter gives a team four times its quota. Azure's page says its policy "tracks token usage independently at each gateway". Count in a shared store. If it fails, fall back to share divided by N per instance and alert. Overshoot beats an outage.

## Deep dive 2: routing by eval, and cascades

Lesson [A3.8](/learn/ai/a3-llm-apis/multi-provider-gateway/) gave a formula for a split, where each request goes to one model. A **cascade** differs. Every request goes to the cheap model first. A **check** decides whether to keep the answer. If not, the request **escalates** to the strong model, and the user waits for both. Think of a help desk: a new agent takes every call and passes up the hard ones. The company pays for the junior on every call and for the senior on the passed ones.

The check can be a code check (valid structure, valid tool arguments), a small judge model, or both. Its dangerous error is the **false accept**: it keeps a wrong answer and the user sees it.

### The cost, in words

A cascade costs the cheap call and the check on every request. The escalated share also pays for the strong call. So the cost is the cheap call, plus the check, plus the escalation share times the strong call.

| Setup | Relative cost |
|---|---|
| All Sonnet 5.5 | Highest |
| All Haiku 5.5 | Lowest, about 20 times cheaper than Sonnet |
| Cascade, a third escalate, free check | Far below all-Sonnet |
| Same, with a Haiku judge as the check | A little more than the free check |
| Router that predicts, no double pay | A little below the cascade |

A judge check adds a small cost to every request. A cascade costs a little more than a perfect router, because escalated requests also paid for the cheap call. It needs no predictor, only a check.

### The break-even escalation rate

There is an escalation rate above which the cascade costs more than sending everything to the strong model. With a 20-times price gap between the cheap and strong model, that rate is very high, so **cost is rarely the limit. Quality is.** The margin shrinks when the cheap price jumps. A very long prompt puts Haiku 5.5 in its long-context price tier, the gap narrows, and the break-even rate falls.

### Quality decides

Say, illustratively, all-Sonnet passes 95% of the eval. In the cascade, Haiku keeps most requests and nearly all of those pass. Sonnet handles the harder rest at a slightly lower pass rate. Overall the cascade matches the pass rate and costs much less per passing answer. But if Haiku's accepted answers passed less often, the total would fall below the bar and the cascade would fail. The eval gates the cascade, not the price table.

Two practical points (our reading). A cascade cannot stream the cheap answer while the check runs, or an escalated request would show text and then replace it, so buffer it. That adds the check time to every request. And the escalated path sets p95 latency: cheap call, check, strong call.

### Eval-gated routes

The route table is data. A row says: alias `chat-main`, target Haiku 5.5, share 70%, admitted by golden set `chat-v14` at 96.8% on 2026-10-12 (illustrative), rollback target Sonnet 5.5. A target enters a route only when it clears the bar. Its share then rises in steps with the gates of lesson [A8.8](/learn/ai/a8-evals/rollout/). A drop in the online pass rate moves the share back.

Published savings are upper bounds. The FrugalGPT paper says its cascade can "match the performance of the best individual LLM (e.g. GPT-4) with up to 98% cost reduction". The RouteLLM paper reports cost cuts "by over 2 times" on some benchmarks. Lesson [A9.2](/learn/ai/a9-llmops/cost-engineering/) shows 2.49 times on one test and 1.14 times on another. "Up to" is a ceiling. Measure on your own traffic.

## Deep dive 3: outages, retry budgets, and degraded modes

Lesson [A9.5](/learn/ai/a9-llmops/provider-failover/) taught which errors justify a switch. This dive adds what a shared gateway needs: a retry budget, a fallback order, and proof that a provider can fail with no error.

### The retry budget

When a provider fails, every client retries. With three attempts each, one failing call becomes three at a provider that is already down. Google's SRE book sets "a per-request retry budget of up to three attempts" and a per-client rule: "A request will only be retried as long as this ratio is below 10%". The first rule alone lets traffic grow "to somewhere just below 3X". Adding the ratio "reduces the growth to just 1.1x in the general case". The book warns that if several layers retried, "we'd have a combinatorial explosion".

**Worked example.** Provider A returns 529 on every call. With no budget, load on A triples. With a 10% budget, load rises only a little.

Build it as a token bucket that requests fill. Each first attempt adds 0.1 token. Each retry spends 1. An empty bucket means no retry: the call goes to the fallback or fails fast. Retry in one layer, the gateway. Give apps a fast error with `retry-after`. Never retry a spend-cap 429 or a 402. A fallback is a first attempt at B, but B has limits too, and a sharp rise can trigger an acceleration-limit 429 (the Anthropic page says to ramp up gradually). So B gets its own budget and ramp.

### Fallback order and degraded modes

Walk down a list. The order is a product decision.

1. **Same quality, second provider**, one that passed the route's golden set. Its cache is cold: a cache read costs a small fraction of normal input, so the cached part costs far more at first.
2. **Lower quality:** a smaller model, a lower `max_tokens`, a lower reasoning budget (lesson [A4.7](/learn/ai/a4-context/reasoning-budgets/)). Mark it in `x-gw-model`.
3. **Stale cache**, labeled.
4. **Queue** work that can wait, through the batch path.
5. **Fail fast** with `retry-after`, not a 60 s hang.

Shed by priority: batch and eval traffic stop first. Guarantees scale down together as the pool shrinks.

### A provider can fail with no error

Anthropic's [postmortem of three bugs](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues) shows a failure no status-code monitor sees. Between August and early September 2025, a routing error sent some Sonnet 4 requests to servers set up for a longer context window. It began on 5 August at about 0.8% of Sonnet 4 requests. In the worst hour, 31 August, "16% of Sonnet 4 requests were affected". Routing was "sticky", so a user who landed on a wrong server tended to stay. About 30% of Claude Code users who made requests in the period had at least one message misrouted. Two other bugs corrupted output from 25 August. Every response carried a normal status. The postmortem says: "The evaluations we ran simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting."

Our reading: a gateway that fails over on errors would have seen nothing. Run an **eval probe per route and provider**: a small fixed prompt set sent every few minutes through the real path, graded by code checks where possible. When a provider's score falls under its bar, the route table moves that share to the next target and pages a human. The route table is data, so the move takes seconds.

## Design point: the cache key carries the tenant

<CrossTrackLink id="05-caching/cache-placement">
A cache can sit in the browser, at a CDN, in a gateway, or in a shared store. A shared cache serves many users, so the key must include everything that makes the answer differ between them. If it does not, one user receives another's data. The backend lesson names the places. Here we apply the rule to model answers.
</CrossTrackLink>

Cloudflare's AI Gateway says its caching "is based on exact match of the entire request", with a time to live (TTL) of 60 seconds to one month. That is safe if the entire request is in the key. It stops being safe when someone trims the key for a better hit rate, for example by hashing only the last user message. Then two tenants with different system prompts and private context can share one entry. The Apache APISIX AI cache plugin states the risk: "a cached response produced for one consumer could be served to another". Its fix is `cache_key.include_consumer: true`.

```
key = hash(tenant_id, resolved_model_id, system_prompt, tools, params, messages, context_hash)
```

Put `tenant_id` in. Use the resolved model id, not the alias, so a route change cannot serve an old model's answers. The `context_hash` covers retrieved documents and tool results.

**Poisoning.** A cache also stores bad answers. Say a retrieved page holds hidden instructions (lesson [A10.2](/learn/ai/a10-safety/prompt-injection/)) and the model follows them. If that answer is cached under a key other tenants reach, every reader gets it. Keep the tenant in the key, and do not cache answers built from untrusted context unless its hash is in the key. Metric: cross-tenant cache hits must read 0, except in a namespace a team opts into for public data.

**Cost of isolation.** If many tenants send one public prompt, a shared cache has one miss and many hits, while tenant keys have a miss for each tenant. That is a small price for a leak you cannot undo.

## Design point: exact cost attribution

Lesson [A3.9](/learn/ai/a3-llm-apis/cost-metering/) taught metering one call. A platform adds four rules.

1. **Tag at the door.** The gateway reads the team from the key and the feature from a header checked against a registry. Reject unknown features, since free text breaks every report.
2. **Write rows from the provider's usage fields.** Keep the four token classes, the answering model, and the price version, so a price fix can be replayed.
3. **Mirror tags at the provider where you can.** On AWS Bedrock, an application inference profile takes tags, and "Usage and costs for requests made to that Region with that model will be tracked". That is a second ledger you did not write.
4. **Reconcile monthly.**

**Worked example (illustrative).** The ledger total for all teams is a little below the invoice. The gap is above the 0.5% bar. The hunt finds a script using the raw provider key, and streams the client cut, which have no usage event in the ledger (check how your provider bills a cut stream). The fix: remove raw keys, and log a partial row from the last event seen.

**Why estimates drift.** Tokenizers change: lesson [A3.9](/learn/ai/a3-llm-apis/cost-metering/) quotes noticeably more tokens from Anthropic's newer one, so an old estimator reads low. Streaming hides usage: Azure says that when streaming, "prompt tokens are always estimated". And token classes have different prices: a Sonnet 5.5 cache read costs a small fraction of input, and batch is half.

**Enforcement.** At 80% of budget the budget service alerts. At 100% it zeroes that team's bucket and the gateway answers 402. Others keep running. Rotating the shared provider key would stop everyone.

## Failure modes and an incident drill

| Failure | What happens | How we detect it | What we do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary outage | 5xx, 529, timeouts | Error rate and first-token time; breaker after 5 failures in 10 s | Fall back in budget; probe, ramp back |
| Silent degradation | Status 200, quality falls (2025: 16% of one model's requests in the worst hour) | Per-route probe under its bar | Move the share; page |
| Provider spend-cap 429 | All teams fail until the 1st, 00:00 UTC | `enforced_spend_limit_reached` | Never retry; fail over; own limit lower |
| One team burns the pool | Others get 429s | Tokens per request; spend per hour vs baseline | Team bucket; size cap; kill switch |
| Retry storm | Attempts above 1.1 times first attempts | Retry ratio | Retry budget, one layer |
| Cross-tenant cache read | One team sees another's answer | Cross-tenant hits above 0 | Tenant in key; purge |
| Quota store down | Counts lost | Store health | Local share / N, alert |
| Ledger drift | Chargeback wrong | Gap above 0.5% | Find bypass and cut streams |

## Drill: Eval platform

Spend 10 to 15 minutes. The gateway routes and fails over by evals, and those evals need a home: an **eval platform**, a service that tests model behavior and decides whether a change may ship.

**The bar.** A change that lowers quality on a slice that matters must not merge, and the gate must not block good changes on noise. Measure two rates over 30 days: regressions that reached production on gated slices (target 0), and blocked pull requests a human overrode as noise (target under 5%).

**The parts.**

- **Datasets.** Versioned golden sets, split into a dev half and a test half. Each case has a slice tag such as `refund`.
- **Runners.** They call the gateway as their own tenant, `evals`, at low priority, so eval load cannot starve production. Non-urgent runs can use the Batch API (50% off, its own limits).
- **Judges.** Pin the version and test it like a classifier. Hamel Husain's guide says to "report the judge's True Positive Rate and True Negative Rate separately", and warns "Raw agreement can be misleading when classes are imbalanced". It aims for "30 to 50 examples of each label in each set when possible", and says that below 60 examples "the confidence intervals are often too wide to support a useful conclusion". Use a different model family for the judge than for the candidate.
- **Scoreboards.** Pass rate per slice per version, with the margin.
- **CI gates.** CI means continuous integration: a check on each pull request. The gate script returns an **exit code**, 0 for pass and 1 for fail, and a 1 blocks the merge button. The promptfoo docs mark `--fail-on-error` with "Fail on any test failures". Their pass-rate example is a script that exits with status 1 below 95%.
- **Trace import and labeling.** Bad production traces (lesson [A9.1](/learn/ai/a9-llmops/tracing/)) go to a human labeling queue (lesson [A8.9](/learn/ai/a8-evals/feedback-flywheel/)). Labels become new cases and retest the judge.

**A failure to design for: a noisy gate.** A golden set is a sample, so its pass rate has a margin of error. A hard pass line on a small set blocks good changes about as often as it passes them when the true rate sits near the line. Two fixes: compare with the baseline on the same cases and count flips (lesson [A8.1](/learn/ai/a8-evals/eval-driven-design/)), and gate by slice, so no slice may fall more than a set number of points. A flaky gate gets switched off, so stability is part of the bar.

Step through the gate. First a regression, then the fix.

**Cost of the gate.** Eval cost per release is cases times runs times the price per judged answer, plus the candidate calls. A full golden set at release is a modest bill. A small smoke set on each pull request is far cheaper. So run the full set at release and the smoke set on each pull request. Labels cost more, as people's time, which we do not price.

## Staff answer vs senior answer

| Topic | Senior answer | Staff answer |
|---|---|---|
| Quotas | A proxy with keys and a request limit | Token buckets on TPM and RPM that reserve and refund, guarantees per team, burst credit from overflow, a surge test |
| Routing | The cheapest model that seems fine | Eval-gated routes, a cascade with a measured false-accept rate, a break-even rate |
| Failure | Retry, then fall back | One retry layer, a 10% budget, a fallback order, eval probes per provider |
| Isolation | Separate keys | Tenant in the cache key, a cross-tenant hit counter at 0, a per-key kill switch |
| Cost | A monthly total from estimates | Tags at the gateway, rows from usage fields, a monthly reconcile with a 0.5% bar |

## What experienced teams know

1. **A spend-cap 429 never clears.** Retrying, "including the SDK's automatic retries, fails until access resumes", at 00:00 UTC on the first of next month. Our reading: set your own limit lower and stop retries on `enforced_spend_limit_reached`. [Rate limits](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits).
2. **`max_tokens` counts under one provider and not another.** Anthropic leaves it out of OTPM. OpenAI uses the maximum of it and an estimate, and advises setting it close to the expected answer. A gateway that reserves output should do the same. [OpenAI rate limits](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/rate-limits).
3. **A provider can degrade with no error.** Anthropic's own evals "simply didn't capture the degradation". Our reading: probe each route yourself. [Postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues).
4. **Cap retries as a ratio.** Retry only while retries are under 10% of requests, which cuts the worst case from about 3 times the load to barely above 1. [Google SRE book](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/).
5. **Gateway token limits are approximate.** Azure says concurrent requests "can temporarily exceed the configured token limit", and each gateway counts alone. Our reading: leave headroom, count in a shared store. [Azure policy](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/api-management/llm-token-limit-policy).
6. **Shared caches need the tenant in the key.** APISIX warns of cross-consumer reads. Cloudflare keys on the entire request and lets the caller set a custom key. Our reading: audit everything that can shorten the key. [APISIX](https://apisix.apache.org/docs/apisix/plugins/ai-cache/), [Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai-gateway/features/caching/).

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bucket per team + burst credit | Isolation and high use | A borrowing rule to test | A shared platform with spiky teams |
| Reserve max_tokens, then refund | Safe under unknown output | Held tokens; a startup drain if too large | You must protect other tenants |
| Cascade | Large cost cut | Check cost, double pay, buffered output | Many easy requests, a reliable check |
| Retry budget | No retry storm | Some recoverable calls fail fast | Any shared provider path |
| Tenant in cache key | No cross-tenant reads | Lower hit rate | Always, with an opt-in shared namespace |
| Eval probe per route | Catches silent degradation | Probe cost | Quality is the product |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "The bar is isolation: a team inside its guarantee sees almost no 429s when another surges. Each team gets token buckets on TPM and RPM. I reserve input plus the route's p95 output and refund the rest, because cost is unknown until the call ends. Guarantees sum under the provider limit, and overflow is lent as burst credit. Routes are gated by evals. I retry in one layer under a 10% budget, and I run eval probes per provider, because a provider can degrade with a 200. The cache key has the tenant. Spend comes from usage fields and is reconciled against the invoice." That names the bar, the mechanism, and the proof.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not limit by request count alone: one team can send huge prompts at a normal rate. Do not reserve the model maximum. Do not call a cascade cheaper without a pass rate. Do not retry in three layers. Do not hash only the last message into a shared cache key. Do not estimate cost when the response carries the real count.
</Callout>
