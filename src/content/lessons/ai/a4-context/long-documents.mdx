## The problem

A legal team has a 150,000-token contract bundle. They want answers to 20 questions, for example "Who can end the agreement, and when?" Other questions are "Which clauses mention a penalty?" and "Summarize the risks." The text is long, the questions differ, and each answer must be right. A wrong answer is worse than a slow one.

You have four ways to feed that document to a model. Each fails in its own way. This lesson names them, prices them, and shows how to choose. Lesson [A4.3](/learn/ai/a4-context/context-budgeting/) ([context budgeting](/learn/ai/a4-context/context-budgeting/)) showed that the window is finite and that more text is not always better. Lesson [A2.5](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/prompt-caching/) ([prompt caching](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/prompt-caching/)) showed how to pay less for a repeated prefix. Here we combine both.

## Option 1: stuff the whole document in

**Stuffing** means putting the entire document in the prompt and asking the question. It is the simplest design: one call and no moving parts. The model sees everything, so a question that needs the whole text gets it.

It has two costs. The first is money. Each question re-reads 150,000 tokens. The Sonnet 5.5 input price is $2 per million tokens (as of 2026-10-16, [Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). At that price, the cost is $0.30 per question before the answer. The prompt cache turns this into a $0.015 read after the first question. Lesson A4.5 works the 20-question total: $6.00 against $0.66.

The second cost is quality. The same pricing page says 4.6 and later models, except Haiku 5.5, include the full 1M-token window at standard prices. A window of that size can hold the document. It does not guarantee the model uses all of it well. Liu et al. ("Lost in the Middle") found the following pattern. Performance is "often highest when relevant information occurs at the beginning or end of the input context". Performance "significantly degrades" for information in the middle. Chroma tested 18 models on LongMemEval. Prompts of about 113,000 tokens scored significantly lower than focused prompts of about 300 tokens holding the same needed information. Both studies used older models than the ones you will call today. So measure your own model on your own questions. The direction is the warning: more tokens can mean lower recall.

Also check the price page for your model. Haiku 5.5 charges more for a prompt over 100,000 tokens ($0.50 per million input instead of $0.10). That rate applies even on a cache hit.

## How to write a stuffed prompt

Anthropic's prompting guide gives three habits for long inputs. These are vendor advice, so test them on your set.

1. Rule: **put the document first and the question last**. The guide says to place long documents "near the top of your prompt, above your query, instructions, and examples". It also says that queries at the end "can improve response quality by up to 30 percent in tests". That is Anthropic's claim, from tests we cannot see. It also fits the cache: the large, stable part comes first and the small, changing question comes last (lesson [A4.5](/learn/ai/a4-context/cache-friendly-layout/)).
2. Rule: **give each document a structure**. Wrap each one in `<document>` tags with `<document_content>` and `<source>` subtags. That way, the model can tell documents apart.
3. **Ask for quotes first.** For long-document tasks, ask the model to quote the relevant parts before it does the task. The guide says this helps the model "focus on the relevant content and ignore the rest of the document".

The **citations feature** automates the third habit. You mark a document block with `citations: {enabled: true}`. The API then returns the exact supporting passages, with pointers into your document. Anthropic documents three effects. `cited_text` does not count toward your output tokens, so quoting is cheaper than asking the model to write the quote. The pointers are guaranteed to be valid locations in the document you sent. And in Anthropic's own tests the feature picked the most relevant quotes more often than prompt-only quoting. The cost is a slight rise in input tokens. Citations also change the system prompt text, so toggling them breaks the prefix cache (lesson [A4.5](/learn/ai/a4-context/cache-friendly-layout/)).

## Option 2: map-reduce

When the text is too long, or you want to avoid the whole window, split it. **Map-reduce** has two steps. In the map step, one call per chunk produces a short summary or extraction. In the reduce step, one call reads all the summaries and answers. The names come from data processing: map runs per item, reduce merges the results. A backend engineer will see the shape of a scatter-gather query.

<Ai4LongWalk client:visible />

The OpenAI cookbook ("Summarizing long documents") gives a real run. It splits on a delimiter, with a minimum chunk size of 500 tokens by default and no overlap between chunks. Each chunk is summarized alone. On a document of about 14,631 tokens, the lowest detail setting used 1 chunk. It wrote a 235-token summary and took about 9.7 seconds. The highest detail setting used 31 chunks, wrote a 6,742-token summary, and took about 4 minutes 8 seconds. The page says a summary of a 20,000-token document will not be twice as long as one of a 10,000-token document. It also says a recursive mode costs more but "can increase consistency and coherence".

Map-reduce has a built-in risk. The summarizer of chunk 17 does not know your question. It keeps what looks important to a summary, and a fact you need later can be dropped. The reduce step cannot restore it. Finding: **we found no sourced number for how much information map-reduce loses**. The cookbook names the risk but gives no loss rate. Do not quote one. Measure it: ask questions with known answers and count the misses.

Map-reduce is a good fit when the question needs every part, such as "summarize the risks", and the text does not fit in the window. It is a poor fit for "find one clause".

## Options 3 and 4: route and retrieve

Method: **chunk and route** keeps the chunks. It builds a cheap table of contents (one line per chunk). For each question, it runs two calls. A router call reads only the table of contents and picks a few chunks. An answer call reads only those chunks. You avoid reading all 150,000 tokens per question, and you need no vector database.

**Retrieval** goes further. It turns each chunk into a numeric vector. It stores the vectors in an index. For each question, it fetches the nearest chunks. This is the subject of module A5 and we do not teach it here. For now, treat it as a function that returns the best few chunks for a question. Its cost per question is very low. It has a different failure: it can miss a passage whose words do not resemble the question.

A rule from the plan for this track: use long context for one document, and retrieval for a corpus. Lesson [A5.7](/learn/ai/a5-rag/graphrag-longctx/) gives the full decision.

## Choose by numbers

The picker below prices all four strategies for your inputs, using the Sonnet 5.5 prices above. The recommendation is a rule of thumb. The costs are arithmetic from the stated assumptions.

What to notice:

1. **Check one cell by hand.** Pick 150K tokens, 20 questions. Stuffing costs $0.66 for the cached input. Questions and answers add 20 x (100 x $2 + 300 x $10) / 10^6 = $0.064. So the total is $0.724.
2. Note: **map-reduce is not cheap**. Each question re-reads every chunk: 30 chunk calls, 156,000 input tokens, and 9,000 output tokens. That is about $0.42 per question, or $8.47 for 20. It makes sense only when the document does not fit, or when you reuse the summaries (our model does not).
3. **Retrieval wins on cost.** It reads about 2,700 tokens per question: $0.0084, or $0.168 for 20. It is the cheapest, and it is also the one that can miss a passage.
4. **At 2M tokens, stuffing is struck out.** The text does not fit in a 1M-token window.
5. **Switch to "Whole document".** The pick changes to stuffing or map-reduce, because retrieval cannot answer "summarize everything" from five chunks.

## Trade-offs

| Strategy | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stuff + cache | One call, full text, simple | Re-reading cost, recall dips in long prompts, window limit | One document, up to hundreds of thousands of tokens, whole-text questions |
| Map-reduce | Handles any size, parallel | Information loss, many calls, high cost per question | The text exceeds the window and the question spans all of it |
| Chunk and route | No index, reads only chosen chunks | An extra call, a table of contents to maintain | Few questions, scattered passages, no search infrastructure |
| Retrieval | Lowest cost per question | Index to build, misses on weak matches | Many questions, a corpus, or a document that changes |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Start from the question type, not the tool: "Is this a lookup, a few passages, or the whole document? For one 150K document and 20 questions I stuff and cache it, put the question last, and ask for quotes. For a corpus I retrieve." Then name your quality check: a golden set with known answers, run on each strategy.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "the window is 1M tokens, so we just send everything". Fitting is not the same as working. Recall can fall as the prompt grows, and each question pays to re-read the text. Also do not claim a loss rate for map-reduce. No source gives one. Say that you would measure it.
</Callout>
