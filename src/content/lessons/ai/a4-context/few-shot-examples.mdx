## The problem

You need a bot to turn messy shipping notes into a fixed record: `{"city": ..., "zip": ..., "priority": ...}`. The task prompt has six rules. The model follows five. On "Rush to Leeds, LS1 4AB, ASAP, thx" it writes the priority as `"ASAP, thx"`, not `"high"`. You add a seventh rule. Then it fails on a different note.

Rules describe what you want. They are bad at showing the exact shape of an edge case. A **few-shot prompt** [junior] puts a handful of worked examples of the task into the prompt. Each example is an input and the output you want. The model reads them and continues the pattern. Three examples of a messy note and its clean record often do more than seven sentences of rules.

Few-shot examples are cheap to try and easy to get subtly wrong. This lesson covers why they work, what goes wrong, how to pick them, and what they cost.

## Why examples work

The model's weights do not change when you add examples. The examples are only text in the window. But the model is trained to continue text. The best continuation of a text with three input and output pairs is a fourth pair. That fourth pair is written in the same style. This is called **in-context learning** [mid]: the model adapts to a task from the prompt alone, with no training step.

Brown et al. (the GPT-3 paper) defined the setup. They typically used K from 10 to 100 examples. They gave this reason: "as this is how many examples can fit in the model's context window". Their model had 2,048 tokens. They also note that "Larger values of K are usually but not always better". The gain can be large. On a task of unscrambling words whose letters were cycled, GPT-3 175B scored 3.66% with no examples. It scored 21.7% with one example and 37.9% with a few (Table 3.10). That was a 2020 model on a puzzle task, so do not read it as a promise for your task. It shows that examples can teach a format the instructions alone do not.

For current models, Anthropic's prompting guide says to make examples relevant, diverse, and structured. They should be wrapped in `<example>` tags. The guide also says "Include 3–5 examples for best results". That is vendor guidance, not a law. The right count for your task is the number where your own eval stops improving. Plan to measure it, as shown below.

## What goes wrong

**Order changes the answer.** Zhao et al. (2021) tested GPT-3 on classification and found three biases. Two matter here.

- **Recency bias** [mid]: the model leans toward the label of the last examples. With four sentiment examples in the order Positive, Positive, Positive, Negative, the paper reports this result. In that result, "nearly 90% of predictions" were Negative.
- **Majority label bias** [mid]: the model leans toward the label that appears most often in the examples. On a 4-shot question-answering test (LAMA), the paper measured repeat answers. The paper says "50.2% of the model predictions are a repeat of one of the four training answers". By contrast, the correct repeat rate is 24.7%.

Order alone can swing the score. For 4-shot SST-2 on GPT-3 2.7B, the paper looks at permuting the same examples. It says this "can cause accuracy to go from near chance (54.3%) to near state-of-the-art (93.4%)". Step through it below. The first bars are from the paper. The middle one is marked illustrative.

These are results on 2021 models. Newer models are more robust, but you should not assume the effect is gone. It is cheap to test: shuffle the examples and rerun your eval.

Risk: **the model copies the surface**. If three of five examples mention "Acme Corp", the model may write "Acme Corp" into answers about other companies. It copied a detail and not the rule. The fix is to vary names, numbers, and phrasing across examples. So the only thing the examples share is the pattern you want.

## Choosing the examples

There are two ways to pick them.

**A fixed set.** You choose 3 to 8 examples once. Make them diverse. Cover the common case, one edge case, and one case where the right answer is "none" or "unknown". Balance the labels. Test the set on held-out cases, which are cases that are not in the prompt. A set that scores well on its own examples only proves the model can copy.

**Dynamic selection.** You keep a bank of 200 labelled examples. For each request, you pick the K nearest to the query and put those in the prompt. Liu et al. found that choosing examples semantically similar to the test input beats choosing at random. Their abstract reports 45.5% on the NQ dataset and 41.9% on ToTTo. The abstract does not say if these are absolute or relative gains. The nearness test uses embeddings (the [Embeddings](/learn/ai/a1-model-function/embeddings/) lesson). Embed the query and each stored example, then rank by cosine similarity:

<Math block tex="\text{sim}(q, e) = \frac{q \cdot e}{\lVert q \rVert \, \lVert e \rVert}" />

In words: the cosine of the angle between the two vectors. It is near 1 when two texts mean the same thing. Take the top K. Work through one case. The query "Rush to Leeds, ASAP" has similarity 0.91 to a stored example. That example is about "Urgent to York, as soon as possible". The query has similarity 0.34 to "Standard parcel, no rush needed to Bath". The first one enters the prompt. (These two scores are made up to show the ranking step.)

The cost of dynamic selection is an embedding call and a vector lookup on every request. It also changes the prompt prefix on every request. So the examples cannot sit in a shared cached prefix (the [Prompt Caching: Mechanics and Economics](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/prompt-caching/) lesson). Use a fixed set when 5 diverse examples cover the task. Use dynamic selection when the task has many sub-types and a fixed set cannot cover all of them.

## Numbers: what examples cost

Examples add input tokens to every call, and that cost repeats on every request. If the fixed set sits in a cached prefix, most calls read it at a deep discount. So a fixed set that hits the cache is far cheaper than the same examples sent uncached. Prices are on the [Anthropic pricing page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing). A cache entry lasts a few minutes, so the saving needs steady traffic. For the full mechanism, see the [Prompt Caching: Mechanics and Economics](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/prompt-caching/) lesson. Examples also take space in the window (the [Context Window Budgeting](/learn/ai/a4-context/context-budgeting/) lesson), and they can crowd out retrieved facts.

## How to find your K

1. Build a labelled eval set that is separate from the example bank, at least 50 cases, with edge cases.
2. Run K = 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 examples. For each K, run 3 different shuffles.
3. Plot the mean score and the spread. Pick the smallest K where the mean stops rising, and where the spread across shuffles is small.
4. Re-run when the model changes.

If the curve is flat from K = 0, the instructions are enough and the examples only cost tokens. If the spread across shuffles is large, your eval set may be too small. Or the task may be fragile to order.

## Trade-offs

| Option | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rules only (zero-shot) | Shortest prompt | Weak on format and edge cases | The model already does the task well |
| Fixed few-shot set | Simple, cacheable | Tokens every call; copying risk | 3 to 8 examples cover the task |
| Dynamic selection | Targets each query's sub-type | Embedding and lookup per call; no shared prefix | Many sub-types, large example bank |
| Fine-tuning | No examples in the prompt | Training and upkeep | A stable task at very high volume |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say it as a procedure: "I start with 3 to 5 diverse examples, which is what vendor guidance suggests. Then I measure K on a held-out set with three shuffles each, and pick the knee. Examples go in the cached prefix if the set is fixed." Then name the risks: order bias and copying the surface of an example.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "more examples are always better". Brown et al. say larger K is usually but not always better, and every example costs tokens on every call. Also do not score the prompt on the same cases you used as examples. The score will look good and tell you nothing.
</Callout>
