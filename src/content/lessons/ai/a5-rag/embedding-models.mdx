## The problem

You are building search over 50 million document chunks. The [Embeddings](/learn/ai/a1-model-function/embeddings/) lesson showed what an embedding is: a vector where distance stands for meaning. It did not say which embedding model to pick. That choice is harder to undo than most.

Every vector in your index comes from one model. Vectors from two models live in two different coordinate systems, so you cannot compare them. If you change the model, you embed every chunk again and rebuild the index. So the pick sets your storage bill, your query quality, and the price of a future switch.

## Naive solution: pick the top of a leaderboard

The usual first move is to open a public leaderboard and take the model at the top. The **MTEB** [mid] paper (Massive Text Embedding Benchmark) built that kind of table. It covers 8 task types, 58 datasets, and 112 languages, and it tested 33 models. Its own abstract says "no particular text embedding method dominates across all tasks."

That sentence is the warning. A model that wins on average can lose on your task. The leaderboard changes often, and the live page did not load for us, so we quote no rankings here. Use MTEB to build a shortlist. Do not use it to decide.

## The mechanism: four knobs and one test

Think of an embedding model as a function with four properties. Three show up in the price list, and one hides in your data.

**Dimensions.** OpenAI documents `text-embedding-3-small` at 1,536 numbers per vector and `text-embedding-3-large` at 3,072. Each number takes a fixed number of bytes. So storage grows in a straight line with dimensions. So does search time, because every distance reads every number. The [ANN Indexes and Billion-Scale Search](/learn/ai/a5-rag/ann-indexes/) lesson shows how much that hurts at scale.

**Max input.** OpenAI's models take up to 8,192 tokens per input. Cohere lists `embed-v4.0` at a 128k context and its v3 models at 512 tokens (docs.cohere.com, as of 2026-10-16). The limit sets how big a chunk can be in one call. It is not a target. A chunk can be short and still sit under the limit. The [Chunking and Contextual Retrieval](/learn/ai/a5-rag/chunking-contextual/) lesson covers how to pick chunk size.

**Price.** OpenAI lists $0.02 per million input tokens for `text-embedding-3-small`, $0.13 for `text-embedding-3-large`, and $0.10 for the older `text-embedding-ada-002`. These prices are as of 2026-10-16, from developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing. Embedding is a one-time cost per chunk, plus the cost of each query, which is one short text.

**Domain fit.** A general model has seen mostly web text. If your corpus is legal clauses, source code, or product part numbers, the model may place them less well. We have no sourced number for how much. That is the reason for the test below.

### Shorter vectors: Matryoshka

Some models are trained so the first numbers of the vector carry the coarse meaning and later numbers add detail. This is **Matryoshka representation learning** [senior] (MRL), named after nesting dolls. You can cut the vector to a prefix and still use it. OpenAI exposes this as a `dimensions` parameter. Its guide says `text-embedding-3-large` cut to 256 dimensions still beats `text-embedding-ada-002` at 1,536 dimensions on MTEB. It also says that if you slice a vector yourself, you must divide by its L2 norm. Dividing by that norm brings the length back to 1.

The MRL paper has hard numbers, but on images. Accuracy stays nearly flat as the vector shrinks from thousands of dimensions to a few dozen, then falls fast (Table 1). The abstract claims "up to 14x smaller embedding size" at the same accuracy. Step through the cut. The shape is what to take away: a long flat stretch, then a knee. For text, find the knee with your own queries.

A much smaller vector costs the paper's model under one point of accuracy. Notice what you cannot read off the paper: where the knee sits for your text model. Test it.

### The test: recall@k on your own queries

Build a small set of real queries. For each one, mark which chunk holds the answer. Then run each query against the index and ask: did the right chunk appear in the top k results? **Recall@k** [mid] is the share of queries where it did. If most queries find their chunk in the top 5, recall@5 is high.

Do this for each shortlisted model and for each dimension you might cut to. A few hundred labelled queries is enough to tell models apart when the gap is large. Keep the set. You will need it again when a new model ships, and in the evaluation lesson of this module.

```python title="compare_models.py"
from openai import OpenAI

client = OpenAI()

def embed(texts, model, dims=None):
    kwargs = {"model": model, "input": texts}
    if dims:
        kwargs["dimensions"] = dims  # shortens the vector, still length 1
    resp = client.embeddings.create(**kwargs)
    return [d.embedding for d in resp.data]

# for each (model, dims) pair: embed chunks, embed queries,
# take top-5 by dot product, count queries whose gold chunk is in it
```

## Numbers: the switch bill

The example uses 50 million chunks of 500 tokens, which is 25 billion tokens.

The bigger model costs several times more per token than the small one, and the older model sits between them. These are list prices. They leave out retries, rate-limit waits, and batch discounts, and OpenAI's page lists no batch price for embeddings.

Storage tells the other half. At this scale, float32 vectors take hundreds of gigabytes before any index. A short MRL cut shrinks that by a large factor.

So the dollar cost of a re-embed is small next to the other costs of a switch. You also pay for the engineering time to rebuild. Costs include a period with two indexes and a re-run of your whole eval. Plan the switch like a database migration. Build the new index beside the old one. Send a slice of queries to both, compare recall@k, then cut over.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|
| More dimensions | More detail per vector | Storage and search cost grow in a straight line | Hard queries, small corpus |
| Fewer dimensions (MRL cut) | Smaller index, faster search | Some quality, measured on your data | Large corpus, tight memory |
| Bigger, pricier model | Often better recall | Several times the per-token price (large against small) | Quality gap shows in your eval |
| Long max input | Big chunks in one call | Vague vectors for mixed text | Long, tightly linked passages |
| Domain-tuned model | Better fit to your jargon | Fewer choices, a vendor tie | Public models fail your eval |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Show the whole bill: "I would shortlist two or three models from MTEB. Then I would run recall@5 on a few hundred of our own labelled queries. I would also test a shorter dimension. At 50 million vectors, storage is hundreds of gigabytes, so a short dimension cut matters." Then name the switch cost: a re-embed of every chunk, and an old and new index side by side.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "use the best model on MTEB" and stop. Do not mix vectors from two models in one index, or compare them with each other. And do not cut dimensions by slicing without renormalizing, or by assuming a model supports it. Only models trained for dimension cuts, or an API with a `dimensions` parameter, cut cleanly.
</Callout>

<Callout type="deep" title="Bi-encoders and why vectors are precomputed">
An embedding model here is a **bi-encoder** [mid]: it encodes the query and each document separately. That is why you can embed the corpus once and store it. A model that reads the query and a document together can judge them better. But such a model must run once per pair. You cannot precompute that, so it only fits a short list of candidates. That is the reranker of the [Reranking and Query Rewriting](/learn/ai/a5-rag/reranking-rewriting/) lesson.
</Callout>
