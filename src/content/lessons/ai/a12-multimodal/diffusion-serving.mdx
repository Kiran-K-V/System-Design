## The problem

Your product lets users type a prompt and get an image back. A chat model streams its first token in under a second. An image model gives you nothing until the whole picture is done. The user watches a spinner.

So three numbers decide whether the feature works. How long is the spinner? How much does one image cost? How many users can you serve at once without the spinner turning into a minute?

All three come from one fact about how these models work. They do not write an image in one pass. They start from noise and clean it up over many passes. This lesson builds the cost and latency picture from that fact, then shows how to serve it.

Here is a made-up product bar, marked **illustrative**. Quality: at least as good as your 30-step baseline in a side-by-side vote. Latency: 95 out of 100 images back in 5 seconds. Cost: under $0.005 per image. By the end you can check whether a design meets it.

## How an image gets made

Four terms first, each at the moment you need it.

A **latent** is a small grid of numbers that stands for an image. Think of a thumbnail-sized sketch that a decoder can blow up into a full picture. Working on it is cheaper than working on every pixel. Latent diffusion models, the family behind Stable Diffusion, do their work in this smaller space. The Stable Diffusion paper says this "significantly" cuts compute.

A **VAE** (variational autoencoder) is the pair of networks that move between pixels and latents. You use its decoder once at the end, to turn the final latent into pixels. It is like unzipping a file.

A **step** is one round of cleaning. Generation starts from random noise in latent shape. Each step runs a neural network on the noisy latent and the prompt. The network predicts what part of the latent is noise. For Stable Diffusion v1 that network is a **UNet**, a network shaped like a letter U that works at several sizes of the grid. Newer models, such as FLUX, use a transformer instead (the architecture you met in [the Transformer Forward Pass lesson](/learn/ai/a1-model-function/forward-pass/)). A **scheduler**, a small piece of plain code with no weights, then subtracts a slice of that predicted noise. After 20 to 50 repeats the latent is clean.

**Guidance** (classifier-free guidance, CFG) is a trick to make the image follow the prompt more closely. Each step the model makes two predictions: one with your prompt and one with an empty prompt. The final prediction pushes away from the empty one toward the prompted one. The Hugging Face tutorial runs both as one batch of two, "to avoid doing two forward passes". It is still about twice the work in that call. This is derived from the batch doubling, not a measured number.

Every step uses the same weights and reads the same amount of data. So work is flat per step, unlike an LLM where each new token has a longer context. The one-line model is:

> latency = steps x time per step

## The numbers you can source

A vendor benchmark gives the cleanest public numbers I could verify. **Vendor numbers**: Salad sells GPU capacity, so it has a stake in the result.

[Salad's Stable Diffusion v1.4 benchmark](https://blog.salad.com/stable-diffusion-gpu-benchmark-v1-4/) generated 512x512 images with 50 steps. Read two things from it:

| GPU | Seconds per image | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| RTX 3060 (on SaladCloud) | 6.6 | page text |
| A100 (on AWS) | 4.3 | chart |
| T4 | 14.9 | chart |

The RTX 3060 also has two cost figures in the text: 5,244 images per dollar and "around $191" per million images. The A100 chart shows 300 images per dollar. The page does not list hourly prices. I read the A100 and T4 values from the chart and not from text, so treat them as less firm. I left out the RTX 3090 on purpose: the page text and the chart disagree on its time.

Time per image is roughly the number of steps times the time per step. The A100 spends about 0.09 s per step (~, from the benchmark above). That figure also holds the text encoding and the VAE decode. The animation above uses it for that reason. So fewer steps means a proportionally faster image.

These numbers are for Stable Diffusion v1.4 at 512x512. They are old. I could not find a source that states seconds per image for SDXL or FLUX on a named GPU with resolution and step count, so I give none. The Hugging Face benchmark dataset gives only forward-pass times with no GPU named, so it does not help here. Measure your own model on your own GPU before you plan capacity.

## Cost per image

A GPU is rented by the hour. So the cost per image is the GPU price per hour spread over the images it makes in that hour. A faster image means more images per hour, and so a lower cost per image.

Live price, **Lambda** on-demand, **A100 SXM 80 GB**, per GPU-hour, excluding tax: ~$2.79 (as of 2026-10-18, [Lambda pricing](https://lambda.ai/pricing)). The page lists it in the 8-GPU instance table, and I matched the row from the page layout. For a managed API, [Replicate](https://replicate.com/pricing) lists an A100 80 GB at a higher hourly rate (as of 2026-10-18; the page shows no date).

Now the picture. I pair Salad's A100 speed with Lambda's A100 price. They are different clouds, so this is a rough estimate, not a quote. At 50 steps an image costs a fraction of a cent. At 30 steps it costs noticeably less, because each image uses less GPU time. At 4 steps, with a distilled model, it costs a small fraction of that again.

One more comparison shows that "faster GPU" does not mean "cheaper image". The RTX 3060 was slower than the A100, yet its images cost far less, because its hourly price was far lower. Price per hour matters as much as speed. Pricing basis matters too: Salad notes that cheaper spot-style instances exist on another provider.

## Fewer steps: distillation

The biggest cost lever is the step count. Cut steps and both latency and cost fall in the same ratio.

**Distillation** is training a student model to copy a teacher in fewer steps. You met it for language models in [the Distillation and Training Data lesson](/learn/ai/a11-finetuning/distillation-data/). The idea is the same here. The teacher takes 30 or 50 steps. The student is trained so that one step lands where several teacher steps would have. Think of a hiker learning a shortcut so the trip takes 4 stops, not 40.

Three published methods, from their abstracts only:

| Method | Steps | What the authors say |
|---|---|---|
| Latent Consistency Models (LCM) | 2 to 4 | High-quality 768x768 images from a pretrained latent model |
| Adversarial Diffusion Distillation (ADD) | 1 to 4 | Four steps reach the quality of the SDXL teacher; one step beats earlier few-step methods |
| SDXL-Lightning | one-step and few-step | Open weights at 1024 px |

I found no quality score (such as a distance-to-real-images number) on those abstract pages, so I quote none. Quality claims are the authors' own. Also, the "one step" result is weaker than the four-step result. You pick a point on the curve.

Play with the curve. The quality line is **illustrative**. I made it up to show the shape, not to measure anything.

One honest caveat on the model: I assumed a distilled step costs as much as a standard step. Some distilled models drop the guidance pass, which would make a step cheaper still. I have no source for how much, so it is not modeled.

## Serving: a queue and a GPU pool

An LLM request is variable. The output length is unknown, so servers use continuous batching, which you met in [the Prompt Caching: Mechanics and Economics lesson](/learn/ai/a2-inference-cost/prompt-caching/). A diffusion job is fixed. Same step count, same image size, same work. Every job takes about 2.58 s on this GPU. That makes capacity easy to calculate and a different design natural.

Think of a car wash, not a restaurant. Every car takes the same time. You size the number of bays, put cars in a line, and open a new bay when the line grows.

The design is: the API accepts a request, puts a job on a queue, and replies with a job id. A GPU worker pulls a job, runs the steps, writes the image, and marks the job done. The client polls or gets a push. The queue lesson covers the mechanics.

<CrossTrackLink id="06-async/queues">
A queue holds jobs between the API and workers. It smooths bursts, lets workers pull at their own speed, and makes retries safe. The same pieces apply here: a job id, a visibility timeout so a crashed worker does not lose the job, and a dead-letter queue for jobs that always fail. This lesson only adds what is specific to image work.
</CrossTrackLink>

Size the pool from peak load, with headroom. Take the peak rate of images per second, and multiply by the seconds one image needs. That tells you how many GPUs are busy on average. You never want to run at 100%, because a burst then builds a long line. So plan for about 70% busy and round up. The cost per image then rises above the always-busy figure. That gap is the headroom you pay for.

Batching on the GPU works differently too. All jobs have the same shape, so you can group several into one batch and they finish together. Whether that helps depends on whether one image already fills the GPU. The guidance trick already makes each step a batch of two. I have no source for the gain, so measure it. Also fix the image size per worker pool. The Hugging Face guide warns that calling a compiled pipeline on a different image size "retriggers compilation which is slow and inefficient". So run one pool per size.

### Autoscale on queue depth

CPU use is a poor signal here, because a GPU worker looks busy whenever it has any job. The signal that matters is how many jobs are waiting. The Kubernetes autoscaler rule (it supports external metrics) is:

desired replicas = ceil(current replicas x current metric / target metric)

The idea: if each worker has many jobs waiting and the target is about one, the scaler multiplies the pool until the waiting jobs per worker fall back to the target. A pool with a long line grows fast. I took the formula from the Kubernetes docs. The docs do not name queue length as a metric, so the choice of queue depth is a design choice of mine.

### Cold starts and idle time

A new worker is not ready at once. The container must start, the model weights must load onto the GPU, and the first call may compile the graph. [Modal's docs](https://modal.com/docs/guide/cold-start) say containers boot in about 1 s, and the warm-up after that "can range from seconds to minutes". That page is general, not about diffusion. The Hugging Face guide says compilation is slow the first time, and regional compilation cuts compile time by 8 to 10 times. Warm up at container start, with a dummy image, and not on the first user request.

Cold starts break the latency budget. Illustrative: a new worker needs tens of seconds. A small share of requests lands during a scale-up and waits that long. So most requests are fast, but the slowest few percent are very slow. The 99th percentile blows past the target, even when the median looks fine. Fix it with a floor of warm workers (Modal calls it `min_containers`) and by scaling up earlier.

A floor costs money. Idle GPUs bill by the hour whether or not they have work. This is the opposite pressure. A larger floor means better latency and lower utilization, and utilization sets cost as the slider showed. Pick the floor from your traffic. For spiky traffic, a distilled model helps twice: shorter jobs mean shorter lines and cheaper cold capacity.

## Safety filter and delivery

Short, because these are small next to the GPU time.

**Output safety.** Generated images can break your policy even when the prompt looked fine. Run a classifier on the finished image before the user sees it, and also check the prompt. Reject or blur what fails. The classifier is a small model, so it adds little to the GPU bill, but it adds a step to the latency budget and it makes mistakes in both directions. I have no public figure for any filter's error rate. Measure yours on a labeled set, and decide which mistake costs more. A blocked image still cost you GPU time.

**Storage and delivery.** The worker writes the PNG or JPEG to object storage and returns a URL. A CDN in front serves repeat views. The backend track covers this in the [blob storage lesson](/learn/backend/08-technologies/blob-storage/). One AI-specific point: the same prompt with a different random seed gives a different image. So the image is not a cache hit for the next user, and storage is per job, not per prompt.

## Numbers for the product bar

Back to the bar from the start.

- **Quality bar:** at least as good as the 30-step baseline in a side-by-side vote. How to measure that is the [Evals for Generated Media](/learn/ai/a12-multimodal/media-evals/) lesson. For now, hold out 200 prompts, compare, and require a tie or better on most.
- **Latency budget:** 95 of 100 images in 5 s. Illustrative split: queue wait 1.5 s, generation 2.6 s, safety check and upload 0.9 s.
- **Cost:** 30 steps at 70% busy meets the cost cap, with headroom paid for. A 4-step distilled model costs a small fraction of that at the same busy share, if quality holds.
- **Failure rate:** cold starts hit a small share of requests in the illustrative case above. State your own number and test it.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 to 50 steps | Best quality for a model | 7 to 12 times the time and cost of 4 steps | Quality is the product and volume is low |
| Distilled, 1 to 4 steps | Seconds under 1, cost cut by roughly the step ratio | Some quality loss, a second model to maintain | High volume or interactive use |
| Warm floor of workers | Stable latency | Idle GPU bill (one A100 about $2,009 a month) | Latency target is tight |
| Scale from zero | No idle cost | Cold starts of seconds to minutes | Batch or low-traffic jobs |
| Managed API | No GPU operations | Higher price per GPU-hour ($5.04 vs $2.79 for an A100 here) | Early stage or unknown demand |

## What experienced teams know

1. **Step count is the largest lever.** Distilled models run in 1 to 4 steps, per the [ADD paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17042). Test one before you buy more GPUs.
2. **Fix resolution per worker pool.** Compiled graphs retrace when the size changes, per the [Hugging Face guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/diffusers/en/optimization/fp16). Mixed sizes in one pool cause repeated slow calls.
3. **Warm up at start, not on the first user.** The first compiled call is slow, and the same guide says regional compilation cut compile time by 8 to 10 times.
4. **Pricing basis changes cost more than speed does.** In [Salad's benchmark](https://blog.salad.com/stable-diffusion-gpu-benchmark-v1-4/) the 4.3 s A100 cost about 17 times more per image than the 6.6 s RTX 3060.
5. **Scale on queue depth, not CPU.** The Kubernetes [autoscaler](https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/run-application/horizontal-pod-autoscale/) can use external metrics. A GPU worker with one job looks as busy as one with ten.
6. **Check what a benchmark counts.** A time per image includes the text encoder and decode. Ask what resolution, step count, and GPU a number came from before you reuse it.

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "Latency is steps times step time, and cost per image is GPU price per hour divided by images per hour. I would cut steps with a distilled model first, serve through a queue with workers autoscaled on queue depth, keep a small warm floor for cold starts, run a safety classifier on the output, and deliver through object storage and a CDN. I would state the quality bar, the 95th percentile budget, and cost per image." That names the model, the lever, the serving shape, and the three numbers.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not size the pool from average load with no headroom, or scale on CPU use. Do not quote seconds per image without the GPU, resolution, and step count. Do not forget that idle GPU hours and cold starts add to cost and tail latency.
</Callout>
