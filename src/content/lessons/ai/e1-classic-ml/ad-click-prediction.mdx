## The problem

A user opens a page. The page has one ad slot. Fifty advertisers want it. Who wins?

Most ad systems rank by **expected value per impression**: the bid for a click, times the chance that this user clicks this ad on this page. That chance is the **pCTR**, the predicted click-through rate. Say it is 2%. Then a $1.00 click bid is worth two cents of expected revenue.

The pCTR is not only a ranking score. It is a **price input**. The auction multiplies it by money. If the model says 4% when the truth is 2%, the auction thinks the ad is worth twice as much as it is. It wins slots it should lose and charges advertisers for value that never arrives. Advertisers see cost per click rise, cut their bids, and leave.

So an ad model must be a good ranker and an honest probability. The second job is harder, and a dashboard that shows only the first will not warn you.

This lesson answers five questions:

1. What is the quality bar, when ranking quality can hide a pricing error?
2. What does the architecture look like?
3. How do you keep the probability honest when no-click examples are downsampled and clicks arrive late?
4. How do you store and update embedding tables too large for one machine?
5. How do you read features at auction rate, and roll out a model without a 3% revenue surprise?

Two drills reuse the machinery: ETA prediction, where one error direction costs more, and dynamic pricing.

## Requirements and the quality bar

The product is the click-probability service inside an ad auction. All traffic and latency numbers are **illustrative** unless a source is named.

**Functional.** FR1: score every candidate ad with a click probability for each auction. FR2: learn continuously from the clicks and non-clicks that follow. FR3: report predicted against observed click rate per segment, so bias is visible.

**Out of scope.** Candidate retrieval (lesson [E1.5](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/retrieval-ranking/)), advertiser bidding, budget pacing, and click fraud (lesson [E1.12](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/fraud-detection/)).

**Non-functional.** Fast (the auction has a tight deadline), fresh (interest moves), honest (the number is a probability), and safe to change (a deploy must not cost money).

**The bar.** Two metrics carry it, both from lesson [E1.6](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/offline-online-metrics/).

- **Logloss** scores each prediction by how much probability it gave to what happened. It is a **proper scoring rule**: the best score comes only from reporting your true belief. Lower is better.
- **Calibration error** groups predictions near 2% and asks whether about 2% of those impressions got a click. **Expected calibration error** (ECE) averages the gap between predicted and observed rate over such groups, weighted by size. The scikit-learn docs state the idea: among samples given a score near 0.8, about 80% should be positive ([scikit-learn, probability calibration](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)).

Our bar, a choice and not a source: logloss no worse than the current model, and calibration error under a stated band **in every segment that carries money** (country, device, ad format, new versus old advertiser). An average can look fine while one large segment is off by 30%.

**Why not AUC?** **AUC** is the chance that a random clicked impression scores higher than a random non-clicked one. It ignores the size of scores. Multiply every prediction by 2 and AUC is unchanged, but the auction now doubles every price. Google's ads team: "Accurate and well-calibrated predictions are not only essential to run the auction, they also allow for a loosely coupled overall system design separating concerns of optimizations in the auction from the machine learning machinery" ([McMahan et al., Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/41159.pdf)). Our reading: AUC says the model orders ads well. Only calibration says the auction can trust the number.

## Eval plan

The eval comes before the design (lesson [A0.2](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/ai-design-method/)); the methods are in lessons [E1.6](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/offline-online-metrics/) and E1.8.

**Offline.** Use a time-split test set: train on days 1 to N, test on day N+1. A random split leaks the future. Report logloss, calibration error, and AUC, each **per segment**, plus a **reliability plot** (predicted rate against observed rate; a calibrated model sits on the diagonal).

**Why offline misleads for ads.** The log was produced by the old model, so it holds only clicks on ads the old model chose. A new model that wants other ads is judged on data with no answer for them. Google notes that, "without strong additional assumptions, the inherent feedback loop in the system makes it impossible to provide theoretical guarantees for the impact of calibration" ([McMahan et al.](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/41159.pdf)). An offline win earns an online test, never a ship.

**Online.** Revenue per thousand auctions is the headline. Guardrails face advertisers: cost per click, spend against budget, and the share of advertisers whose cost per click moved past a band. A model that lifts revenue by overcharging borrows against next month's budgets.

**The slice that matters.** New ads and advertisers (few clicks, noisy predictions), small segments, and the high-pCTR tail.

## Budgets

**Latency.** An auction has a deadline in the tens of milliseconds (**illustrative**). Scoring gets a slice of it. Inside that slice, feature reads are the largest stage: network calls for the user, ad, and page, repeated per candidate. Embedding lookups come next. The dense network is small and calibration is one table lookup. The conclusion: the wall clock goes mostly to **reading data**, not to the neural network, the opposite of the LLM case. The DLRM authors call the model "memory-capacity and bandwidth intensive", because embeddings hold most of the parameters ([Naumov et al., DLRM](https://arxiv.org/html/1906.00091v1)).

**Dollars.** Cost per thousand auctions is instance price per hour, divided by auctions scored per hour at target load, times one thousand. I give no dollar figure: I found no primary price page for a serving instance to cite, and throughput depends on your model. Embedding memory sets the instance count more often than compute does.

## Core entities and API

**Entities.** `Impression` (request id, ad id, features used, model version, raw and calibrated pCTR), `Click` (request id, time), `Example` (impression plus label and label wait), `ModelVersion` (weights, calibration map, keep rate, status).

**API.** `POST /score` takes a user context and ad ids and returns one calibrated pCTR per ad plus the model version. `GET /calibration?segment=...` returns predicted and observed rate for dashboards and the rollout gate. Impressions and clicks flow on streams into joined examples.

## A naive design and where it breaks

A gradient-boosted or logistic model trained nightly on yesterday's clicks. Features are written in SQL for training and again in Java for serving. No-click examples are downsampled so training fits on one machine. A click is labelled when it shows up in the table.

| Break | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Prices drift up | Advertisers see higher cost per click | Dropped no-clicks taught a base rate far above reality |
| Prices drift down on new ads | New ads underbid and rarely win | Late clicks were labelled as misses |
| A feature means two things | Offline wins, online losses | SQL and Java versions differ: training-serving skew (lesson [E1.4](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/training-serving-skew/)) |
| Yesterday's model on today's trend | A noon sale is seen tomorrow | Nightly batch training |
| New ids collide | Ids hashed into one vector | A fixed-size table cannot grow |

The first three rows are all about the probability being wrong, and AUC shows none of them.

## High-level design

Step through the build. Each step names the requirement it meets.

<AiE1AdBuild client:visible />

Four rules.

1. **Calibration is a first-class box** between the raw score and the auction, fitted and monitored per segment.
2. **Log what you predicted.** Each impression stores the features used and the pCTR shown. Without this you cannot rebuild matching training examples or measure calibration.
3. **The label joiner owns time.** How long it waits before calling an impression a non-click trades label accuracy against freshness.
4. **Deploys go through a gate** that reads calibration and revenue, not AUC alone.

**The model shape.** The common deep shape is the **DLRM** (Deep Learning Recommendation Model, from Facebook researchers). Each sparse feature, such as an ad id, gets an **embedding**: a learned vector, a row in a big table looked up by id. Dense numbers such as ad age go through a small network, the **bottom MLP**. The model takes the **dot product of every pair** of these vectors, joins the results with the dense vector, passes them through a **top MLP**, and ends with a **sigmoid** that squeezes the output into 0 to 1 ([Naumov et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/1906.00091v1)). Picture the tables as a phone book of learned profiles and the networks as a small clerk comparing two entries. Sparse ids are the strongest signal in ads, and a plain network cannot digest a million-way category without a table.

## Deep dive 1: calibration under delayed labels and class imbalance

Two things push the pCTR off the truth. They look alike on a dashboard and have different fixes.

### Imbalance and downsampling

**What it is.** Google says typical CTRs are "much lower than 50%", so clicks are rare and "relatively more valuable in learning CTR estimates" ([McMahan et al.](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/41159.pdf)). Training on every no-click is costly, so teams **downsample**: keep every click and a fraction of no-clicks, the keep rate.

**Picture.** One in 50 townspeople owns a boat. You survey every boat owner but only 1 in 10 others. Reporting "the share of boat owners" from that survey gives a number far too high.

**Why it is a problem.** The model sees a world with more clicks than the real one, so its output is a probability for the downsampled world. The auction reads it as a real one.

**Fix 1: correct at serving.** If the model gives q and the keep rate is r, the true estimate is

<Math block tex="p = \frac{q\,r}{q\,r + 1 - q}" />

In words: shrink the odds of a click by the keep rate. Monolith, ByteDance's online-training system, does this: "we apply log odds correction during serving, making sure that the online model is an unbiased estimator of the original distribution" ([Liu et al., Monolith](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.07663v2)). Google instead weights each kept no-click by 1 divided by the keep rate during training, so the loss matches the full data ([McMahan et al.](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/41159.pdf)). Both work. Pick one and document it. Doing both, or neither, is the usual bug.

### Delayed labels

**What it is.** A click can arrive minutes to days after the impression. Monolith's authors: "a user may take a few days before they decide to buy an item they were presented days ago" ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.07663v2)).

**Picture.** A pollster closes the phone lines at noon and reports the vote. Late voters are not counted, so the result looks like a quiet election.

**Why it is a problem.** An impression with no click yet looks like a true no-click. Train on it and the model learns these ads are less clickable than they are. The newest data is most affected, which is what online learning reads first.

**Options.** Wait longer: hold the impression in the joiner until most clicks arrive, at a cost in freshness. Monolith keeps pending features in memory and spills older ones to an on-disk store, so a long wait does not exhaust memory ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.07663v2)). Or emit a no-click early and a corrected positive if a click arrives: faster, but the model briefly trains on wrong labels. The log-odds fix cannot help here, since it never sees clicks that were never labelled.

### Try it

First keep the no-click share low with the fix off. Then turn the fix on. Then add late clicks.

With a low keep rate and no fix, every dot sits far above the diagonal, and the auction overvalues clicks by a large factor. The fix returns the dots to the line. Late clicks push them below it, and the fix does not help. So calibration is monitored from real outcomes, not assumed from a formula. ### The calibration layer

Even with both fixes, models drift. Google describes a **calibration layer** mapping predicted CTR to observed rate. A flexible form is **isotonic regression**, a step-wise rising curve fitted by least squares, which cannot reorder predictions. Google reports the piecewise form "significantly reduced bias for predictions at both the high and low ends of the range" ([McMahan et al.](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/41159.pdf)). A layer fitted on everyone can fix the average and miss small segments. So fit and monitor per segment, with a minimum count before a segment gets its own map.

## Deep dive 2: sparse embedding tables

**What it is.** The DLRM tables hold one vector per id: users, ads, advertisers, pages, cross features. The DLRM authors: embeddings "contribute the majority of the parameters, with several tables each requiring in excess of multiple GBs of memory" ([Naumov et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/1906.00091v1)). Monolith says its production models are "usually several terabytes in size" ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.07663v2)).

**Why it is hard.** Three problems, three answers.

1. **It does not fit on one machine.** Split the tables across machines: **model parallelism**. Keep the small MLPs copied everywhere, each machine on different examples: **data parallelism**. Because the pieces live apart, a step needs an **all-to-all** exchange: each device sends the vectors it looked up to the devices that own the matching examples ([Naumov et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/1906.00091v1)). Our reading: it is database sharding plus a scatter-gather. Lessons [E2.3](/learn/ai/e2-training/data-parallel-zero/) and E2.4 cover the parallelism side.
2. **Ids keep growing.** Hashing ids into a fixed table makes two ids share one vector: a **collision**. Monolith argues that "a small group of users or items have significantly more occurrences", so popular ids collide with rare ones and quality drops. Their answer is a **collisionless** table built on cuckoo hashing, which gives each key its own slot by moving an older key to its alternate slot on a clash ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.07663v2)).
3. **Memory is finite.** So admit and evict. Monolith filters ids "before they are admitted" by how often they occur, since ids that appear a handful of times "have limited contribution", and expires ids after an inactive period. Our reading: this is a cache policy on a learned dictionary, the "do not cache one-hit wonders" rule plus a TTL.

<CrossTrackLink id="05-caching/eviction">
Eviction decides which entry leaves a full cache, and a TTL expires an entry after a fixed time. Both reappear in the embedding table: rare or stale ids are dropped to save memory, with a tunable threshold per table.
</CrossTrackLink>

**Syncing to serving.** Sending a multi-terabyte model whole would "pose huge pressure to both the network bandwidth and memory", the Monolith authors say. So they track a **touched-keys set**, the ids changed since the last sync, and push only those at a "minute-level" interval. Dense layers move slower, so they sync daily ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.07663v2)). The paper also reports that enlarging its snapshot interval to one day gave "nearly no loss of model quality" (a **paper claim**, on ByteDance's data), and frames this as trading reliability for freshness. Our reading: for a fast-decaying model, sync matters more than frequent backups. Test the failure case before you trust it.

## Deep dive 3: feature reads at auction rate, and skew

**What it is.** An online feature store answers "give me these features for this id" quickly (lesson [E1.3](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/features-store/)). The ad-specific part is the load.

**Why it is a bottleneck.** One auction scores many candidates, each needing ad-side features, and the user needs features once. Read load is auction rate times candidates times lookups. More GPUs do not help. A different access pattern does.

- **Batch the reads:** one call per entity type per auction.
- **Cache the slow-moving ones:** ad-side features change rarely, so a local cache with a short TTL absorbs most reads. User features go to the store.
- **Co-locate** hot embeddings and features with the scorer.
- **Degrade, do not fail.** If the store is slow, fall back to a default and flag the request, or use a cheaper model. Never let a feature timeout fail the auction.

**Skew.** Lesson [E1.4](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/training-serving-skew/) taught this. In ads the cure is to **log the served features**: write the exact values used for each impression into the impression log and train from those. Monolith's online joiner does this, joining features with user-action labels on a unique request key ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.07663v2)). A recomputed feature may use data that did not exist at serving time. A logged one cannot.

<Callout type="deep" title="Why a fallback value is dangerous">
When the store times out and you fill a missing feature with zero or the mean, the model scores a different world than it trained on. Its pCTR moves in a way that looks small but is biased for every request that fell back. Track the fallback rate per feature, and route to a safer model past a threshold. A feature outage is a calibration incident that looks like an infrastructure incident.
</Callout>

## Failure modes and an incident drill

| Failure | What happens | Detect | Do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Miscalibration after deploy | Auction misprices ads; cost per click moves | Predicted over observed per segment, with a band | Roll back; refit calibration; add the segment to the gate |
| Feature outage or fallback | Many requests score on defaults | Fallback rate per feature; score shift | Cheaper model or cached features |
| Label pipeline stall | Online model goes stale or trains only on non-clicks | Label lag; positive share against baseline | Pause online updates; serve last good model |
| Delayed-label bias | New ads underpredicted | Predicted over observed by ad age | Longer wait; two-pass labels |
| Embedding collision or eviction burst | Popular ids lose vectors | Table hit rate; sudden score shift | Raise admission threshold carefully; restore snapshot |
| Feedback loop | Model learns only what it shows | Exploration share; coverage of new ads | Small exploration slice with logged propensity |
| Sync mismatch | Sparse and dense parts from different versions | Version tags; score drift | Version both; refuse mismatched parts |

## Drill: ETA prediction

**The task.** A food-delivery app shows "arrives in 32 min" at checkout. Predict delivery time per order. Take 15 minutes.

**The bar.** A **regression** problem (a number, not a probability). Illustrative: late rate (orders after the promise) under a set share, with MAE (mean absolute error) as secondary. A/B by region.

**Why it is not plain regression.** Early by 5 minutes is fine. Late by 5 brings complaints, refunds, and lost repeat orders. A model trained for the lowest MAE aims at the **median**, so half of orders are late by construction. A skewed cost needs a skewed loss.

**The loss.** The **quantile (pinball) loss** charges alpha per minute under the truth and 1 minus alpha per minute over, and its best answer is the alpha-quantile of the outcome ([scikit-learn, model evaluation](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/model_evaluation.html) lists pinball loss as the scoring function for quantiles). If a late minute costs three times an early minute, the best promise is the 75th percentile of delivery time. Uber's DeepETA uses an **asymmetric Huber loss** (smooth for small errors, linear for large ones, so outliers do not dominate): "By varying omega, you can control the relative cost of underprediction vs overprediction" ([Uber Engineering](https://www.uber.com/blog/deepeta-how-uber-predicts-arrival-times/), company claim for the design; no MAE figures are given there).

**The features.** Route distance and travel time from a routing engine, traffic, time, weather, restaurant prep time (often the biggest and noisiest), courier supply, and order size. DeepETA trains a model to "predict the residual between the routing engine ETA and real-world observed outcomes", so the model can change without rebuilding the routing engine, and "must return an ETA within a few milliseconds at most" ([Uber Engineering](https://www.uber.com/blog/deepeta-how-uber-predicts-arrival-times/)). Our reading: prep time and the long tail are why a ride-hailing recipe needs adapting for food.

## Drill: Dynamic pricing as ETA plus CTR

**The task.** A ride or delivery marketplace sets a price per trip. Take 15 minutes. Dynamic pricing is the two models of this lesson, stacked.

- A **demand model**: the chance a user accepts at price p. This is a calibrated probability, like pCTR. If it says 60% accept where 40% do, prices rise past the level that kills demand.
- A **supply and time model**: an ETA. A high price with a long wait loses the user anyway.
- An **optimizer** that picks the price maximizing price times accept probability, minus the cost of delays: the auction's multiplication with a price in place of a bid.

**The bar** is revenue per session, plus guardrails, which are the reason this is a drill and not a copy of ads.

- **Caps and smoothing.** A maximum multiplier and a limit on how fast price moves.
- **Fairness checks.** A model can learn a proxy (an area code that tracks income) and charge more to people with fewer options. Compare price and accept rate across slices before and after launch.
- **Emergency rules.** A kill switch holding prices at base during a disaster or outage. The model trained on normal days.
- **Experiment hygiene.** Users in an area share supply, so price tests leak across arms. Randomize by region and time block, and measure supply too.

**The failure.** A demand model trained during a promotion has an accept probability too high after it ends, so prices rise too far and revenue falls. The cure is the ads monitor: predicted over observed accept rate per segment, with a band. This section is design reasoning, not a company claim, and I give no price figures.

| Deploys | "A/B test it." | Shadow, then canary gated on calibration and cost per click, one-switch rollback |

## What experienced teams know

1. **Calibration lets the auction and the model be built separately.** Google says well-calibrated predictions allow a "loosely coupled" design ([McMahan et al.](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/41159.pdf)). Our reading: when the contract is "a true probability", each team changes its side without a joint release.
2. **Downsampling needs a named correction.** Google weights at training; Monolith corrects log odds at serving ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.07663v2)). Our reading: the mistake is zero or two corrections, or a constant that changed on one side, as in the drill.
3. **The feedback loop limits what you can prove.** See the Google quote in the eval plan. Our reading: treat offline results as a hypothesis, and keep exploration traffic logged with its selection probability.
4. **Sparse ids need their own memory system.** Monolith reports a collisionless table with frequency admission and expiry, at several terabytes per model ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.07663v2), paper claim on ByteDance data). Our reading: size the embedding tier like a database, with growth, eviction, and a restore plan.
5. **Match the loss to the cost of each error.** Uber chose an asymmetric Huber loss with a tunable asymmetry ([Uber Engineering](https://www.uber.com/blog/deepeta-how-uber-predicts-arrival-times/), company claim). Our reading: if you cannot say which error is worse, you have not chosen a loss.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Downsample no-clicks | Smaller, faster training | A correction to keep in sync | Click rate is low and data is large |
| Correct at serving | Training code untouched | Keep rate must travel with the model | One fixed keep rate per model |
| Weight at training | Output already honest | Higher gradient variance | You control the training loop |
| Long label wait | Fewer false no-clicks | Slower learning | Late clicks are common |
| Two-pass labels | Fresh updates | Brief wrong labels | Freshness beats purity |
| Collisionless table | Full signal per id | Memory, a hash-table engine | Many ids, uneven frequency |
| Hashed table | Fixed size, simple | Popular and rare ids share a vector | Small team, quality loss tolerable |
| Per-segment calibration | Honest where money is | More maps to fit and monitor | Segments carry money |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "The pCTR is a price input, so my bar is logloss and calibration per segment, not AUC. I would use a DLRM-shaped model with sharded embedding tables. I downsample no-clicks, correct in one named place, and store the keep rate in the model version. I choose the label wait as freshness against bias, and log served features to avoid skew. A new model goes shadow, then canary, gated on predicted over observed rate and advertiser cost per click, with one-switch rollback." Then name the exploration slice and the feedback loop.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not report AUC as the quality of an ad model. Do not downsample and forget the correction, or correct twice. Do not label every unclicked impression a no-click at once. Do not recompute features for training that you could have logged. Do not judge an ETA model by MAE alone when late costs more than early.
</Callout>

## Further reading

- [Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches (Google)](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/41159.pdf): subsampling with weights, calibration layers, and what did not work.
- [Deep Learning Recommendation Model for Personalization and Recommendation Systems (Meta)](https://arxiv.org/html/1906.00091v1): the DLRM shape and its parallel scheme.
- [Monolith: Real Time Recommendation System With Collisionless Embedding Table (ByteDance)](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.07663v2): collisionless tables, online joiner, minute-level sync.
- [DeepETA: How Uber Predicts Arrival Times (Uber)](https://www.uber.com/blog/deepeta-how-uber-predicts-arrival-times/): post-processing a routing ETA with an asymmetric loss.
- [scikit-learn: probability calibration](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html): reliability curves and isotonic regression.
