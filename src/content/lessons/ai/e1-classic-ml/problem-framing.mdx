## The problem

A product manager says: "We want people to enjoy the video feed." An engineer opens a notebook and asks: "What do I put in the loss function?"

Nothing in the first sentence tells you. "Enjoy" is not a column in any table. A **loss function** is the number a training job tries to make smaller. It needs one number per training example, and "enjoy" is not that.

So someone must translate. They must turn a goal about people into a number a computer can improve. This lesson is about that translation, and about how it goes wrong. It is the step that decides whether the rest of an ML system (data, features, serving, monitoring) is aimed at the right target.

The [AI Design Method](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/ai-design-method/) lesson gave you a six-step design method with a quality bar at the start. This elective module covers classic ML systems: ranking, recommendation, fraud, and ads. Here we zoom into the first part of that method for this kind of system.

This lesson answers five questions:

1. Should you use ML at all?
2. What is the chain from goal to model?
3. What is a proxy, and why does it drift?
4. How do you see the drift early?
5. How do you pick the first proxy?

## Should you use ML at all?

The first answer is often "no, not yet". Google's "Rules of Machine Learning" is a long list of advice from engineers who ran ML in production. Its Rule 1 says: "Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning" ([Rules of ML](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/guides/rules-of-ml)). A hand-written rule can ship a first version before you have any training data.

Here is a picture. A new shop has no sales history. The owner puts the most popular items from last year's market stall at the front. That is a **heuristic**: a simple rule of thumb. It is cheap, it is easy to explain, and it works on day one.

Why start there? Because ML needs data, and data comes from a running product. A model trained on guesses learns the guesses. The heuristic gives you three things the model needs: users, logs, and a baseline to beat.

Rule 3 gives the other half: "Choose machine learning over a complex heuristic." After a while the rule set becomes a tangle. Every new case adds an exception, and nobody dares touch it. At that point a learned model is easier to update, because you retrain it on new data instead of rewriting rules.

For example (illustrative), a payments team might start with "block any card used in two countries within one hour". That rule ships in a day. After a year it has 300 exceptions. The data is now there, and the rules are a burden. That is the moment for ML.

So the order is: rule first, ML when the data exists and the rules are too tangled. If you cannot explain why a rule is not enough, you are not ready to frame an ML problem.

## The chain from goal to model

Suppose you do need ML. Chip Huyen's book on machine learning systems makes the key point plainly: "most businesses don't care about ML metrics unless they can move business metrics", and business goals "need to be translated into ML objectives" (chapter 2 of the [book's summary](https://github.com/chiphuyen/dmls-book/blob/main/summary.md)).

That translation has links. Walk down them one at a time.

**1. The business goal.** A sentence about people or money. "People find videos worth their time." "Lose less money to fraud."

**2. The label.** A **label** is the right answer for one training example, as a number or a category. For a video, it might be "the user watched at least half". You cannot observe "worth their time". You can observe watching, and a label is a thing you can observe. The label is already a stand-in for the goal.

**3. The model task.** The kind of question the model answers. Examples: "yes or no for this user and this video" (**binary classification**), "a number such as minutes" (**regression**), or "which items first" (**ranking**). The label decides which task fits.

**4. The metric.** A **metric** is a number you report to judge the model. The Rules of ML make a useful split. A metric is "a number that you care about", which "may or may not be directly optimized". An **objective** is "a metric that your algorithm is trying to optimize". So the objective is the metric the training job pushes, and the other metrics are ones you watch.

Some metrics you will meet in this module, each defined once here:

- **Click-through rate** (CTR): the share of shown items that get clicked.
- **AUC**: a score from 0.5 to 1 for how well the model puts true positives above true negatives. Pick one random positive and one random negative. AUC is the chance that the model scores the positive higher. 0.5 is a coin flip.
- **Log loss**: a score that punishes confident wrong answers. A model that says "99% sure" and is wrong pays much more than one that says "60% sure".
- **Recall**: of all the true positives (for fraud, the real frauds), the share the model catches.

## What a proxy is, and why it drifts

A **proxy** is a measurable thing used in place of the thing you really care about. A school uses exam scores as a proxy for learning. Most of the time, higher scores mean more learning. But once teachers are judged by scores, they teach to the test. The score goes up and the learning does not.

Why must we use proxies? Because the real goal is slow, vague, or hidden. Satisfaction shows up in a survey that few people answer. Long-term retention takes weeks. A click takes a second and is logged for everyone. So we pick the fast, dense signal.

Why do they drift? Because the model is a strong optimizer. It does not know the goal. It knows only the number you gave it. If there is any way to raise the number without raising the goal, a trained model tends to find it. The more you optimize, the more likely the model finds the gap.

This is sometimes called **Goodhart's law**: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. You do not need the name. You need the habit of asking, for every proxy, "how could a model raise this while the goal falls?"

Here is the same story in words. A news feed ships a model trained on clicks. In the first week clicks rise, and the dashboard is green. The model has found that sensational titles earn clicks. It shows more of them. Readers click, find the article thin, and feel a little worse. A survey two weeks later shows lower satisfaction. The click dashboard said "success" the whole time.

Notice what did not happen. Nobody made a bug. The code did exactly what it was told. This is why the fix is not "test harder". It is to design the chain with its failure in view.

## Three ways proxies fail

Three patterns cover most cases. They give you a quick checklist for any new chain.

**The proxy is easier to game than the goal.** Clicks are the example above. A model can raise clicks with titles that mislead. Rule of thumb: if a human could cheat the metric cheaply, a model will find a way.

**The proxy lags the goal.** Fraud labels are the example. A cardholder disputes a payment days or weeks after it happened. The [Data and Labels](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/data-labels/) lesson covers this. A model trained on the labels you have today learns last month's fraud, and attackers have moved on.

**The proxy measures a part, not the whole.** Watch time is the example. It is a good sign of interest, but a slow, long video earns more watch time than a short, sharp one that people loved. Total watch time can rise while the time feels wasted.

When you pick a proxy, say which of the three could bite you. Then pair the proxy with a second metric that would catch it. That second metric is a **guardrail**: a number that must not get worse when you ship.

## See the drift early

You cannot wait for the long-term goal to show up. You need an early alarm. There are three cheap tools.

**A guardrail metric.** Alongside clicks, watch a signal that moves the opposite way when the proxy is gamed. For a click-trained feed, that could be the share of sessions that end within ten seconds of a click, or the rate of "not interested" taps. The Rules of ML warn that launch decisions weigh several metrics: "the only easy launch decisions are when all metrics get better (or at least do not get worse)" (Rule 39). If one goes the wrong way, you stop and ask why.

**A small survey or a human review.** A few hundred sampled sessions rated by people ("was this worth your time?") cost little. They are slow and noisy, but they are tied to the goal and not to the proxy. Use them as a check on the proxy, not as the training signal.

**A holdout.** Keep a small share of users on the old behavior, so you can compare them on the slow metric over weeks. The [Online Evals and A/B for LLM Features](/learn/ai/a8-evals/online-evals-ab/) lesson covers online tests for LLM features. The [A/B Testing Models](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/ab-testing-models/) lesson covers them for models like these.

Now state the habit. Every time you pick a proxy, write down three things: what it measures, what slow metric it stands for, and what second metric will warn you when they split.

## Picking the first proxy

You still have to pick something. A good first proxy has four properties.

- **You can observe it for most examples.** A label that exists for 2% of users gives a thin, biased training set.
- **It arrives fast enough.** If it takes six weeks, you cannot iterate.
- **It moves with the goal in the cases you know.** Check by reading real examples, not only charts. Sort the top 50 items the model likes. Do they look like what you want?
- **It is hard to game.** Prefer a signal that costs the user something. A purchase is harder to fake than a click. A completed video is harder to fake than a started one.

Rule 25 gives another warning: "When choosing models, utilitarian performance trumps predictive power." In plain words, the best model by score is not always the best model for the product. What matters is what the predictions are used for.

And Rule 4 says: "Keep the first model simple and get the infrastructure right." Begin with a simple model and a simple proxy. Rule 14 adds that starting with an interpretable model, such as logistic regression, makes debugging easier. If something goes wrong with a simple model, you can find out why. You can swap the proxy later. You cannot easily fix a pipeline you never understood.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Launch with a heuristic first | Ships now, no data needed, easy to explain | Rules pile up and get brittle | You have no logs yet |
| Move to ML | Learns from data, easy to retrain | Needs data, labels, serving, monitoring | Rules are tangled and data exists |
| Fast proxy (click) | Dense, quick signal, cheap labels | Easy to game | You need quick iteration |
| Slow metric (retention, survey) | Close to the real goal | Slow, sparse, noisy | As a guardrail or a periodic check |
| Single objective | Simple to train and explain | One blind spot | First version |
| Several metrics with guardrails | Catches proxy gaming | More to measure and argue over | Anything with money or trust at stake |

## What experienced teams know

1. **Ship a heuristic first.** Google's [Rules of ML](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/guides/rules-of-ml), Rule 1: "Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning." The heuristic gives you the data and a baseline.
2. **The objective is a proxy by design.** Rule 13 says the ML objective should be easy to measure and a proxy for the true objective. Assume the gap exists and plan to measure it.
3. **Business metrics are the real exam.** Huyen's [summary of chapter 2](https://github.com/chiphuyen/dmls-book/blob/main/summary.md) says most businesses do not care about ML metrics unless they move business metrics. A model score that does not move the business number is not a win.
4. **Many metrics, one launch decision.** Rule 39 says easy launch decisions are those where all metrics improve or at least do not get worse. Write down the guardrails before the experiment, not after.
5. **Start simple.** Rule 4: keep the first model simple and get the infrastructure right. A framing mistake is easier to find in a model you can read.
6. **Watch for silent failures.** Rule 10 warns that a stale input table can lower quality slowly with no error. A proxy can fail the same way: quietly, over weeks. The [Drift and Model Aging](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/drift-monitoring/) lesson returns to this.

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "First I would ask if we need ML at all. A rule can ship now and give us data. If we do use ML, I write the chain: goal, label, task, metric. Then I name the proxy gap and a guardrail that would catch it. For a feed, the goal is satisfaction, the label might be a long watch, the proxy is watch time, and the guardrail is a survey plus 28-day retention." That shows that you separate the goal from the loss.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "we will maximize clicks" and stop. An interviewer will ask what happens when the model learns clickbait. Do not name only one metric. And do not skip the heuristic baseline: a design that cannot say why ML beats a simple rule has not earned its complexity.
</Callout>
