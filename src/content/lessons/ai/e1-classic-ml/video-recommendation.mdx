## The problem

A video app has millions of videos and one screen. The viewer opens the app and expects the right twenty videos in the time it takes to blink. Nobody typed a query. The system must guess.

The first guess is easy: show what many people clicked. It works for a week. Then a thumbnail with a shocked face and a false promise climbs the chart. People click it, watch ten seconds, and leave. The click counter goes up. The viewer is worse off. YouTube's paper says it plainly: "Ranking by click-through rate often promotes deceptive videos that the user does not complete ('clickbait') whereas watch time better captures engagement" ([Covington and others, YouTube recommendations paper](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/45530.pdf)).

So there are two hard parts. The first is speed: millions of videos, a few hundred milliseconds. The [Two-Tower Retrieval and the Ranking Cascade](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/retrieval-ranking/) lesson taught the answer, a cascade that goes from cheap to heavy. The second is the target: what number should the system push up? A wrong target does damage quietly, and a dashboard shows it as a win.

This lesson answers five questions:

1. What is the quality bar, when clicks are the wrong target?
2. How do we test that bar, and where does the time budget go?
3. How do we build the stages and keep video vectors fresh?
4. How do we rank for more than one goal, and how do new videos get a first audience?
5. Why is the log of past views biased, and how do we measure long-term effects?

Two drills reuse the ideas: search ranking, and People You May Know.

## Requirements and the quality bar

The product is the home feed of a video app. All traffic figures here are **illustrative**.

**Functional.** FR1: return about twenty videos for a viewer, and more on scroll. FR2: rank for the viewer's satisfaction, not clicks. FR3: keep the feed diverse and safe (no banned videos, no single creator filling the screen). FR4: give new videos and creators a fair first audience.

**Out of scope.** Upload and playback (the YouTube lesson), ads in the feed (the [Capstone: Ad Click Prediction](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/ad-click-prediction/) lesson), the search box (a drill below), and creator payouts.

**The target.** A product goal is not a loss function (the [From Business Goal to ML Objective](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/problem-framing/) lesson). "Long-term satisfaction" cannot be computed from one log line, so we use a chain of proxies and say what each one misses.

| Level | Signal | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| Training label | A good watch: a large share watched, or a like | A viewer can finish a bad video because autoplay ran out the timer |
| Online metric | Watch time per viewer per day | Rewards addictive content and long videos |
| Guard metrics | Satisfaction survey, complaints, creator reach, diversity | The survey is sparse; it asks only a few percent of viewers |
| Real goal | The viewer is glad to use the app a month later | Only 28-day retention comes close |

**The bar** (our choice, not a source): watch time per viewer goes up, the survey does not go down, and 28-day retention is not down. "Not down" means the change is not below zero by more than the error bars that the holdout in deep dive 3 measures. Google's Rules of ML say the same: "Launch decisions are a proxy for long-term product goals", and "The only easy launch decisions are when all metrics get better (or at least do not get worse)" ([Rules of Machine Learning, Rule 39](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/guides/rules-of-ml)). Any guard can block a release, not only the main number.

## Eval plan

The eval comes before the design (the [AI Design Method](/learn/ai/a0-orientation/ai-design-method/) lesson). Each stage fails differently, so each gets its own check.

**Offline, per stage.** Candidate generation is judged by **recall at k**: of the videos the viewer later watched, what share did the stage return in its k results? A video this stage misses cannot be recovered later. The ranker is judged by **NDCG** (normalized discounted cumulative gain), a score for list order: each video has a relevance score, lower positions count for less, and the sum is divided by the best possible sum, so a perfect order scores 1. The ranker is also judged by **calibration**: when it says 30% chance of a good watch, about 30% of such videos should get one.

**A split that does not leak.** Split the log by time, not at random. The YouTube paper says that holding out a random watch and predicting it from the other items "leaks future information", so it rolls back each viewer's history and uses only earlier actions. The same paper warns that "live A/B results are not always correlated with offline experiments." Offline is a filter that removes bad ideas. It does not pick the winner.

**Online.** An A/B test by viewer (the [A/B Testing Models](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/ab-testing-models/) lesson; the AI version is [Online Evals and A/B](/learn/ai/a8-evals/online-evals-ab/)) with one primary metric, the guards above, and a **long-term holdout**: a small group kept on an old frozen system for months.

**The slice that matters.** New viewers, new videos, and heavy viewers. An average hides all three.

## Budgets

**Latency.** The [Two-Tower Retrieval and the Ranking Cascade](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/retrieval-ranking/) lesson gave the funnel: millions of videos, about a thousand candidates, a hundred, then the screen. The shares below are **illustrative**, for a 300 ms feed call.

Gateway, viewer features, vector lookup, re-rank, and logging each take a small slice: they are key-value reads, fast ANN search, rules, and non-blocking writes. The ranker, a heavy model run once per candidate, takes the largest.

The conclusion: the ranker owns the budget. More candidates or a bigger model comes straight out of every other stage's headroom, so the number of candidates stage one passes is a design knob.

**Dollars.** The cost of a feed is the ranker. Its fleet is sized by candidates scored per second, not viewers per second, because each call scores hundreds of candidates. We have no sourced ranker throughput, since it depends on model size and batch, so measure candidates per second per GPU on your model. One price anchor: [Lambda's pricing page](https://lambda.ai/pricing) lists the H100 SXM at $3.99 per GPU-hour on the 8-GPU instance and $4.29 on the 1-GPU instance, as of 2026-10-28. Two levers cut the bill: fewer candidates per call, and a smaller or distilled ranker. The **embedding refresh job** is the other bill: a batch job that re-encodes every viewer and video. It scales with catalog and viewer count and sets how stale vectors get.

## Core entities and API

**Entities.** `Viewer`, `Video` (creator, age, topic), `Impression` (viewer, video, position, scores, **propensity**, weight version, time), `Watch` (impression id, seconds, like or skip), `FeedConfig` (objective weights, exploration share, version).

**API.** `GET /feed?viewer=...&cursor=...` returns videos with an impression id each. `POST /events` records watch, skip, like, report, "not interested", tied to an impression id. Internal: `POST /embeddings/refresh`, `PUT /feed-config`. Without the impression id you cannot join "what we showed" to "what they did", and deep dive 3 breaks.

## A naive design and where it breaks

One query: the videos with the best click rate in the last day, minus ones this viewer saw.

Clickbait wins, because it has a high click rate and a short watch. Everyone sees the same feed. New videos never start, because no clicks means no rank. The rich get richer, as early exposure collects clicks and the tail starves. One score cannot hold "watch time" and "felt good".

All of these come from one cause: the system optimizes what is easy to count, over data its own choices produced. The rest of the lesson fixes that.

Four rules from the picture.

1. **Two models, two jobs.** The first is cheap and wide: it must not miss a good video. The second is heavy and narrow: it orders a few hundred well. The YouTube paper "is split according to the classic two-stage information retrieval dichotomy" into candidate generation and ranking. RAG uses the same pattern ([ANN Indexes](/learn/ai/a5-rag/ann-indexes/), [Reranking and Query Rewriting](/learn/ai/a5-rag/reranking-rewriting/)).
2. **The ranker is multi-goal.** It outputs several predictions, and one combine step makes a score (deep dive 2).
3. **Rules sit after the models.** Banned videos, creator caps, and diversity are code in re-rank. A model can be tuned to love a topic. A rule cannot be argued with.
4. **The log is a first-class part.** It carries the propensity of each pick, so training and evaluation can correct for what we chose to show.

## Deep dive 1: two-tower candidates and embedding refresh lag

**What it is.** A two-tower model has one network that encodes the viewer into a vector and one that encodes a video; the score is their dot product. The [Two-Tower Retrieval and the Ranking Cascade](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/retrieval-ranking/) lesson built this. Video vectors are computed ahead and stored in an ANN index ([ANN indexes](/learn/ai/a5-rag/ann-indexes/), [vector databases](/learn/backend/10-advanced/vector-databases/)).

**Picture.** A map where every reader and every book has a position. Find the books nearest the reader without checking them all.

**Why the effort goes into the vectors.** At serving time, the YouTube paper says, "the scoring problem reduces to a nearest neighbor search in the dot product space", and "A/B results were not particularly sensitive to the choice of nearest neighbor search algorithm." Our reading: do not over-invest in the ANN algorithm. Invest in what goes into the vectors.

**Example: what goes into the viewer vector.** Two choices from the paper are worth copying. First, **example age** as an input: it "removes an inherent bias towards the past" and "increased the watch time dramatically on recently uploaded videos." Old logs teach that old videos win, so tell the model each example's age and set it to zero at serving. Second, train on all watches, "even those embedded on other sites", not only watches of your own recommendations. Otherwise "it would be very difficult for new content to surface and the recommender would be overly biased towards exploitation."

**The refresh lag.** The viewer vector can be fresh (per request) or batch (daily). Pinterest's PinnerFormer chose batch on purpose: it builds the viewer embedding from a sequence of recent actions and trains it to predict long-term engagement. Its abstract says the design significantly closes the gap between daily batch embeddings and realtime ones, that A/B tests showed improved retention and engagement, and that it has been in production since Fall 2021 ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04507)). The abstract gives no retention figures, so we give none. Our reading: a daily vector trades minute-level freshness for a simple pipeline and a long-run target.

The cost: a viewer who watches ten cooking videos at 9 pm still has the 8 am vector at 9:05. Three covers, in order of cost.

| Approach | Gain | Pay |
|---|---|---|
| Accept the lag | Simplest pipeline | Slow reaction to a new interest |
| Session features in the ranker | The ranker sees this session | A fresh feature read (the [Features and Feature Stores](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/features-store/) lesson) |
| A second candidate source from the session | Candidates follow the session | One more source to run and test |

The ranker fix is usually first: the candidate stage can be a day old while the ranker is minutes old.

**Failure from the batch.** If the refresh job fails, the index serves yesterday's vectors, then the day before's. No error shows. Store the build time in the index and alert when its age passes a limit.

## Deep dive 2: ranking for several goals, exploration, and cold start

**What it is.** The ranker predicts several outcomes per candidate: chance of a click, expected watch time, chance of a like, chance of a "not interested" tap, chance of a good survey answer. A **combine step** turns them into one score by a weighted sum, with weights set by the product team.

**Why.** One label cannot hold the goal. YouTube trains with weighted logistic regression, positives weighted by watch time, and the paper says the ranking objective "is constantly being tuned based on live A/B testing results but is generally a simple function of expected watch time per impression" ([paper](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/45530.pdf)). Our reading: the weights are the lever the product team holds. They must be a logged, versioned config, not a number in code.

**Example.** Video A has a high click chance and low expected watch. Video B has a modest click chance and a long expected watch. Click-heavy weights pick A, watch-heavy weights pick B. The incident drill shows what happens when weights move too far.

**Weight drift.** Each change looks small. After a year the sum may favor clicks again. Log the weight version on every impression, and test every weight change against the guards.

**Exploration.** A new video has no history, so the ranker has no reason to show it. Tastes also change, and the ranker only learns about topics it shows. **Exploration** spends a small share of slots on videos the system is unsure about. Like a restaurant that always cooks the same best seller, it cannot find a better dish by accident. A learning slot is a slot not spent on the best known video, so the question is how big the share should be.

With no exploration, the long-term value is stuck. With a small share, it rises a lot while the short-term value barely moves. At a large share, both fall. The peak is a few percent here, in an **illustrative** world, so do not copy the number. The lesson is the shape: a small cost now, a large gain later, a peak between. Real systems also explore smarter than a coin flip, giving extra exposure where the predicted value is most uncertain.

**Cold start.** A **new video** has no watches: use its content (title, topic, creator history, a video embedding) for a first estimate and the explore slot for real data. A **new viewer** has no history: show broadly popular, region-relevant videos with a higher explore share, ask a few interest questions at signup, and switch to personal vectors as watches arrive.

## Deep dive 3: feedback loops, biased logs, and long-term metrics

**What it is.** A **feedback loop** is when a system's output changes its own future input. The recommender shows videos, viewers watch what is shown, and that log trains the next model. The model learns from a world it built. Picture a teacher who tests only the front row and concludes the front row is the best class.

**Why it matters.** A video never shown has no clicks, and that does not mean nobody wants it. Position makes it worse. Google's rules: "The position of content dramatically affects how likely the user is to interact with it", so "Avoid feedback loops with positional features" ([Rule 36](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/guides/rules-of-ml)). The search drill shows the effect in a picture.

**What to log.** Four fields on every impression: position and page; model scores and weight version; the **propensity** (the chance the system had of showing this video in this slot, known exactly for a randomly drawn explore slot); and the viewer's group (test arm or holdout). With propensity you can weight each example by the inverse of its chance, so unlikely picks count more. Without it you can only guess.

**A randomized slice.** Keep a tiny share of impressions chosen at random, or at random within a band of ranker scores. It is poor for those viewers, so keep it small. It gives unbiased data for measuring position effects and evaluating new models. Our reading: this is the explore slot used twice, once to learn and once to measure honestly.

**Long-term metrics and the holdout.** A two-week A/B measures two weeks. The harm from a clickbait feed shows later, in who comes back. A **long-term holdout** is a small, fixed random group (a share such as 1% is a design choice, not a standard) kept on an older version, with a rule on how old it may get. Every new version is compared with it on 28-day retention and the survey.

Size it from the effect you need to see (the [A/B Testing Models](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/ab-testing-models/) lesson): bigger costs those viewers an older product, smaller gives wide error bars. Two rules keep it honest: assign viewers once by a stable hash of the viewer id, and never ship to everyone except the holdout without a recorded exception.

## Failure modes and an incident drill

| Failure | What happens | Detect | Do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Popularity collapse | Same head videos for everyone | Impression share of top 1% of videos; creator reach | Raise exploration; diversity term in re-rank |
| Failed embedding batch | Stale vectors, no error | Index build age; recall on new videos | Block the swap; alert on age; keep last good index |
| Feature outage | Ranker gets defaults | Coverage per feature (the [Drift and Model Aging](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/drift-monitoring/) lesson) | Fail to a simple ranker, not random order |
| Weight drift | Objective moves to clicks | Weight version per impression; guards | Guard-metric A/B for weights; revert |
| Cold-start starvation | New videos get no views | Time to first 1,000 views | Raise explore share for new videos |
| Feedback narrowing | Feed narrows each retrain | Topic entropy per viewer | Random slice; propensity weights |
| Candidate misses | Good videos never reach the ranker | Recall at k on held-out watches | More sources; raise k |

## Drill: Search ranking

**The task.** A user types a query into the app's search box. Return ten good results in under 200 ms. Take 15 minutes.

**Bar.** Illustrative: NDCG at 10 offline; online, more users find something to watch from the first page. Guards: share of queries with no click, and share of reformulated queries.

**Design.** The same funnel as the feed, with a query in place of a viewer.

1. **Query understanding.** Fix spelling, detect language and intent (video, creator, or topic). Small models and rules.
2. **Retrieval.** A keyword index for exact words and a vector index for meaning, merged as in [hybrid search](/learn/ai/a5-rag/hybrid-rrf/).
3. **Learning to rank.** A model scores each candidate for this query and viewer, using text match, video quality, freshness, and past behavior. The loss compares pairs or lists.
4. **Re-rank.** Policy, diversity, business rules.

The latency split is **illustrative** (query understanding 15 ms, retrieval 50, learning to rank 60, re-rank 15, network and logging 60). As in the feed, the heavy model gets the largest slice and the rest must be cheap.

**Position bias in click logs** is the new idea. Training data is clicks, and clicks depend on where a result sat. Joachims and colleagues propose a propensity-weighted method to learn despite this ([Unbiased Learning-to-Rank with Biased Feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.04468)).

With the bias at 1, scoring by raw click rate barely improves the new ranking, because the old order made good results look bad. The divided score brings back the true order. With the bias at 0, the problem goes away, which is why a randomized slice helps.

How real teams cope. Airbnb adds position as a training feature. A straightforward version cost about 1.3% NDCG, since the model leaned on the feature, which is set to 0 at scoring. So they apply dropout to it: during training, they randomly set position to 0 ([Haldar and others, Improving Deep Learning for Airbnb Search](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.05515)). Google's Rule 36 points the same way.

**Evaluation: NDCG offline, interleaving online.** Relevance labels come from raters or from clicks (biased, as shown). In an A/B test each viewer sees one ranker. **Interleaving** mixes two rankers' results into one list for the same viewer and credits each ranker with clicks on its own results, like two chefs sharing one tasting plate. A study on two major search engines found interleaving was in nearly all comparisons "much more sensitive" than the other online measures it tested ([Chapelle and others, 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/people/tj/publications/chapelle_etal_12a.pdf)). Our reading: it is a fast filter for many ranker ideas. The launch call still needs an A/B with guards, since interleaving measures list preference, not retention (the [A/B Testing Models](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/ab-testing-models/) lesson).

A new ranker trained on raw click logs can show a better offline NDCG and no live win. The cause is that both the training labels and the test labels come from the old ranker. The cure is to log propensity, add a randomized slice, and handle position in training.

## Drill: People You May Know

**The task.** Suggest other members a person might connect with. Take 15 minutes.

**Bar.** Illustrative: accept rate (share of shown suggestions that become connections), with guards on hides ("do not show this person") and reports. Recall at k for candidates, AUC with calibration for the ranker.

**Design.** LinkedIn describes its own system as a multi-stage funnel. The first stage ("L0") picks "a few thousand candidates" from billions, judged by recall at k. A light ranker (L1) cuts that to "a select few hundred". A rich ranker (L2) predicts events such as invitations sent and accepted. A final re-ranker applies fairness and diversity, and avoids "overrepresenting platform power users" ([LinkedIn Engineering](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/recommendations/building-a-large-scale-recommendation-system-people-you-may-know)). Its candidate sources include graph walks, embedding retrieval, and simple rules such as new members in your area. The post gives no latency figures, so we give none.

**Why hops explode.** A **hop** is one step along a connection: one hop is your connections, two hops are friends of friends. The count multiplies by about the average connection count at each step.

Set 200 connections: the two-hop count passes the ranker's budget and three hops reach the millions. So the usual answer is two hops with a rule such as "at least two mutual connections", a sample, or a cheap first score. Our reading: the mutual count is both a filter and a strong feature.

**Cold start.** A new member has no graph. Use contacts they chose to upload, the same employer or school, and region.

**Privacy and fairness.** A suggestion reveals something: it says the system thinks two people are linked. Our reading, not from the LinkedIn post: treat these as hard rules, not model features. Never use a signal the member did not agree to for this use. Honor blocks and "do not suggest" lists at every stage. Fairness gets its own stage and metrics.

**Feedback.** Accept is positive, ignore is weak (it may mean "not now"), hide is a strong negative. The post does not say how these are used, so this is our design: a hide is a lasting rule for that pair, not only a training example a model may forget.

## What experienced teams know

1. **Clicks are not the target.** YouTube says click ranking "often promotes deceptive videos" and uses expected watch time ([paper](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/45530.pdf)). Our reading: pick the target from what you want in a month, then add guards for what it misses.
2. **Offline is a filter, not a verdict.** The same paper says the surrogate problem has "outsized importance on performance in A/B testing" but is "very difficult to measure with offline experiments". Our reading: budget for online tests from the start.
3. **Train on labels you did not choose.** The paper trains on all watches, including other sources, so new content can surface. Our reading: any such source protects against your own feedback loop.
4. **Position is a feature you control.** Airbnb's naive position feature cost about 1.3% NDCG and dropout fixed it ([Airbnb paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.05515)); Rule 36 says to train with position and keep it out of serving. Our reading: log position on day one, since you cannot add it to old logs.
5. **Batch embeddings can be deliberate.** PinnerFormer is built for daily batch embeddings and long-term engagement ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04507)). Our reading: a daily vector plus fresh ranker features is a common split.
6. **Re-ranking is where policy lives.** LinkedIn's final stage handles fairness, diversity, and power-user overexposure ([LinkedIn Engineering](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/recommendations/building-a-large-scale-recommendation-system-people-you-may-know)). Our reading: keep policy outside the learned models, where it can be tested and audited.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rank by watch time | Resists clickbait | Rewards long, addictive videos | Always, with survey and retention guards |
| Daily batch viewer vectors | Simple, cheap | Slow reaction to new interest | Taste changes slowly; ranker reads session features |
| Higher exploration share | More learning, fairer to creators | Weaker feed today | Cold start is a big share of traffic |
| Long-term holdout | Sees slow harm | Holdout viewers get an older product | A metric moves over weeks |
| Interleaving | Fast signal, fewer viewers | Measures list preference, not retention | Screening many ranker ideas |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "My bar is watch time with a survey guard and 28-day retention not down. I build a funnel: a two-tower candidate stage with a recall bar, a multi-goal ranker, and a re-rank for policy and diversity. Viewer vectors refresh daily and the ranker reads session features. New videos get an explore budget, and I log position and propensity for every impression. I test offline by stage, online by A/B with guards, and keep a long-term holdout. If CTR is up and retention is down, I suspect the objective weights first and roll back the config."
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not name clicks as the metric and stop. Do not say "retrain daily" as the answer to a stale or biased feed. Do not train and test on the same old-ranker click logs without naming their bias. Do not skip cold start for new videos. Do not present an exploration or holdout size as a fact: it is a design choice set from a measured effect size.
</Callout>
