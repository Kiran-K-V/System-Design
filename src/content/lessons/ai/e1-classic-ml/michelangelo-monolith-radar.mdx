The sources are three primary documents, all read on 2026-10-28. The first is Uber's engineering post "Meet Michelangelo: Uber's Machine Learning Platform" by Mike Del Balso and Jeremy Hermann (2017-09-05). The second is the ByteDance paper "Monolith: Real Time Recommendation System With Collisionless Embedding Table" (Liu et al., presented at an online-recommender workshop in 2022). The third is "How we built it: Stripe Radar" by Ryan Drapeau (2023-03-29). Every claim from a source is marked **Company claim** (or **Paper claim**). Every inference of ours is marked **Our reading**. A figure that a source does not state is "not published", and we do not fill it in. One Stripe documentation page adds detail where noted.

## What they built and why it was hard

Three companies, three different pains, one shared job: keep a model useful after the first launch.

- **Uber, 2017.** Company claim: Michelangelo is "Uber's internal ML-as-a-service platform" and had been serving production use cases for about a year, with dozens of teams building and deploying models on it. The hard part was not one model. It was that every team did everything alone.
- **ByteDance, 2022.** Paper claim: recommendation data is mostly sparse categorical features, such as user and item IDs, and user interests shift fast ("concept drift"). Both facts clash with general-purpose training frameworks.
- **Stripe, 2023.** Company claim: Radar looks at more than 1,000 characteristics of a payment and decides in under 100 milliseconds whether to let it through, block it, or send it to extra checks. Fraud is roughly 1 in every 1,000 payments. Radar incorrectly blocks 0.1% of the legitimate payments. The [Capstone: Fraud Detection](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/fraud-detection/) lesson builds a fraud scorer of this kind from scratch.

Why read them together? They sit at three points on one line. Uber is about **making many models easy to ship and run**. ByteDance is about **making one model fresh**. Stripe is about **making one model fast to retrain against an opponent**. A reader who has finished lessons [Features and Feature Stores](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/features-store/) to [Online and Continual Learning](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/online-learning/) will find each of those lessons in at least one of them.

## The before

Each source says what it left behind. Step through them.

<AiE1MikeBefore client:visible />

**Uber.** Company claim: before Michelangelo, data scientists used a wide range of tools, and separate engineering teams built one-off systems to serve their models. Training was limited to datasets that fit on a data scientist's desktop. "There was no established path to deploying a model into production", and teams often had to build a custom serving container for each project. Reliable, reproducible pipelines for training and prediction data did not exist.

**Our reading:** this is the state of any company in which ML grows from a few notebooks into dozens of teams. The cost is not one slow project. It is that every team re-solves data access, model storage, serving, and monitoring, and each re-solution has its own bugs. The [Training-Serving Skew](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/training-serving-skew/) lesson names the worst of them: the training code and the serving code compute a feature in two ways.

**ByteDance.** Paper claim: a general framework keeps embeddings in fixed-size dense variables, so a table cannot grow as new users and items appear. The usual workaround is a **hash trick**: map each ID to a slot with a hash function. Collisions follow, and the paper says low-collision hashing "relies on an over-idealistic assumption" that IDs are spread evenly in frequency, when in practice a few users or items dominate. When two IDs share a slot they share a vector, and the paper reports that a collisionless table consistently beat the hashed version on a public movie dataset (collision rates of 7.73% for user IDs and 2.86% for movie IDs under one hash) and on an internal production model.

**Stripe.** Company claim: the model before mid-2022 combined XGBoost with a neural net, a design called "Wide and Deep". The cost was training time. Moving to a neural net alone cut training time by over 85%, to under two hours, and the post says removing XGBoost on its own would have cost about 1.5% recall.

## The design

### Uber: six steps and a shared feature store

Company claim: Michelangelo covers a six-step workflow: manage data, train models, evaluate models, deploy models, make predictions, and monitor predictions. Each step is a piece of shared infrastructure that replaces a one-off piece. Five of them map straight onto an earlier lesson, so here is the map, then the two pieces that matter most.

| Uber step | What the post says | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Manage data | Feature and label pipelines, with a shared feature store | [Features and Feature Stores](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/features-store/) |
| Train | Distributed offline training for trees, linear models, deep nets and more | not covered here |
| Evaluate | A versioned model repository with configuration, data references, and accuracy metrics | the [Offline and Online Metrics](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/offline-online-metrics/) lesson covers the online side |
| Deploy | Offline (batch) or online (a prediction service cluster) | [A/B Testing Models](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/ab-testing-models/) |
| Predict | Raw features pass through a compiled feature expression, then the model scores them | [Training-Serving Skew](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/training-serving-skew/) |
| Monitor | Log predictions, join them to outcomes later, track live accuracy | [Drift and Model Aging](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/drift-monitoring/) |

**The feature store.** Company claim: teams publish a feature to a central store with metadata such as an owner, a description, and an SLA. A model refers to a feature by name and the system does the joins. Offline, Spark and Hive jobs read the data lake. Online, a feature is either **precomputed in batch** and loaded into Cassandra on a schedule (the post's example is a restaurant's average meal prep time over seven days) or **computed near real time** from Kafka by a Samza streaming job (the example is a one-hour average). The post says features are "automatically calculated and updated daily" and that "approximately 10,000 features" sit in the store. It also says that for batch precompute, "the same data and batch pipeline is used for both training and serving".

**The expression language.** Company claim: feature selection and transforms are written in a pure functional language, a subset of Scala, that is part of the model configuration. The post says "the same expressions are applied at training time and at prediction time". Our reading: this is the cure for skew by construction. If the same expression runs on both paths, the two cannot drift apart without someone editing the model configuration, which is a visible change.

**The prediction flow.** Company claim: a request carries raw features. The compiled expression modifies them or fetches more from the feature store. The final vector goes to the model. Several models can run side by side on one cluster, which supports gradual rollouts and A/B tests.

**The monitoring loop.** Company claim: Michelangelo can log predictions, hold back a percentage, and later join them with observed outcomes to produce live accuracy. For regression models it publishes error metrics to a time-series monitor with alerts. This is the delayed-label join of the [Data and Labels](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/data-labels/) lesson made into a service.

### ByteDance: a table that grows, and a model that never stops

Monolith has three parts. Each answers one of the problems above.

**1. A collisionless embedding table.** Paper claim: the table uses **cuckoo hashing**, which keeps two hash tables with different hash functions. Inserting a key may evict and relocate existing keys, with a rehash if a cycle occurs, so no two keys share a slot. A picture: two parking lots and a rule that a car which finds its spot taken moves the owner to the other lot. The why: every ID keeps its own vector. The example: a popular video and a brand-new one no longer share a row. Two controls keep memory in check. IDs are admitted only after a tunable occurrence threshold, with a probabilistic filter that cuts memory further, and IDs **expire** after a set period of inactivity.

**2. A streaming path from action to training example.** Paper claim: Kafka queues carry user actions and features, and a Flink job joins them. Each request has a unique key so an action can find its features, and delayed actions are matched through an on-disk key-value store. The paper applies a log-odds correction at serving to offset negative sampling. This is the label joiner and the downsampling fix that the [Capstone: Ad Click Prediction](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/ad-click-prediction/) lesson teaches.

**3. Two clocks for syncing the model.** Paper claim: training servers push updated parameters to serving servers. **Sparse** parameters (the embedding rows that were touched) go at minute-level intervals. **Dense** parameters (the small network) go once a day, at a quiet time. The reason given: embeddings for active IDs move fast, the dense part moves slowly, and the sparse update is small because only touched keys are sent. The paper estimates about 400 MB per minute for 100,000 updated IDs at 1024 dimensions. Our reading: that figure is a sanity check on the idea, and not a measurement.

**Fault tolerance.** Paper claim: the system snapshots all training parameter servers once a day. The authors first expected to need more frequent snapshots and found a one-day interval caused "nearly no loss of model quality". Their estimate: with a 0.01% daily failure rate per server, 1,000 servers, and 15 million daily active users, about one day of feedback from around 15,000 users is lost every 10 days. They call it acceptable. **Our reading:** a clear case of buying freshness with durability. The authors had a failure model and a number. We would still test a restore before we trust it.

### Stripe: one network, a rules layer, and reasons

Company claim: the team started with simpler models such as logistic regression and moved to deep nets. In mid-2022 it replaced the Wide and Deep ensemble with a neural net only, using a multi-branch design inspired by an image-network family. Feature work stays a major lever, and the team reviews past attacks and meets weekly on dark-web fraud trends. A 10x increase in training data produced significant improvements, and a 100x experiment was in progress. Radar's "risk insights", launched in 2020, show which features contributed to a decline. The post says deep nets are harder to explain than simpler models, a trade-off the team accepted, and that users can send better data and write their own allow or block rules.

The Stripe documentation adds one detail the post lacks. Each payment gets a risk score from 0 to 99. By default, 65 or above is elevated risk and 75 or above is high risk, and high-risk payments are blocked ([Stripe docs, risk evaluation](https://docs.stripe.com/radar/risk-evaluation)).

**Not in the post:** the model's size, the features, the share of training data, and any recall level. The [Capstone: Fraud Detection](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/fraud-detection/) lesson reasons about these from first principles, and so must you.

**What is not published.**

- **Uber:** the features read per prediction, so the feature-store read rate at 250,000 predictions per second cannot be derived. Our reading: if each prediction reads many features the store sees a large multiple of that rate, but the multiple is the missing number. Also not published: which percentile covers the 250,000 figure's latency, and any accuracy gain from the platform.
- **Monolith:** any latency, and the live A/B's baseline. The paper does not say whether the 14.03% to 18.08% gains are relative or absolute, and it notes that AUC moves across days with concept drift. Read it as "online training beat batch on every day tested", not as a size.
- **Radar:** the latency percentile, recall, model size, and what the 1,000 characteristics are.

A note on the 14.03% to 18.08% figure. **Our reading:** it reads as huge, so check the unit before you repeat it. Because the paper gives no baseline and no unit, we do not use it as a number to plan around.

## Our reading

Each point below is an inference. The first three come from reading the sources side by side.

**1. The three latency numbers are not a ranking.**

Uber gives a percentile (P95) for one prediction call and says where the time goes: with features from Cassandra it is under 10 ms, and without it is under 5 ms. Stripe gives a ceiling for a whole payment decision and names no percentile. Monolith gives none. Our reading: do not say "Uber is 20 times faster than Stripe". The two clocks start and stop in different places, and the posts do not say where. Do note one thing both agree on: reads of features are on the critical path. Uber's two figures suggest that a feature read can double a call.

**2. Freshness is a design axis, with a price on each setting.** Michelangelo's features update daily, with near-real-time features for a few cases. Monolith syncs sparse parameters every minute and dense parameters daily. Radar retrains in under two hours and keeps rules for minutes. The [Online and Continual Learning](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/online-learning/) lesson covers online learning. These systems sit on a ladder, and each rung adds work: more moving parts, a stricter label join, and a harder rollback. Our reading: Monolith's design is justified by an ads feed with fast-moving interests, and it would be overkill for a fraud scorer that retrains daily.

**3. "Fewer model types" is a form of speed.** Stripe's gain came from dropping a model family so that one framework and one training loop remained. Uber's gain came from one workflow for many teams. Both traded freedom for a shorter path. Our reading: platform teams make most of their gains by deleting options, and a platform that supports everything tends to keep every team's custom path alive.

**4. The three share a skew-prevention move.** Uber runs the same expressions at training and at prediction. Monolith joins actions to the features that were served for that request. Stripe's explanation tooling depends on the features the model saw. All three tie training to what serving did. The [Training-Serving Skew](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/training-serving-skew/) lesson argues for this and the sources agree.

**What we would test before copying.**

- **Monolith's one-day snapshot claim,** on our own failure rate and our own cost of losing a day of feedback.
- **Whether our traffic needs minute-level sync.** Measure the decay curve (the [Drift and Model Aging](/learn/ai/e1-classic-ml/drift-monitoring/) lesson). If a daily model loses little, the sync pipeline is not worth its upkeep.
- **Whether a single model family meets our recall bar.** Stripe's 1.5% recall figure is a reminder to measure what dropping a model costs.
- **The feature-store read rate,** by counting reads per prediction in our own system, since Uber does not publish it.

## What to copy and what not to

| Idea | Copy it when | Skip it when |
|---|---|---|
| One shared path from feature to prediction (Uber) | More than a few teams ship models and each rebuilds serving | One model, one team |
| Same feature expression for training and serving (Uber) | A skew bug has already cost you a week | You have one engineer and one feature path |
| Collisionless embedding table with admission and expiry (Monolith) | Huge, uneven ID space and a model that is sensitive to collisions | A few thousand IDs, or a model that runs on hashed features without loss |
| Sparse sync every minute, dense daily (Monolith) | Interests move in minutes and you have an online joiner | The decay curve is flat over a day |
| Daily snapshot instead of frequent ones (Monolith) | The cost of a lost day is small and you have tested a restore | A lost day is costly, such as money movement |
| One model family, fast retrain (Stripe) | The opponent adapts, so a short loop is a defense | Retraining is rare and the larger ensemble clearly wins |
| Reason codes next to the score (Stripe) | A person must act on a decline | Scores feed only an internal ranker |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "Michelangelo's lesson is a shared path: one feature definition for training and serving, a model repository, and a prediction log joined to outcomes. Monolith's lesson is that freshness is a design axis: a collisionless table, an online joiner, and minute-level sync for the fast sparse part. Stripe's lesson is that a shorter retrain loop is a defense against an adaptive opponent. I would copy the idea that fits my decay curve, and I would measure before I copy the number." Then say which figures the sources do not publish.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not compare Uber's P95 with Stripe's 100 ms as if they were the same clock. Do not quote the Monolith AUC gain as a size. Do not say "Radar is 99% accurate" or any figure the post does not state. Do not copy minute-level sync before you have measured how fast your model ages. Do not present a vendor figure as a measured fact.
</Callout>
