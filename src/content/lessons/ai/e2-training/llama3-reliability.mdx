The one source is the Llama 3 paper from Meta, "The Llama 3 Herd of Models" (July 2024). We read version 3 on 2026-10-28, in full for the infrastructure and reliability sections (3.3 and 3.4). Every claim from the paper is marked **Paper claim**. Every inference of ours is marked **Our reading**. Lesson [E2.6](/learn/ai/e2-training/failures-at-scale/) taught the general ideas: failures grow with cluster size, detection, spares and restart. This lesson does not repeat them. It reads one real system and asks what it published.

## What they built and why it was hard

Meta trained its largest Llama 3 model, with 405 billion parameters, on up to 16,384 GPUs. **Paper claim**: "Llama 3 405B is trained on up to 16K H100 GPUs, each running at 700W TDP with 80GB HBM3". HBM3 is the fast stacked memory attached to each GPU. The model was "pre-trained using 3.8 × 10^25 FLOPs" on 15.6 trillion text tokens. A **FLOP** is one arithmetic operation on a number. The total is the work budget of the run (the core lesson on [scaling laws](/learn/ai/a1-model-function/scaling-laws/) shows where a budget like this comes from).

Why is this hard to keep running? The paper says: "The complexity and potential failure scenarios of 16K GPU training surpass those of much larger CPU clusters that we have operated. Moreover, the synchronous nature of training makes it less fault-tolerant—a single GPU failure may require a restart of the entire job."

Two jobs have to be done at once. Keep the GPUs busy while they run. And keep them running at all. The paper treats both. The first is measured by **model FLOPs utilization**, or MFU: the share of the chip's peak arithmetic speed that goes into useful model math. The second is **effective training time**, which the paper defines as "the time spent on useful training over the elapsed time" (lesson E2.6). The two multiply. A fast job that is down a third of the time is slow.

## The before

The paper does not describe an earlier Meta design for this problem, so we cannot show a "before" from the source. We can build one from its numbers. This is **Our reading**.

Take the 54-day snapshot the paper reports: 419 unexpected interruptions. Divide time by count and you get one unexpected stop about every 3 hours, around the clock. Now picture the plain design. A job runs. A GPU fails. The job dies. A person gets paged, looks, restarts it. Every stop costs the time it takes a person to notice and act, plus the restart. At that rate a person who needs even an hour per stop would keep the cluster idle a third of the time. At 16,384 GPUs, an hour of idle cluster is a large bill.

So the plain design is not slow, it is impossible at this size. Whatever Meta did had to work without a person for nearly every stop. Step through the data for the snapshot.

## The design

The paper describes four layers of the design. We take them in turn: the machine, the layout of work, the tools that find problems, and the work outside the code.

### The machine

**Paper claim.** Each server "is equipped with eight GPUs and two CPUs", and the eight GPUs are joined by NVLink, the very fast link inside a server. Jobs are scheduled by MAST, "Meta's global-scale training scheduler". A **scheduler** is the program that decides which machines run which job. Storage is Tectonic, Meta's general-purpose distributed file system. The paper gives it "240 PB of storage out of 7,500 servers equipped with SSDs", with "a sustainable throughput of 2 TB/s and a peak throughput of 7 TB/s", and notes that "a major challenge is supporting the highly bursty checkpoint writes that saturate the storage fabric for short durations." A checkpoint, as lesson E2.6 explained, is the saved copy used to restart. The paper adds that each GPU's model state in a checkpoint ranges "from 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU".

The network is a RoCE fabric. **RoCE** (RDMA over Converged Ethernet) lets one machine write straight into another machine's memory over ordinary Ethernet switches, with little work by the CPU. **Paper claim**: the cluster "comprises 24K GPUs connected by a three-layer Clos network". A footnote says "we use only up to 16K of these 24K GPUs for Llama 3 pre-training". A **Clos network** is a set of switch layers wired so that many paths join any two racks. The bottom layer has racks of 16 GPUs. Above it, a pod of 3,072 GPUs has "full bisection bandwidth", which means that no part of the pod is starved when all GPUs talk at once. Eight pods make the cluster, and there "the aggregation layer does not maintain full bisection bandwidth and instead has an oversubscription ratio of 1:7". **Oversubscription** means that the links going up are thinner than the links coming in, so a crowd can block each other. The paper then says the parallelism methods and the scheduler "are all optimized to be aware of network topology, aiming to minimize network communication across pods."

The reliability point: the machine has a shape, and the job has to be placed to fit it.

### The layout of work: four dimensions

The model does not fit on one GPU, so the work is split in four ways at once. Lesson E2.4 teaches each in depth. In one line each. **Tensor parallelism** (TP) splits each weight matrix across GPUs. **Pipeline parallelism** (PP) puts different layers on different GPUs, like stations on a line. **Context parallelism** (CP) splits a very long input among GPUs. **Data parallelism** (DP) keeps full copies of the model that each see different data. The paper's DP is a sharded version, FSDP, that spreads the optimizer state and gradients (lesson E2.3).

**Paper claim.** "GPUs are divided into parallelism groups in the order of [TP, CP, PP, DP]." A GPU's position is a list of four numbers. Pick a GPU in the widget and see who shares each kind of group. The 16-GPU example is the paper's own. Below it, the three real configurations from the paper.

Read the three rows. In the first two, TP is 8, PP is 16, CP is 1, and DP changes from 64 to 128 as the cluster doubles from 8,192 to 16,384 GPUs. MFU slips from 43% to 41%. **Paper claim**: the drop "is due to the lower batch size per DP group needed to keep the global tokens per batch constant during training." In plain words: each step must still use 16 million tokens, so twice the data groups means half the batch each, and the groups spend more of their time waiting for each other. In the third row, the context is 131,072 tokens. CP becomes 16 and DP becomes 8. MFU is 38%.

**Our reading.** The first two rows show that TP is 8, and a server has 8 GPUs, joined by the fast NVLink. The groups are made in the order TP, CP, PP, DP, and TP comes first, so a tensor group likely fills one server and keeps its heavy traffic on the fastest link. We did not find the paper saying this in so many words, so check it before you rely on it. It does match the reason lesson E2.4 gave for why TP stays inside a node. It also means that a GPU failure takes out a whole server's tensor group, and with PP at 16 and CP at 1, the tensor and pipeline groups together span 128 GPUs that hold one slice of the model. A fault in any one of the 128 stalls that slice, and, since the job is synchronous, every other data group waits.

### Tools that find the problem

Detection is where most of the paper's reliability section goes.

**The flight recorder.** **Paper claim**: "We extensively use PyTorch's built-in NCCL flight recorder, a feature that captures collective metadata and stack traces into a ring buffer, and hence allowing us to diagnose hangs and performance issues quickly at scale." NCCL is Nvidia's library that GPUs use to exchange data with each other, and a **collective** is one such exchange among a group, for instance averaging gradients across all GPUs. The paper says it "efficiently record[s] every communication event and the duration of each collective operation, and also automatically dump[s] tracing data on NCCLX watchdog or heartbeat timeout." NCCLX is Meta's own fork of NCCL, "significantly" faster "especially for higher latency networks". A ring buffer is a fixed-size memory that overwrites its oldest entry, as the black box of an aircraft does. Lesson E2.6 explains the idea.

**Switching on more detail live.** **Paper claim**: heavier tracing is enabled "selectively as needed live in production through online configuration changes … without needing a code release or job restart." A restart to turn on debugging would cost the thing you are trying to save.

**Turning a stall into an error.** **Paper claim**: failures of "the remote GPU or NVLink connectivity often manifest as stalled load/store operations within CUDA kernels without returning a clear error code." And: "NCCLX enhances the speed and accuracy of failure detection and localization through a tight co-design with PyTorch". The system "monitors the state of the communication library and automatically times out when such a stall is detected." The paper also says that "stalls due to NVLink failures cannot be completely prevented". So the design does not prevent the stall. It finds it and ends it fast. When a collective fails, the library gives "a snapshot of the failing NCCLX collective's internal state, including finished and pending data transfers between all ranks." A **rank** is the number of one GPU in the job.

**Stragglers.** **Paper claim**: "Even a single straggler can slow down thousands of other GPUs, often appearing as functioning but slow communications." Meta "developed tools to prioritize potentially problematic communications from selected process groups", and "by investigating just a few top suspects, we were usually able to effectively identify the stragglers."

### Recovery and automation

**Paper claim**: "To increase the effective training time, we reduced job startup and checkpointing time, and developed tools for fast diagnosis and problem resolution." And the whole loop worked without people: "significant manual intervention was required only three times during this period, with the rest of issues handled by automation." Maintenance, such as "firmware and Linux kernel upgrades", is automated too, and the paper says it "resulted in at least one training interruption daily".

Note what is claimed and what is not. The sentence says startup and checkpoint time were reduced. It does not say from what or to what.

### Outside the code

Two items in the paper are not about software at all. **Paper claim**: "we noted a diurnal 1-2% throughput variation based on time-of-day. This fluctuation is the result of higher mid-day temperatures impacting GPU dynamic voltage and frequency scaling." That is, a hot afternoon makes the chips slow themselves down. And: "tens of thousands of GPUs may increase or decrease power consumption at the same time, for example, due to all GPUs waiting for checkpointing or collective communications to finish, or the startup or shutdown of the entire training job. When this happens, it can result in instant fluctuations of power consumption across the data center on the order of tens of megawatts, stretching the limits of the power grid."

**Our reading.** A checkpoint is a moment when every GPU waits for storage at once. The paper says that this synchronised wait pulls the power draw down for the whole data centre together. A design choice in the software shows up in the power grid.

## Our reading

**The table counts stops, not lost time.** A faulty GPU is 148 stops. A rare network hang might cost more hours than all of them. The paper gives counts only. Do not rank causes by cost from this table. Rank them by count, and then ask how long each took.

**Two figures in the first row do not agree.** **Our reading**: 148 out of 419 is about 35%, not 30.1%. The other rows match their count divided by 419. The paper's 58.7% for GPU issues matches a count of about 246 in the GPU rows, not the 268 that the printed counts give. So either the first-row count or the first-row share is off, and we cannot tell which from the page. We quote both as printed, we use no derived figure from that row, and we would ask the authors. The totals are unaffected: 466, 47, and 419 all agree with the text.

**What more than 90% implies.** Ten per cent of 54 days is about 130 hours. Spread over 466 interruptions, that is at most about 17 minutes each, and less, since the paper says "higher than 90%". This is a ceiling on the average cost of one stop, if every lost minute came from stops. It tells you the average stop was cheap. It does not say how: detection, replacement, startup, checkpoint load and redone work together fit inside it. It also covers planned stops, so the unplanned ones cost less than that.

**Effective time and MFU multiply.** MFU is the speed while running. Effective time is the share of time running. **Our reading**: a team that quotes 41% MFU and above 90% effective time is describing about 37% or more of peak useful work over the whole snapshot. The two kinds of loss have different cures. A stall-fixing tool raises effective time. A better batch layout raises MFU.

**Hardware dominates the cause list, so the toolbox is hardware-shaped.** With about 78% of unexpected stops tied to hardware, replace-and-resume and diagnose-the-machine tools pay back more than software debugging. Software bugs are still the third row at 54, so the code path is not free of faults. This is a count-based judgment, so it may shift if you weigh by hours lost.

**The snapshot may cover most of the run.** **Our reading**, by order of magnitude: 16,384 GPUs at roughly 400 TFLOPs each do about 6.5 × 10^18 operations a second. A budget of 3.8 × 10^25 FLOPs at that speed takes about two months. So a 54-day snapshot is a large part of the run, not a small sample of it. The paper does not say if it is the whole run, and the speeds changed across the phases, so treat this as a plausibility check.

**What we would test before copying any of it.** Count your own interruptions by cause for a month. Measure each stage of your own loop: detect, replace, load, redo. Check whether your tensor groups fit inside a server, and what one dead GPU removes from a job. Log hang evidence on every time-out. The paper gives the shape of a good design, not the settings.

## What to copy and what not to

| Idea | When it fits | When it does not |
|---|---|---|
| Record every collective in a ring buffer and dump it on time-out | Any multi-node job, where a hang has no error message | Single-node jobs, where logs already show who waited |
| Enable heavier tracing live, without a restart | Long runs, where a restart costs hours | Short jobs that you can rerun in the debug mode |
| Turn stalls into time-outs, then act on them | Jobs on shared fast links, where stalls hide | Never skip: an undetected stall costs all the GPUs |
| Compare ranks to find stragglers | Large jobs, where one slow GPU hides in the average | Small jobs with few ranks, where a plain log is enough |
| Fit tensor groups inside a server | Layouts whose heaviest traffic needs the fastest link | Models small enough for no tensor splitting at all |
| Automate recovery, including maintenance | A stop every few hours | A rare failure that a person can handle |
| Report effective time and MFU separately | Always, since they have different causes | Never combine them in one figure without saying how |
| Copy Meta's cause mix as your own | Never | Your hardware, cooling and software differ: measure your own |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "Meta reported 466 interruptions in 54 days on up to 16K GPUs, and more than 90% effective training time. Of the 419 unexpected, about 78% were hardware. The design is detection and automation: a flight recorder that dumps on time-out, live tracing, stall time-outs, straggler tools, short startup and checkpoint time, and only three manual interventions. I would separate effective time from MFU, and I would ask for time lost per cause, because the paper gives counts."
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "Meta has a 3% failure rate" or quote 466 as the failure count. 47 of the 466 were planned. Do not treat a count of stops as a count of lost hours. Do not copy the cause mix to your own cluster, and do not claim Meta used spare nodes or elastic training: the paper does not say.
</Callout>

## Further reading

- [The Llama 3 Herd of Models](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3): the primary source. The sections to read are "Infrastructure, Scaling, and Efficiency" and the reliability part inside it.
- [Revisiting Reliability in Large-Scale Machine Learning Research Clusters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21680): Meta's later study of two A100 research clusters. It has failure data across 11 months and a model for effective training time. You do not need its full method to use the ideas here.
- [MegaScale: Scaling Large Language Model Training to More Than 10,000 GPUs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15627): a second company's view of stability and diagnosis at similar scale.
