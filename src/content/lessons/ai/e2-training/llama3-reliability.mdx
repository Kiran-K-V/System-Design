The one source is the Llama 3 paper from Meta, "The Llama 3 Herd of Models" (July 2024). We read version 3 on 2026-10-28, in full for the infrastructure and reliability section (3.3). Every claim from the paper is marked **Paper claim**. Every inference of ours is marked **Our reading**. The [Failures at Scale](/learn/ai/e2-training/failures-at-scale/) lesson taught the general ideas: failures grow with cluster size, the four kinds of break, and the detect, replace, reload, redo loop. This lesson does not repeat them. It reads one real system and asks what it published.

## What they built and why it was hard

Meta trained its largest Llama 3 model, with 405 billion parameters, on up to 16,384 GPUs. **Paper claim**: "Llama 3 405B is trained on up to 16K H100 GPUs, each running at 700W TDP with 80GB HBM3". HBM3 is the fast stacked memory attached to each GPU. The model was "pre-trained using 3.8 × 10^25 FLOPs" on 15.6 trillion text tokens. A **FLOP** is one arithmetic operation on a number. The total is the work budget of the run (the core lesson on [scaling laws](/learn/ai/a1-model-function/scaling-laws/) shows where a budget like this comes from).

Why is this hard to keep running? The [Failures at Scale](/learn/ai/e2-training/failures-at-scale/) lesson quoted the paper's answer: training is synchronous, so one GPU failure can restart the whole job. Two jobs have to be done at once. Keep the GPUs busy while they run, and keep them running at all. The first is measured by **model FLOPs utilization**, or MFU: the share of the chip's peak arithmetic speed that goes into useful model math. The second is **effective training time**, the share of elapsed time spent on useful training. The two multiply. A fast job that is down a third of the time is slow.

## The before

The paper does not describe an earlier Meta design for this problem, so we cannot show a "before" from the source. We can build one from its numbers. This is **Our reading**.

Take the 54-day snapshot the paper reports: 419 unexpected interruptions. Divide time by count and you get one unexpected stop about every 3 hours, around the clock. Now picture the plain design. A job runs. A GPU fails. The job dies. A person gets paged, looks, restarts it. At that rate a person who needs even an hour per stop would keep the cluster idle a third of the time. At 16,384 GPUs, an hour of idle cluster is a large bill.

So the plain design is not slow, it is impossible at this size. Whatever Meta did had to work without a person for nearly every stop. Step through the data for the snapshot.

## The design

The paper describes four layers of the design: the machine, the layout of work, the tools that find problems, and the work outside the code.

### The machine

**Paper claim.** Each server "is equipped with eight GPUs and two CPUs", joined by NVLink, the very fast link inside a server. Jobs are scheduled by MAST, "Meta's global-scale training scheduler". A **scheduler** is the program that decides which machines run which job. Storage is Tectonic, Meta's distributed file system: "240 PB of storage out of 7,500 servers equipped with SSDs", with "a sustainable throughput of 2 TB/s and a peak throughput of 7 TB/s". The paper names the hard part: "the highly bursty checkpoint writes that saturate the storage fabric for short durations." A checkpoint is the saved copy used to restart (the [Failures at Scale](/learn/ai/e2-training/failures-at-scale/) lesson).

The network is a RoCE fabric. **RoCE** (RDMA over Converged Ethernet) lets one machine write straight into another machine's memory over ordinary Ethernet switches, with little work by the CPU. **Paper claim**: a 24K-GPU cluster, of which "up to 16K" were used for Llama 3 pre-training. A pod of 3,072 GPUs has "full bisection bandwidth", which means no part of the pod is starved when all GPUs talk at once. Eight pods join through an aggregation layer with "an oversubscription ratio of 1:7": the links going up are thinner than the links coming in, so a crowd can block each other. The paper says the parallelism methods and the scheduler are "optimized to be aware of network topology, aiming to minimize network communication across pods."

The reliability point: the machine has a shape, and the job has to be placed to fit it.

### The layout of work: four dimensions

The model does not fit on one GPU, so the work is split four ways at once: tensor, pipeline, context and data parallelism, all taught in the [Tensor, Pipeline, Sequence, and Expert Parallelism](/learn/ai/e2-training/model-parallelism/) lesson. **Paper claim.** "GPUs are divided into parallelism groups in the order of [TP, CP, PP, DP]." A GPU's position is a list of four numbers. Pick a GPU in the widget and see who shares each kind of group. The 16-GPU example is the paper's own. The [Tensor, Pipeline, Sequence, and Expert Parallelism](/learn/ai/e2-training/model-parallelism/) lesson explains why MFU slips from 43% to 41% when the cluster doubles. The third row shows what long context costs: a 131,072-token input needs CP 16 and DP 8, and MFU is 38%.

**Paper claim**: the innermost dimension "requires the highest network bandwidth and lowest latency, and hence is usually constrained to within the same server." TP is innermost and is 8, and a server has 8 GPUs, so a tensor group fills one server. **Our reading**: one dead GPU therefore removes a whole server's tensor group. With PP at 16 and CP at 1, the tensor and pipeline groups together span 128 GPUs that hold one full copy of the model. A fault in any one of the 128 stalls that copy, and since the job is synchronous, every other data group waits.

### Tools that find the problem

Detection is where most of the paper's reliability section goes. The [Failures at Scale](/learn/ai/e2-training/failures-at-scale/) lesson explained the ideas: a flight recorder, live tracing, watchdog time-outs and comparing ranks to find stragglers. Here is what the paper adds.

**Paper claim**: Meta uses "PyTorch's built-in NCCL flight recorder", which "captures collective metadata and stack traces into a ring buffer", and it "automatically dump[s] tracing data on NCCLX watchdog or heartbeat timeout." NCCL is Nvidia's library that GPUs use to exchange data, and a **collective** is one such exchange among a group. NCCLX is Meta's fork of it, which "significantly improves the performance of NCCL, especially for higher latency networks".

**Paper claim**: "stalls due to NVLink failures cannot be completely prevented", but the system "monitors the state of the communication library and automatically times out when such a stall is detected". When a collective fails, the library gives "a snapshot of the failing NCCLX collective's internal state, including finished and pending data transfers between all ranks." A **rank** is the number of one GPU in the job. So the design does not prevent the stall. It finds it and ends it fast. For stragglers, the paper names no method beyond ranking suspect communications and checking "a few top suspects".

### Recovery and automation

**Paper claim**: the whole loop worked without people: "significant manual intervention was required only three times during this period, with the rest of issues handled by automation." Maintenance such as "firmware and Linux kernel upgrades" is automated too, and "resulted in at least one training interruption daily". The paper says startup and checkpoint time were reduced. It does not say from what or to what.

### Outside the code

Two items in the paper are not about software at all. **Paper claim**: "we noted a diurnal 1-2% throughput variation based on time-of-day. This fluctuation is the result of higher mid-day temperatures impacting GPU dynamic voltage and frequency scaling." That is, a hot afternoon makes the chips slow themselves down. And: "tens of thousands of GPUs may increase or decrease power consumption at the same time, for example, due to all GPUs waiting for checkpointing or collective communications to finish, or the startup or shutdown of the entire training job. When this happens, it can result in instant fluctuations of power consumption across the data center on the order of tens of megawatts, stretching the limits of the power grid."

**Our reading.** A checkpoint is a moment when every GPU waits at once. A design choice in the software shows up in the power grid.

## Our reading

**The table counts stops, not lost time.** A faulty GPU is the biggest row by count. A rare network hang might cost more hours than all of them. Rank causes by count from this table, and then ask how long each took.

**Two figures in the first row do not agree.** **Our reading**: 148 out of 419 is about 35%, not 30.1%. The other rows match their count divided by 419. The paper's 58.7% for GPU issues matches a count of about 246 in the GPU rows, not the 268 that the printed counts give. So either the first-row count or the first-row share is off, and we cannot tell which from the page. We quote both as printed and use no derived figure from that row. The totals are unaffected: 466, 47, and 419 all agree with the text.

**What more than 90% implies.** Ten per cent of 54 days is about 130 hours. Spread over 466 interruptions, that is at most about 17 minutes each, and less, since the paper says "higher than 90%". This is a ceiling on the average cost of one stop, if every lost minute came from stops. It tells you the average stop was cheap. It does not say how. It also covers planned stops, so the unplanned ones cost less than that.

**Effective time and MFU multiply.** **Our reading**: a team that quotes 41% MFU and above 90% effective time is describing about 37% or more of peak useful work over the whole snapshot. The two kinds of loss have different cures. A stall-fixing tool raises effective time. A better batch layout raises MFU.

**Hardware dominates the cause list, so the toolbox is hardware-shaped.** With about 78% of unexpected stops tied to hardware, replace-and-resume and diagnose-the-machine tools pay back more than software debugging. Software bugs are still the third row, so the code path is not free of faults. This is a count-based judgment, so it may shift if you weigh by hours lost.

**What we would test before copying any of it.** Count your own interruptions by cause for a month. Measure each stage of your own loop: detect, replace, load, redo. Check whether your tensor groups fit inside a server, and what one dead GPU removes from a job. Log hang evidence on every time-out. The paper gives the shape of a good design, not the settings.

## What to copy and what not to

| Idea | When it fits | When it does not |
|---|---|---|
| Record every collective in a ring buffer and dump it on time-out | Any multi-node job, where a hang has no error message | Single-node jobs, where logs already show who waited |
| Turn stalls into time-outs, then act on them | Jobs on shared fast links, where stalls hide | Never skip: an undetected stall costs all the GPUs |
| Fit tensor groups inside a server | Layouts whose heaviest traffic needs the fastest link | Models small enough for no tensor splitting at all |
| Automate recovery, including maintenance | A stop every few hours | A rare failure that a person can handle |
| Report effective time and MFU separately | Always, since they have different causes | Never combine them in one figure without saying how |
| Copy Meta's cause mix as your own | Never | Your hardware, cooling and software differ: measure your own |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "Meta reported 466 interruptions in 54 days on up to 16K GPUs, and more than 90% effective training time. Of the 419 unexpected, about 78% were hardware. The design is detection and automation: a flight recorder that dumps on time-out, live tracing, stall time-outs, straggler tools, short startup and checkpoint time, and only three manual interventions. I would separate effective time from MFU, and I would ask for time lost per cause, because the paper gives counts."
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "Meta has a 3% failure rate" or quote 466 as the failure count. 47 of the 466 were planned. Do not treat a count of stops as a count of lost hours. Do not copy the cause mix to your own cluster, and do not claim Meta used spare nodes or elastic training: the paper does not say.
</Callout>

## Further reading

- [The Llama 3 Herd of Models](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3): the primary source. The sections to read are "Infrastructure, Scaling, and Efficiency" and the reliability part inside it.
- [Revisiting Reliability in Large-Scale Machine Learning Research Clusters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21680): Meta's later study of two A100 research clusters. It has failure data across 11 months and a model for effective training time. You do not need its full method to use the ideas here.
- [MegaScale: Scaling Large Language Model Training to More Than 10,000 GPUs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15627): a second company's view of stability and diagnosis at similar scale.
