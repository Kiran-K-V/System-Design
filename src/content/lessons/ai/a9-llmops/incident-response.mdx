## The problem

On Monday, your support lead says: "The bot's answers got worse last week." You open the dashboards. Error rate: normal. Latency: normal. Every request returned status 200. No deploy went out this week.

There is nothing to roll back and nothing to restart. Yet users are right. The answers are worse.

This is a **model regression**: the same product, with no visible failure, gives worse output than before. It is the hardest kind of incident in an LLM system. A crashed server makes noise. A worse answer makes none, because a wrong answer and a right answer are both just text.

This lesson answers five questions:

1. How can an answer get worse when nothing is "broken"?
2. Why did the usual alarms stay quiet?
3. How do you find out that it happened?
4. How do you find what changed?
5. How do you stop the damage, and make sure it does not repeat?

## Question 1: how can an answer get worse when nothing is broken?

Ask a doctor why you feel worse. The doctor asks what changed: a new medicine, a new diet, a new place. The same question works here. An answer comes out of a chain of parts, and a change in any part can make it worse.

Four places can change.

- **The provider.** The company that runs the model changes something on its side. A model update, a new server setting, or a bug.
- **Your harness.** The **harness** is all the code and settings around the model call: the system prompt, default settings such as how hard the model thinks, the tools, and the way you keep history. You change it, so it is on your side.
- **Your data.** The documents your retrieval step finds, or the tool results the model reads.
- **Your traffic.** New users, new topics, longer inputs.

Real cases exist for the first two. You will see them again in this lesson, so here are the facts.

**Anthropic, August to September 2025.** Anthropic's engineering team published a postmortem of three bugs that made Claude's answers worse. A postmortem is a written review of an incident. Each bug had a plain cause.

- *Wrong servers.* A change sent some requests to servers set up for a different context window. The **context window** is how much text a model can read at once. At first 0.8% of requests to one model were affected. In the worst hour, 31 August, it was 16%. About 30% of Claude Code users had at least one message sent to the wrong servers.
- *Odd characters.* A bad setting on the servers made answers contain unexpected characters, such as Thai or Chinese in an English reply.
- *Skipped words.* A compiler bug in the code that picks the next word sometimes left out the best choice.

**Anthropic, March to April 2026.** A second postmortem, for the Claude Code product, found three changes on the product side: a lower default thinking effort, a bug that erased the model's earlier reasoning on every turn, and a system prompt line that limited answer length. Anthropic says the API was not affected. These were harness changes, not model changes.

**OpenAI, April 2025.** OpenAI shipped a GPT-4o update that made ChatGPT too agreeable. OpenAI called the trait sycophancy: praising and agreeing with users even when they are wrong. Its post says the update added a reward signal from users' thumbs-up and thumbs-down data. OpenAI's own page was not reachable when this lesson was written. The quotes here come from [Simon Willison's notes on that post](https://simonwillison.net/2025/May/2/what-we-missed-with-sycophancy/), so treat this account as secondhand.

## Question 2: why did the usual alarms stay quiet?

A backend alarm watches something that fails loudly: errors, timeouts, crashes. A model regression fails quietly. The text is fluent. It is only wrong, or worse, or odd.

Anthropic's postmortem explains why its own checks missed it. It says its validation "ordinarily relies on benchmarks alongside safety evaluations and performance metrics", and its teams "perform spot checks and deploy to small canary groups first". A **canary** is a release shown to a small share of traffic first, like the miner's bird. Then the post says: "The evaluations we ran simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting." And: "we relied too heavily on noisy evaluations".

Two more reasons stand out. Anthropic's privacy rules limit when engineers can read user conversations, so the team could not simply look at bad answers. And each bug "produced different symptoms on different platforms at different rates". The 29 August spike was not tied to the load balancer change at first.

OpenAI's account has the same shape. Offline tests looked good. A/B tests showed that the small number of users who tried the model liked it. Some expert testers said the model "felt" slightly off, and that signal did not stop the launch.

The cost of ignoring this is time. OpenAI rolled back three days after the update (25 April to 28 April, from Simon Willison's account). Anthropic's routing fix took 30 days. In the Claude Code case, the effort default stayed lower for 34 days (4 March to 7 April), and the reasoning bug for 15 days (26 March to 10 April). The prompt line lasted only 4 days (16 April to 20 April).

A model regression is the extreme version of a "gray" failure. Every health check passes, and the product is still wrong.

| | Backend outage | Model regression |
|---|---|---|
| First signal | Alerts on errors or latency | A user, a support lead, or a quality score |
| Cause is usually | A deploy, a dependency, load | A change at the provider, in the harness, or in the data |
| How you know it is fixed | Errors drop to zero | Quality scores recover, and users stop complaining |
| Time to notice | Minutes | Days to weeks, in the cases above |

## The naive way: tune the prompt and hope

The first reaction is to edit the prompt. "Be more careful." "Answer in full sentences." It feels productive. It fails for three reasons.

You do not yet know the cause. If the provider sent some requests to the wrong servers, no prompt will fix that. You would be changing the one part that is fine.

A prompt edit is also a new change on top of an unknown one. Now two things changed, and the trail is muddier.

And without a score, you cannot tell if it helped. You read three answers, they look better, and you ship. A model's output varies from run to run, so three answers prove nothing.

The better move is a runbook. A **runbook** is a written list of steps for a known kind of incident, so people follow it under stress instead of inventing it. It has four parts.

## The real fix: a runbook with four steps

### Idea 1: detect

**What it is.** Detection means turning "someone felt it" into an alarm. Use three signals, from slowest to fastest to set up.

- **Complaints.** Count thumbs-down, support tickets, and bug reports. Split the count by model, platform, and route. Anthropic's post lists user channels such as the `/bug` command and the thumbs-down button. It says users were reporting degraded responses that its evaluations did not catch.
- **Online evals.** An **eval** is a repeatable test that scores answers. An **online eval** scores a sample of real, live traffic, so you measure what users actually get. [Lesson A8.7](/learn/ai/a8-evals/online-evals-ab/) builds them.
- **Symptom monitors.** Cheap checks on each answer: unexpected characters, answer length, refusal rate, schema failures, empty answers, tool errors. Anthropic says it added "detection tests for unexpected character outputs". A check like that takes minutes to write.

**Everyday picture.** A smoke detector and a nose do different jobs. The detector is fast and dumb. The nose catches what the detector was not built for. You need both.

**Why it exists.** Each signal catches what the others miss. A symptom monitor is fast and cheap but only sees what you thought of. Complaints see everything, but late and noisy. Online evals sit in between.

**Number.** A small eval set cannot see a small drop. Say your pass rate is 90%, measured on 100 cases. Chance alone moves the number by about the **standard error**, which is how much a measured rate wobbles from run to run. It is the square root of (0.9 x 0.1 / 100), which is 0.03, or 3 points. Compare two runs, and the wobble in the difference is about 1.4 times larger: 4.2 points. In its 2026 postmortem, Anthropic reports that a broader evaluation showed a 3% drop after a system prompt length limit, combined with other prompt changes. A drop that size is no larger than the noise of a 100-case set. To catch a fall from 90% to 87% with 95% confidence, and a 4 in 5 chance of catching it when it is real, you need about 1,770 cases per version. That is the usual formula for comparing two rates. Run the same formula backwards: with 100 cases, the smallest fall you can reliably see is about (1.96 + 0.84) x 4.2, or 12 points.

Two ways to cut the number. Run both versions on the same inputs, so the difficulty of the inputs cancels out. And sample live traffic, so the count grows each day.

### Idea 2: triage

**What it is.** Triage means finding what changed. Start with the four places from Question 1, and add a fifth: a slice. A **slice** is one part of your traffic, such as one platform, one route, one tenant, or one language.

**Everyday picture.** The doctor again. "What changed?" comes first. Then "where does it hurt?"

**Why it exists.** Guessing wastes hours. If every request logs the same few facts, the question "what changed?" becomes a query. Log these on every call: the model id the provider reports in its reply, the platform, the route, the prompt version ([lesson A9.3](/learn/ai/a9-llmops/versioning/)), the settings such as thinking effort, and the tool and retrieval versions. [Lesson A9.1](/learn/ai/a9-llmops/tracing/) shows how to attach these to a trace, the record of one request.

Anthropic's own case shows the value. Each bug hit different platforms at different rates. With a per-platform breakdown, the pattern is visible. Without one, it looks like random noise.

**Number.** The Anthropic routing bug reached 16% of requests in its worst hour and 0.8% at the start. A slice view shows a spike like that. An average over all traffic shows a smudge.

Try the decision path. Pick what you found.

### Idea 3: mitigate

**What it is.** Mitigation means stopping the damage now, before you know the full cause. Order your options from fastest and safest to slowest.

1. **Roll back.** Return to the last version that worked. This only works if you can: keep the old prompt, the old settings, and the old model available ([lesson A9.3](/learn/ai/a9-llmops/versioning/)).
2. **Route away.** Send traffic to a backup provider or an older pinned model. This needs the backup to have passed your evals already ([lesson A9.5](/learn/ai/a9-llmops/provider-failover/)).
3. **Turn the feature off.** A **kill switch** is a flag that disables one feature without a deploy. The user sees a simpler path.
4. **Hand to a human.** Send the hard cases to a person while you work.

**Everyday picture.** In a fire, you shut off the gas first. You find the cause later.

**Why it exists.** Finding the cause can take days. In the Anthropic case, the first rollback came on 2 September, and the full fix landed over weeks. Users should not wait for the postmortem.

**Number.** Anthropic rolled back one model on 4 September and another on 12 September. Each rollback was a single step. A rollback is also blunt: it can undo a good change that shipped in the same release. Route-away keeps your change but needs its own proof.

One warning. A temporary fix can hide the cause. In December 2024, Anthropic deployed a workaround for a different problem. The postmortem says the workaround "had been inadvertently masking" the real bug, and removing it exposed the bug in August. Write down every mitigation with an owner and a date to remove it.

### Idea 4: learn

**What it is.** Turn the incident into a test, a monitor, and a process change, so the same class of bug cannot return silently.

**Everyday picture.** After a break-in, you fix the lock. You also put a camera at the door.

**Why it exists.** Incidents are the best test cases you will ever get. They are real, and they are the ones your tests missed.

**Number.** Anthropic's 2025 post lists three outputs: detection tests for odd characters, evals that run continuously on production, and faster tools for reading user feedback. Its 2026 post adds process rules: soak periods, a broader eval suite for every system prompt change, gradual rollouts, and gating model-specific changes to their target model. A **soak period** is a time when a change runs at small scale before it goes wide. Add the failing cases to your golden set ([lesson A8.9](/learn/ai/a8-evals/feedback-flywheel/) shows the loop).

A good postmortem is **blameless**: it asks "what let this happen?" and not "who did it?". People then report problems earlier.

The replay cost: 500 x (3,000 x $2 + 500 x $10) / 1,000,000 = 500 x $0.011 = $5.50. Prices are from [Anthropic's pricing page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing). A replay is cheap, so run it at the first sign of trouble.

Name three targets for your own design. Quality bar: a pass rate on the golden set that you write down, for example 0.90. Detection time: the longest you accept between first bad answer and alert, for example one day. Mitigation time: the longest from "confirmed" to "traffic is safe", for example one hour for a rollback. These are your choices. The cases above show what happens when no one chooses.

## Trade-offs

| Mitigation | You gain | You pay | Choose it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roll back your version | Fast, safe, fully in your control | Undoes good changes in the same release | The change is yours and you can name it |
| Route away | Keeps your latest changes | Needs a tested backup, a cold cache, a quality gap | The cause is at the provider |
| Kill switch | Stops the harm at once | The feature is gone for everyone | The harm is severe and the cause is unknown |
| Human fallback | Highest quality for hard cases | Slow, costs people, does not scale | Few cases, high stakes |
| Prompt tweak | Cheap | Adds a new unknown on top of the old one | Only after the cause is known |

## What experienced teams know

1. **Users notice before your evals do.** Anthropic says its evaluations "simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting". Count complaints per model, platform, and route, and treat a rise as an alarm. ([Anthropic postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues))
2. **A 3-point drop is invisible to a small eval set.** The noise on 100 cases is about 3 points, as derived above. Size the set for the smallest drop you must catch, run old and new on the same inputs, and add online sampling. ([Anthropic Claude Code postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem), for the 3% figure)
3. **The regression often lives in your harness.** In 2026 the model and API were fine. A default setting, a caching bug, and a prompt line were the causes. Version your settings and system prompt as part of the release, and log them on every call. ([Anthropic Claude Code postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem))
4. **A workaround can hide the real bug.** A December 2024 workaround masked the top-k bug until it was removed. Record every mitigation with an owner and a removal date. ([Anthropic postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues))
5. **Cheap symptom checks catch whole classes.** After the 2025 odd-character bug, Anthropic added exactly this kind of check: a test for unexpected characters. It is a few lines of code. Add checks for length, refusals, schema failures, and language too. ([Anthropic postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues))
6. **Do not let automated tests overrule a person who says it feels off.** In OpenAI's account, offline tests and A/B tests looked good, while some expert testers felt the model was off. Give one named owner the right to stop a launch, and gate any change that could affect intelligence with a soak period and a gradual rollout. (account via [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/May/2/what-we-missed-with-sycophancy/); process rules from [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem))

A last point on trust. Anthropic's post says: "We never reduce model quality due to demand, time of day, or server load." That is a claim by the provider. You cannot check it from outside. What you can do is measure the quality of your own traffic, which is the point of this lesson.

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say it as four verbs: "Detect with complaints, online evals, and cheap symptom monitors. Triage by what changed: model id, platform, route, prompt version, and settings, all logged per request. Mitigate in order: roll back, route away, kill switch, human fallback. Learn by adding the cases to the golden set and a monitor." Then give one number: a 100-case eval set cannot see a 3-point drop.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not answer "I would improve the prompt". It skips triage and adds a second change. Do not say "our canary passed, so we are safe": Anthropic lists canaries among the checks that missed its bugs. And do not claim zero regressions from a provider's promise. Plan to measure.
</Callout>
