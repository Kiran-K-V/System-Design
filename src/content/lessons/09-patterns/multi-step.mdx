## The problem

A customer places an order. Three things must happen.

1. The order service records the order.
2. The payment service charges the card.
3. The inventory service reserves the item.

Each service owns its own database. That is the point of splitting a system into services: each team changes its data without asking the others. But it means no single database can commit all three changes together.

Now two failures show up.

- **Step 3 fails after step 2 committed.** The card is charged and there is no item to ship. The customer is angry and support must find the charge by hand.
- **The process dies between steps.** Step 2 committed, and the process that should call step 3 is gone. Nobody records that step 3 is still owed.

A **multi-step process** is any business action that crosses more than one system and must end in a sensible state even when a step fails. This lesson shows how to build one from parts you already know.

## Naive solution: one distributed transaction

The first idea is to wrap all three steps in one transaction, using two-phase commit (2PC, [lesson 4.4](/learn/04-data/transactions/)). A coordinator asks every participant to prepare. Each participant writes its change durably, keeps its locks, and votes yes. When all vote yes, the coordinator records "commit" and tells everyone.

Look at the protocol to see why this blocks. After a participant votes yes, it has promised to commit if told to. It may not decide alone, because the coordinator may already have told another participant to commit. So it keeps its locks and waits. If the coordinator crashes after the votes, every participant sits there holding locks until the coordinator comes back and reads its log. Every other request that needs those rows waits too.

That is one cost. There are two more.

- **Every participant must speak the protocol.** A card processor or an email provider will not join your transaction.
- **Every participant must be up.** One slow service holds locks in all the others.

Microservices.io, which documents the saga pattern, states the same conclusion in its forces list: "2PC is not an option."

The second naive idea is to call the three services in order from one request handler, with a retry on each. That fails when the handler dies. Nothing remembers how far the order got. It is the dual-write problem from [lesson 6.5](/learn/06-async/cdc/) with three writes instead of two.

## The mechanism: a saga

A **saga** is a sequence of local transactions. Each step commits in one service's own database. Then it triggers the next step, usually with an event. If a step fails, the saga runs **compensating actions** for the steps that already committed. A compensating action undoes the business effect of a step: a refund undoes a charge.

Four rules make compensation work.

1. **A compensation is a new action, not a rollback.** The charge committed and stays in the history. The refund is a second record. Microservices.io notes that a saga has no automatic rollback: a developer must design each compensation.
2. **Compensations must be retried until they succeed, and they must be idempotent.** A refund that runs twice must refund once ([lesson 3.4](/learn/03-api-design/idempotency/)). A compensation that can fail for good leaves the system broken.
3. **Put steps that cannot be undone last.** You cannot unsend an email. Reserve stock and take payment first. Send the confirmation only after every step has succeeded.
4. **Expect visible middle states.** A saga has no isolation, the "I" in ACID. Between step 2 and the refund, the order is PENDING and the card is charged. Other readers can see that. A status field such as PENDING and CANCELLED makes the state honest, and the UI can show "processing."

## Who drives the saga

Two styles exist. Both are named in the microservices.io pattern.

<MultiStepStyles />

- **Choreography.** There is no coordinator. Each service listens for events and decides what to do. Payment hears "order created" and charges. Inventory hears "payment done" and reserves. It has no central point of failure. The cost is that the flow exists only as a web of subscriptions. To answer "where is order 7 stuck?" you must read logs from several services.
- **Orchestration.** One orchestrator holds the flow. It tells the payment service to charge, waits for the answer, then tells inventory. The flow is in one place. The cost is that the orchestrator must keep its own state durably, or a crash loses the saga.

A rule of thumb: choreography suits two or three steps with little branching. Orchestration suits longer flows, branches, timeouts, and anything the business wants to watch.

## Reliable events: the outbox

Every step does two things: change its database and publish an event. If it does them as two separate writes, a crash between them breaks the saga ([lesson 6.5](/learn/06-async/cdc/) shows that failure). The fix is the **transactional outbox**. The step writes its business row and an outbox row in one local transaction. A relay reads the outbox, by polling it or by tailing the database log, and publishes to the broker. The event exists if and only if the change committed.

The relay can publish the same row twice, for example if it crashes before marking the row sent. So the next step must be idempotent. It can store the event id and skip a repeat ([lesson 6.3](/learn/06-async/delivery-semantics/)). I give no polling interval here, because the right value depends on your latency target and load, and I found no primary source that fixes one.

## Workflow engines: the orchestrator you do not build

Writing a durable orchestrator yourself means writing a state table, a retry timer, a recovery scan, and a way to resume after a crash. A **workflow engine** gives you that. [Temporal](/learn/08-technologies/temporal/) (lesson 8.9) keeps an Event History of every step. If a worker dies, another worker replays the workflow code against that history and continues from the last recorded step. Your saga becomes ordinary code:

```ts
// Pseudocode, not a real SDK call.
async function placeOrder(order) {
  const undo = [];
  try {
    await createOrder(order);      undo.push(() => cancelOrder(order));
    await chargeCard(order);       undo.push(() => refundCard(order));
    await reserveStock(order);
  } catch (e) {
    for (const f of undo.reverse()) await f(); // each one retried
    throw e;
  }
}
```

Temporal runs the three calls as activities. Per the Temporal docs, activities are retried by default and workflow code is not. The workflow code must be deterministic: no clock reads, random numbers, or direct network calls, because it re-runs on replay. AWS Step Functions offers the same idea as a managed service. It has `Retry` and `Catch` on its states, and with no catcher an error fails the whole execution.

## Numbers

Sagas have no latency number of their own. Their cost is the work done when something fails. Take these assumptions: three steps, each fails independently with probability 0.5%, and 100 orders arrive per second.

- A saga completes with no compensation with probability 0.995 × 0.995 × 0.995 = 0.985.
- So about 1.5% of sagas run at least one compensation. That is about 1.5 per second.
- A failure at step 3 costs two compensations. A failure at step 2 costs one.

The point is that compensation is a normal path with real traffic, not a rare edge case. Test it as carefully as the happy path. For a sense of how slow a cross-service hop is, see lesson [1.5](/learn/01-one-machine/numbers-to-know/).

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two-phase commit | Atomic commit across databases | Blocking if the coordinator fails. Locks held over the network. All parties must join | Rare. A few databases you control |
| Saga with choreography | No central service. Loose coupling | Flow hard to trace. Cycles are easy to create | Two or three steps |
| Saga with orchestration | Flow in one place. Easy to monitor | Orchestrator needs durable state | Longer flows, branches, timeouts |
| Outbox for step events | No lost events without 2PC | A relay to run. Duplicates. Developers can skip the outbox write | Every step that publishes an event |
| Workflow engine | Durable state and retries built in | Determinism rules. Workflow versioning. One more system to run | Flows that run long or are business critical |

<Callout type="tip">
Say: "I will not use 2PC across services, since a coordinator crash leaves participants holding locks. I will run a saga. Each step is a local transaction that writes an outbox row. If step 3 fails, earlier steps run idempotent compensations, like a refund. For control and visibility I will use an orchestrator, a workflow engine like Temporal, so a crash resumes from durable state." Then name the visible middle state and how the UI shows it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "roll back the database". The earlier steps already committed in other databases. Do not write "write the DB, then publish to the broker" as the plan: a crash between the two loses the event. And do not forget the compensation of the compensation: a refund can fail, so it needs retries and an alert, not a shrug.
</Callout>

<Callout type="deep" title="Why sagas are not ACID">
Atomicity is replaced by "eventually all steps done or all compensated." Consistency holds at the end, not during. Isolation is gone: another request can read the PENDING order or try to spend the same stock. Durability is per step. Common fixes for the missing isolation are a status field that acts as a soft lock, and ordering the steps so the scarce resource is claimed first. They reduce the problem. They do not remove it.
</Callout>
