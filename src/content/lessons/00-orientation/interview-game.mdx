## The problem

You study for weeks. You know caches, queues, and sharding. Then the interview ends with "we will let you know". You do not know what went wrong.

The cause is often not missing facts. It is that you played a different game. A system design interview is a conversation that an interviewer scores against a list. If you do not know the list, you cannot aim for it.

This lesson describes that list. A caution first: companies write their own rubrics, and none are public in full. What follows is a synthesis of public guidance. Read it as **typical expectations**, not rules.

## What gets scored

Public guides agree on a few themes. Hello Interview groups them as problem navigation, solution design, technical excellence, and communication. We split them into nine finer dimensions:

- **Scope and requirements.** Do you find out what to build before you build it?
- **Breadth.** Do you know the standard building blocks and when each fits?
- **Depth.** Can you go far below the surface in some area?
- **Driving the conversation.** Do you lead, or wait to be asked?
- **Trade-offs.** Do you see what each choice costs?
- **Communication.** Can others follow and trust your reasoning?
- **Handling ambiguity.** Do you make progress when the prompt is vague?
- **Quantitative reasoning.** Do numbers guide your choices?
- **Ops and failure thinking.** What happens when it breaks, and when it changes?

## Weight shifts with level

Pick a level. Watch the bars move.

<RubricBars client:visible />

The pattern in public guidance is consistent. At mid-level, breadth and a working design matter most. At senior level, fundamentals are assumed, and you are expected to go deep in a couple of areas and to find flaws in your own design. At staff level, you are expected to lead almost the whole session, anticipate problems before they are raised, and show depth in several areas.

Hello Interview's per-level write-up gives one telling detail. For mid-level, the interviewer takes over in the later stages. For senior, you steer. For staff, you lead nearly all of it, as a peer.

Notice what the senior answer does that the mid answer does not. It attacks its own first idea. It brings a number (62^7). It names the next bottleneck. None of that needs more knowledge. It needs the habit of asking "what is wrong with this?" before the interviewer does.

The staff answer adds three things. It starts from constraints ("hard to guess?"). It plans for failure. It looks ahead to migration.

## Trade-offs: what to aim for

| Strategy | You gain | You pay | When to choose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aim wide (cover every block) | Safe at mid-level | Little depth, little time for flaws | You are interviewing for mid-level |
| Aim deep in two areas | Strong senior signal | You must choose well, fast | Fundamentals are solid and you have real experience |
| Lead and frame the whole talk | Staff signal | Risk of talking past the interviewer | You can check in often and stay flexible |

You cannot do all three in 45 minutes. Pick the one that fits the level you are interviewing for.

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Say your own weaknesses first. "This counter is a single point of failure. Here is how I would fix it." That one habit moves an answer up a level, because the interviewer no longer has to find the flaw.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
Ask what level you are interviewing for, or read the job posting. Then spend your time to match. A staff-level answer given as a list of boxes will be marked as a mid-level one.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Treating the interview as a quiz. The interviewer is not checking whether you know "the answer". They are watching how you think when no single answer exists.
</Callout>
