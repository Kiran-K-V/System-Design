## The problem

You get a request: "Design a URL shortener." You have a 45-minute design session and a blank whiteboard.

Many engineers start drawing a load balancer at minute one. By minute 30 they have a large diagram that may solve the wrong problem. Nobody in the room can tell whether the reasoning is sound, only that the drawing is big.

You need a path through the session. The one below has six steps. Hello Interview publishes this same six-step shape. The examples and wording here are our own.

## The six steps

Step through it. The example is a URL shortener.

<DeliveryFramework client:visible />

Read the picture in order:

1. **Requirements.** What must the system do, and how well?
2. **Core entities.** What nouns does it store or move?
3. **API or interface.** What contract do users get?
4. **Data flow (optional).** For data-processing systems, what chain of steps turns input into output? Skip it for simple request-response systems.
5. **High-level design.** The simplest set of components that satisfies the API.
6. **Deep dives.** Stress the design against the hard requirements.

Notice the two big arrows. They go from the design steps back to Requirements. This is the core idea. Requirements are not a step you finish and forget. They are the test you run again after steps 5 and 6.

## Functional and non-functional requirements

**Functional** requirements say what the system does: "a user creates a short link", "a short link redirects".

**Non-functional** requirements say how well it does it. They are also called quality attributes. The usual list:

| Attribute | The question it asks |
|---|---|
| Latency | How fast must one request be? |
| Availability | How much downtime is allowed? |
| Consistency | Can a reader see stale data? For how long? |
| Scalability | How much load, and how fast will it grow? |
| Durability | Can we ever lose data once we accept it? |
| Security | Who may do what, and what must stay private? |

Do not list all six. Pick the three or four that matter for this system and give each a number. "Redirect in ~100 ms" beats "should be fast".

**Below the line** means scoping out. Say it aloud: "Custom aliases and analytics are below the line. I will return to them if we have time." This protects your time and shows you chose on purpose.

## Where the time goes

Hello Interview suggests roughly 5 minutes on requirements, 2 on entities, 5 on the API, 5 on data flow if used, 10 to 15 on the high-level design, and about 10 on deep dives. Those are guides, not laws. Try your own split.

Try "Over-engineered HLD". The high-level design eats the clock, and deep dives get three minutes. That is the failure to avoid. Deep dives are where the non-functional requirements get tested, and where experience shows.

## What to say at each step

| Step | Say this | Link forward |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Requirements | "Two core features. Below the line: X. Assumed scale: Y. Latency target: Z." | Estimation in lesson 1.4 |
| 2 Entities | "The nouns are A, B, C. Rough fields only." | Data modeling in Module 4 |
| 3 API | "One endpoint per functional requirement. The user comes from the auth token." | API design in Module 3 |
| 4 Data flow | "This is a pipeline, so the stages are..." or "Not needed here." | Patterns in Module 9 |
| 5 High-level design | "Trace each endpoint through the boxes. Simple first." | Networking in Module 2 |
| 6 Deep dives | "The hard requirement is X. The bottleneck is Y. Here are two options and the trade-off." | Caching, replication, sharding in later modules |

## Common mistakes

- **Jumping to design.** You draw before you know what to draw. Spend the first minutes on requirements.
- **Skipping non-functional requirements.** Without latency and availability targets, no design can be judged.
- **Over-engineering the high-level design.** Caches, queues, and microservices at step 5 leave nothing to discuss at step 6.
- **No numbers.** "It scales" means nothing. "6K writes per second at peak" can be checked.
- **Never revisiting requirements.** The loops are the point. Re-check after step 5 and after step 6.

## Trade-offs of using a framework

| Choice | You gain | You pay | When to choose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Follow all six steps in order | Never lost, easy to time | Can feel stiff | You are unsure where to start |
| Skip data flow | Time for deep dives | Might miss a pipeline | The system is request-response |
| Spend longer on requirements | Better scoped design | Less drawing time | The prompt is vague |

## In practice

<Callout type="tip">
Tell the room your plan in the first minute: "I will start with requirements, sketch an API and a simple design, then go deep on the hardest part. Tell me if you want a different focus." This shows that you drive, and it lets others steer.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Treating the framework as a script. If the team wants to dive into an area at minute 15, follow them. The steps are a map, not a rail.
</Callout>
