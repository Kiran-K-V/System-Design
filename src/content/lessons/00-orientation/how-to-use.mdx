## The problem

Most system design material gives you a finished diagram. You nod, and a week later you cannot rebuild it.

You do not need more diagrams. You need to know why each box is there. Load Bearing teaches from first principles. Every lesson starts with a pain, tries the obvious fix, shows it fail, and only then builds the real design.

This lesson shows you the shape of a lesson, the controls, and a way to study. It takes five minutes.

<Callout type="deep" title="Who this is for">
Load Bearing is written for interview preparation at mid to staff level. It assumes you can code and have shipped a web service. It does not teach beginner programming. It also does not replace building real systems.
</Callout>

Every full lesson follows the same seven parts. Once you know the shape, you can skim for the part you need.

The naive fix is on purpose. A design you have seen fail is a design you can defend. When an interviewer asks "why not just X?", you will already know.

## The step player

Most lessons have step-through animations. Each one shows one idea per step. Try this small one. It has three steps.

<StepPlayerDemo client:visible />

The controls:

| Control | What it does |
|---|---|
| Play | Runs all steps one after another. It never starts on its own. |
| Previous, Next | Move one step. |
| Step dots and scrub bar | Jump to any step. |
| Speed | 0.5x, 1x, or 2x for Play. |
| Left and Right arrows | Step, when the figure has focus. Click the figure first. |
| Space | Play or pause, when the figure has focus. |

Some lessons also have simulators with sliders and buttons. Change a number and watch what the system does. That is the quickest way to feel a trade-off.

## Keyboard, search, and progress

- **Ctrl+K** (or **Cmd+K** on Mac), or **/**, opens search. **Esc** closes it.
- Arrow keys step a focused animation.
- **Mark complete** sits at the end of each lesson. It fills the progress ring. Progress is stored only in this browser. There is no account. Clearing site data resets it.

## How the course is ordered

The course is a stack. Each layer uses only the layers below it.

<CourseMap />

Every lesson lists the lessons it builds on after "builds on" at the top. A lesson never uses an idea from a later lesson. If a term feels new, follow that link back.

Module 0 (this module) sets the rules of the interview. Module 1 starts from one machine. The later modules add networks, data, caching, and patterns, and the practice problems come last.

## How to study

1. **Read** the problem and the naive fix. Stop before the mechanism.
2. **Predict.** In an animation, say out loud what the next step will be. Then press Next. A wrong guess teaches more than a right one.
3. **Do the quiz.** Questions ask you to predict, compute, or choose. Do not look at the answer first.
4. **Explain it aloud.** Pretend an interviewer asked. Two minutes, no notes. Where you stall is what you do not yet know.

<Callout type="tip">
Interviews are spoken. Practice by speaking. Reading a lesson three times helps less than explaining it once to a wall.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not memorize designs. A memorized "design Twitter" breaks when the interviewer changes one requirement. Know the trade-offs, and you can rebuild the design.
</Callout>
