## The problem

You study for weeks. You know caches, queues, and sharding. Then you present a design to your team, and the feedback is "I am not sure about this". You do not know what to fix.

The cause is often not missing facts. A design review is a conversation, and reviewers judge it against a list, even if nobody writes the list down. If you do not know the list, you cannot aim for it.

This lesson describes that list. A caution first: teams use their own standards, and none are public in full. What follows is a synthesis of common practice. Read it as **typical expectations**, not rules.

## What reviewers look at

Nine questions come up again and again:

- **Scope and requirements.** Did you find out what to build before you built it?
- **Breadth.** Do you know the standard building blocks and when each fits?
- **Depth.** Can you go far below the surface in some area?
- **Driving the discussion.** Do you lead, or wait to be asked?
- **Trade-offs.** Do you see what each choice costs?
- **Communication.** Can others follow and trust your reasoning?
- **Handling ambiguity.** Do you make progress when the request is vague?
- **Quantitative reasoning.** Do numbers guide your choices?
- **Ops and failure thinking.** What happens when it breaks, and when it changes?

## Weight shifts with experience

Pick a level. Watch the bars move.

<RubricBars client:visible />

The pattern is consistent. At mid-level, breadth and a working design matter most. At senior level, fundamentals are assumed, and you are expected to go deep in a couple of areas and to find flaws in your own design. At staff level, you are expected to lead almost the whole discussion, anticipate problems before they are raised, and show depth in several areas.

One telling detail is who drives. For a mid-level engineer, a senior colleague takes over in the later stages. For a senior engineer, they steer. For a staff engineer, they lead nearly all of it, as a peer.

Notice what the senior answer does that the mid answer does not. It attacks its own first idea. It brings a number (62^7). It names the next bottleneck. None of that needs more knowledge. It needs the habit of asking "what is wrong with this?" before a reviewer does.

The staff answer adds three things. It starts from constraints ("hard to guess?"). It plans for failure. It looks ahead to migration.

## Trade-offs: where to spend your effort

| Strategy | You gain | You pay | When to choose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aim wide (cover every block) | A safe, complete picture | Little depth, little time for flaws | You are new to the area |
| Aim deep in two areas | Strong, trusted judgment | You must choose well, fast | Fundamentals are solid and you have real experience |
| Lead and frame the whole discussion | Team-level influence | Risk of talking past people | You can check in often and stay flexible |

You cannot do all three in one hour. Pick the one that fits where you are now.

## In practice

<Callout type="tip">
Say your own weaknesses first. "This counter is a single point of failure. Here is how I would fix it." That one habit moves an answer up a level, because the reviewer no longer has to find the flaw.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
Ask what the decision is for, and who will live with it. Then spend your effort to match. A staff-level problem answered with a list of boxes will read as a mid-level answer.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Treating a review as a quiz. Reviewers are not checking whether you know "the answer". They are watching how you think when no single answer exists.
</Callout>
