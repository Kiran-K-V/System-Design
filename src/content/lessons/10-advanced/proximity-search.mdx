## The problem

A ride app has 1 billion places and drivers on the map. Each one has two numbers: **latitude** (how far north or south, from −90 at the south pole to 90 at the north pole) and **longitude** (how far east or west, from −180 to 180, with 0 at a line through Greenwich in London). Together they are a **coordinate**, like a street and house number for the whole planet. A user opens the app. You must show everything within 1 km, in under 100 ms.

The query reads like this: `WHERE lat BETWEEN a AND b AND lng BETWEEN c AND d`. It has two range conditions. Lesson [4.3](/learn/04-data/indexing/) showed what a B-tree does with that. A B-tree sorts in **one** dimension, like names in a phone book. A map has two, so one sorted list cannot keep both neighbors close.

You can build a composite index on `(lat, lng)`, one index that sorts by lat first and then by lng. The first range condition narrows the search. The second one can only filter what the first one already read. That is rule 2 in lesson 4.3.

Do the numbers. Assume the 1 billion places are spread evenly over the Earth. That is false, but it keeps the arithmetic honest. A degree (°) of latitude is about 111 km. A km² is a square 1 km on each side.

- The search box is 2 km by 2 km, at latitude 51.5. That is 0.018° of latitude and 0.029° of longitude.
- It holds about 4 km² / 510 million km² × 1 billion = **~8 places**.
- The index finds a thin band: 2 km tall, all the way around the planet. At this latitude the band is about 25,000 km long, so it covers about 50,000 km². It holds about **~100,000 rows**.

The database reads about 100,000 rows to return 8. It reads about 12,000 times too much. The B-tree is not the problem. The question has two dimensions and the index has one.

## Naive solution: two indexes

Put one B-tree on `lat` and one on `lng`. The query planner (the database part that picks how to run a query) may read both and intersect the row lists, which means keep only the rows that appear in both. Each index still returns a full band: one around the planet by latitude, one pole to pole by longitude. The intersection is small, but the work to find it is not.

Both bands are huge. The work is proportional to the bands, not to the answer. We need **one** key where places that are close in space are close in the sort order.

## The mechanism: geohash

A **geohash** turns a latitude and a longitude into one string. Places that share a long prefix lie in the same small rectangle. A prefix is the first characters of a string, such as `gcp` in `gcpuz`. A B-tree can range-scan a prefix, which means it reads all keys that start with it in one sweep. That is one dimension again, and B-trees are good at it.

The encoder is a repeated halving.

1. Start with longitude in [−180, 180] and latitude in [−90, 90].
2. Take the middle of longitude. A bit is a single 0 or 1. If the point is at or above it, write bit 1 and keep the upper half. If not, write 0 and keep the lower half.
3. Do the same for latitude.
4. Alternate, longitude first.
5. Cut the bits into groups of 5. Each group is a number from 0 to 31. Map it to the **base32** alphabet, a set of 32 letters and digits so each group becomes one readable character: `0123456789bcdefghjkmnpqrstuvwxyz` (it leaves out a, i, l, and o).

Each character adds 5 bits, so it cuts the cell by a factor of 32. Longitude gets the extra bit when the count is odd, so cells alternate between shapes. Here are the sizes at the equator. They are computed from the bit counts, with 111.32 km per degree.

| Length | Bits (lng + lat) | Cell width × height |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 + 2 | ~5,000 km × ~5,000 km |
| 2 | 5 + 5 | ~1,250 km × ~626 km |
| 4 | 10 + 10 | ~39 km × ~20 km |
| 5 | 13 + 12 | ~4.9 km × ~4.9 km |
| 6 | 15 + 15 | ~1.2 km × ~0.6 km |
| 8 | 20 + 20 | ~38 m × ~19 m |

These agree with the error column on [Wikipedia's Geohash page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geohash). The maximum error is half a cell. For example, that error is 2.4 km at length 5 and 19 m at length 8.

Lesson [4.3](/learn/04-data/indexing/) rule 1 now works for us. Store the hash in a column and index it. "Everything in cell `gcpuz`" becomes `WHERE geohash LIKE 'gcpuz%'`. That is a range scan on a B-tree.

### The edge problem

A circle rarely fits inside one cell. If your point sits near the edge of its cell, places just across the edge are in a different cell. They may also have a different prefix. Two points 0.002° of longitude apart, about 140 m, can share **no** first character. The reason is that the line at longitude 0 (through Greenwich) is the very first split the encoder makes. One side starts with a 0 bit and the other with a 1 bit.

The fix is simple. Pick a cell at least as large as the search radius. Then query the point's cell **and its 8 neighbors**: 9 prefix scans. Then filter by the true distance, because the 3 × 3 block is larger than the circle.

<ProxNeighbors />

Count the work for our 1 km radius. Use length 5 cells (4.9 km). Nine cells cover 14.7 km × 14.7 km = 216 km². That holds about 216 / 510 million × 1 billion = **~420 rows**. The exact filter keeps about 6 of them. We read 420 rows instead of 100,000. The cost: 9 range scans instead of 1.

Wrong choice of cell size breaks the guarantee. With length 6 cells, each cell is 1.2 km × 0.6 km. The 3 × 3 block reaches only 0.6 km north and south of the middle cell. A 1 km radius can have places outside it. They are silently missed.

## Quadtree: cells that follow the data

A geohash grid has fixed cells. Midtown Manhattan and the Sahara get the same size. Midtown has thousands of places per cell. The Sahara has none.

A **quadtree** adapts. "Quad" means four. It starts as one cell, like one square of paper. When a cell holds more than a set **capacity** of points, it splits into four equal quadrants. Dense areas split again and again. Sparse areas stay as one big cell.

<ProxQuadtree client:visible />

Notice step 4. Five points fall in the same quarter of a quarter. The split repeats until the points separate. That is the cost of a quadtree: it is a tree that changes. Inserts can split cells, deletes can merge them, and in a sharded database (one split across several machines) the tree itself needs a home (lesson [4.7](/learn/04-data/sharding/)). Many systems keep the quadtree in memory and rebuild it from the table.

A geohash is a table column and a B-tree. A quadtree is a structure you maintain. In practice you pick by the data's shape: even spread favors geohash, and strong clusters favor a quadtree.

## What real systems use

- **Redis GEO.** Redis is a fast in-memory data store. `GEOADD` stores each place in a sorted set, a list kept in order by a number called its score. The score is a 52-bit geohash, built by interleaving (alternating) the longitude and latitude bits. Redis accepts longitude −180 to 180 and latitude ±85.05112878, and its docs state a worst-case distance error of about 0.5%. `GEOSEARCH` finds members in a radius or box. It is a good fit for a "drivers near me" set that fits in RAM (the machine's fast working memory) (lesson [8.2](/learn/08-technologies/redis/)).
- **PostGIS.** A PostgreSQL extension, which is an add-on that gives the database new abilities (lesson [8.1](/learn/08-technologies/postgresql/)). It builds a spatial index with GiST, a tree index that can sort shapes and boxes, not only numbers. `ST_DWithin` uses the index for a bounding-box check (is the place inside a rectangle around the circle?) and then tests exact distance. The `geography` type works in meters on a spheroid, which is a slightly squashed ball that models the Earth.
- **S2** (Google). It projects the sphere onto the six faces of a cube, as if the Earth were wrapped in a box. Then a quadtree splits each face, down to level 30 (each level splits cells into four smaller ones). Cell IDs follow a **Hilbert curve**, a path that visits nearby cells close together. So near IDs are usually near on the map. The S2 statistics page gives level 12 an average area of about 5 km².
- **H3** (Uber). It tiles the sphere with hexagons (six-sided cells, like a honeycomb). Each cell has 7 children, the smaller cells inside it. At every resolution, 12 cells are pentagons (five-sided), because hexagons alone cannot cover a sphere.

All of these are the same idea. Turn two dimensions into a cell ID, then use an ordinary index or sorted set on the ID.

## Trade-offs

| Choice | You gain | You pay | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geohash + B-tree | Plain index, easy to shard by prefix | 9 scans per query; edge cases at the poles and the date line | Evenly spread data, you already have a B-tree store |
| Quadtree | Small cells where data is dense | A tree to maintain; lives in memory | Strong clusters, an in-memory service |
| S2 or H3 | Good cell shapes, library for neighbors and covers | A library and a cell-level choice | Large geo products, global data |
| Redis GEO | One command, very fast | Data must fit in RAM; sphere model | Live positions, up to what one node holds |
| PostGIS | Exact math, joins with SQL data | Heavier queries and index | Maps with rich queries and a relational model |

## Interview lens

<Callout type="tip">
Start by saying why a B-tree on latitude and longitude fails. For example, "it sorts one dimension, so I read a whole band to find a small box". Then give the fix. Say: "I turn two coordinates into one cell ID with a geohash and query the cell plus its 8 neighbors". State the cell size rule. Say: "the cell must be at least as large as the radius, then I filter by true distance". Say what you would use in production: "Redis GEO for live positions, PostGIS for rich queries".
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Do not say "same prefix means within the radius". The cell is bigger than the circle, and two close points can sit in different cells. Do not say "longer geohash means more accurate for every query". A longer hash gives a smaller cell, so a fixed 3 × 3 block may no longer cover the radius. And do not forget that a hot city makes a hot shard. A geohash prefix for midtown can take most of the traffic (lesson [4.7](/learn/04-data/sharding/), hot keys).
</Callout>

<Callout type="deep" title="Why one-dimensional order cannot be perfect">
A geohash walks the grid in a "Z" pattern. The walk jumps at some cell borders. So cells that touch in space can be far apart in the sort order. No order of a 2D grid can keep every pair of neighbors adjacent. The Hilbert curve in S2 has fewer big jumps than the Z pattern, but it has some. That is why every method here needs a neighbor step and a final exact-distance filter.
</Callout>
